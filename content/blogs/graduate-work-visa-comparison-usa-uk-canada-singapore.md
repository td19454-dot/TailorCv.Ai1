## Key Takeaways

- The decisive difference is who controls the permit: you, or an employer. Canada and the UK give the graduate an open permit; the US and Singapore route control through employers.
- Runway length varies enormously, and it determines how many chances you get at a longer-term status.
- The US is the only one of the four where the main long-term route runs through a lottery.
- Canada is the only one where the graduate route is designed as a step toward permanent residence.
- Rules in all four have changed in recent years. Verify current details with the official authority before making decisions.

## The comparison at a glance

| | Who applies | Employer-tied | Typical runway | Route to longer stay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **USA** | You (OPT), employer (H-1B) | OPT no, H-1B yes | 12 months, 36 with STEM | H-1B lottery, or cap-exempt employers |
| **UK** | You | No | Graduate route, fixed period | Skilled Worker visa, employer-sponsored |
| **Canada** | You | No | Tied to programme length | Work experience feeds PR pathways |
| **Singapore** | Employer | Yes | Tied to the job | Renewal, then longer-term residence routes |

The table is a shape, not a legal summary. Every one of these has changed recently and all four have detail the table cannot hold.

## The United States

You apply for [OPT yourself](/blog/usa-opt-cpt-explained-students), and it is not employer-tied — you can change jobs freely. Twelve months, extendable by a further period if your degree is STEM-designated.

The complication is what comes next. The main long-term route for graduates is the H-1B, which is employer-sponsored, capped, and allocated by [random lottery](/blog/usa-h1b-lottery-new-grads). Being an excellent candidate does not improve your odds within a pool.

That single fact shapes everything. Because registration happens once a year, your number of attempts is decided by how long your work authorisation lasts — which is why STEM eligibility matters more than almost any other administrative detail in the US system.

**Best suited to:** graduates in STEM fields who want maximum earning potential and can tolerate uncertainty, or anyone who can access cap-exempt employers such as universities and affiliated non-profits, where no lottery applies.

**Hardest part:** the mismatch between a hiring calendar that runs a year ahead and a permit clock that starts at graduation.

## The United Kingdom

The Graduate route is applied for by you, is not tied to an employer, and does not require a job offer. You can work, switch jobs, be self-employed, or look for work.

The transition afterwards is to the Skilled Worker visa, which does require an employer — and specifically an employer holding a sponsor licence, meeting salary thresholds for the role.

The practical consequence is a two-phase search. Phase one is free: any employer will hire you because there is no sponsorship burden. Phase two is narrow: only licensed sponsors can retain you. Graduates who spend their whole graduate-route period at a non-sponsoring employer arrive at the end with no route forward.

**Best suited to:** graduates who want time to explore without employer dependency, and who are deliberate about moving to a sponsoring employer before the clock runs down.

**Hardest part:** the temptation to take any job because the permit allows it, then discovering the employer cannot sponsor.

## Canada

The [post-graduation work permit](/blog/canada-pgwp-for-new-grads) is an open permit, applied for by you, with length tied to your programme.

What makes Canada structurally different is the destination. The permit is explicitly a bridge to permanent residence: Canadian work experience feeds Express Entry and provincial nominee programmes. You are accumulating something durable rather than waiting for a lottery or an employer's willingness.

That changes how you should choose a first job. The occupational classification of the role, and the province you work in, can matter as much as the salary, because they affect which PR pathways you qualify for and how strongly.

**Best suited to:** graduates whose goal is permanent residence rather than maximum short-term earnings.

**Hardest part:** the permit is normally issued once, so when you use it is a real decision. And rules have changed repeatedly, including field-of-study requirements for some programmes.

## Singapore

The most employer-controlled of the four. The [Employment Pass](/blog/singapore-employment-pass-graduates) is applied for by the employer and tied to that job. There is no equivalent of an open graduate permit you hold independently.

Qualifying salary rises with age, which favours fresh graduates — you face the lowest threshold on the scale. A points-based assessment sits alongside it, scoring things including the employer's workforce composition, which is why two firms respond differently to identical candidates.

**Best suited to:** graduates of local institutions, and anyone with skills scarce enough that employers will absorb the process.

**Hardest part:** you cannot hold status independently, so a job loss is a status problem, not just an income problem.

## How to choose

**If your goal is permanent residence**, Canada's structure is the most direct — the work experience itself is the qualifying asset.

**If your goal is maximum earnings and you are in STEM**, the US remains the highest ceiling, at the cost of lottery uncertainty.

**If you want freedom to explore first**, the UK graduate route gives genuinely unconditional time, provided you use it to reach a sponsoring employer.

**If you want to work in Asia-Pacific with a strong regional network**, Singapore is a serious hub, with the trade-off of employer-dependent status.

Whichever you choose, two things are constant. Apply earlier than domestic classmates, because any authorisation question slows a hiring process. And know your own dates precisely — a candidate who can state their runway in one sentence reads as competent, which is worth more in an interview than any answer technique.

The resume conventions differ by country too — no photo in the US and Canada, different length norms in the UK and Singapore, and different expectations about stating status. Whatever the market, the document is machine-screened first, so check it [parses cleanly](/ats-analysis) before worrying about anything else.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming the permit is the destination.** All four are bridges; the question is what they bridge to.
- **Taking any job because the permit allows it.** In the UK especially, a non-sponsoring employer is a dead end.
- **Ignoring occupational classification in Canada.** It affects PR eligibility more than salary does.
- **Assuming STEM status in the US.** It is determined by programme code, not by how technical the subject sounds.
- **Relying on articles for current rules.** Including this one — all four systems have changed recently.
- **Starting the search after graduating.** Every one of these clocks starts running whether or not you are ready.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which country is easiest for international graduates?

Canada is generally the most structurally welcoming, because the permit is open and leads toward permanent residence by design. Easiest to enter is not the same as best for your field or earnings.

### Can I move between these countries later?

Yes, and many people do. Work experience in any of the four is portable, and several systems weight foreign work experience in their points assessments.

### Does a master's help?

In the US it improves H-1B lottery odds because advanced-degree holders get two draws. Elsewhere its effect is mainly on employability and salary thresholds rather than on permit mechanics.

### Which has the longest runway?

It depends on programme length and field. STEM graduates in the US and graduates of longer Canadian programmes generally have the most, but the comparison is not like-for-like because the routes lead to different destinations.

### Do I need a job offer before graduating?

Not for the US, UK or Canadian graduate routes, which you hold independently. For Singapore, yes — the pass is employer-applied and tied to the role.

### How current is this?

The structures described are stable; the specific durations, thresholds and eligibility criteria are not. Check USCIS, the Home Office, IRCC and MOM respectively before making a decision that depends on a number.
