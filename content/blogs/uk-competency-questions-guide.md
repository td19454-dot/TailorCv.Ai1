## Key Takeaways

- UK employers score against a published competency framework, so preparation means mapping evidence to their named competencies.
- The follow-up questions are the assessment, not the initial answer. Prepared stories that collapse on probing are the common failure.
- Use "we" for context and "I" for your contribution — British modesty norms make candidates disappear from their own stories.
- Results need to be concrete. "It went well" is not a result.
- The same stories are reused across the application form, video interview and assessment centre, so build them once and properly.

## What a competency framework is

Most UK graduate employers define the behaviours they hire for. Typically five to eight, with names like commercial awareness, working with others, resilience, analytical thinking, communication, leadership, adaptability.

Every stage of the process scores against that list. The application form questions map to it, the [recorded video interview](/blog/uk-video-interview-hirevue-guide) maps to it, and the [assessment centre](/blog/uk-assessment-centre-guide) exercises each target specific ones.

Many employers publish theirs. **Finding it and preparing one strong example per competency is the single highest-return preparation available**, and most candidates never look.

## Why the probe matters more than the answer

A US behavioural interview often accepts a well-told story and moves on. A UK competency interview does not.

Expect three or four follow-ups on the same example:

- What was your specific role in that?
- Why did you take that approach rather than the alternative?
- How did you know it had worked?
- What would you do differently now?
- How did the others react?

This is deliberate. Interviewers are testing whether the story is real and whether you have reflected on it. A memorised ninety-second answer that falls apart on the second question is worse than a rougher answer you can genuinely discuss.

Practical implication: **choose stories you actually lived and can talk about for five minutes**, not the most impressive-sounding one. If you cannot remember what someone else said or why you chose one approach over another, the probe will expose it.

## Structure

STAR works: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Two refinements matter in the UK context.

**Keep Situation short.** Two sentences. Candidates routinely spend half the answer on background.

**Make Result concrete.** The most common weakness is ending with an absence of failure — "and we submitted on time", "and it went well". That tells the interviewer nothing.

Weak: *"We worked together and delivered the project successfully."*

Better: *"We delivered two days early. The validation step I added caught three duplicated records, which would have overstated the totals by roughly 8%, and the department reused our template for the following year."*

Numbers help where they exist. Where they do not, a specific consequence works — something changed, someone adopted it, something stopped breaking.

## The "we" problem

British workplace norms discourage self-promotion, and candidates over-correct into invisibility. An answer that says "we decided, we built, we fixed" throughout leaves the interviewer unable to score you, because they are assessing you rather than your team.

The balance: **"we" for context, "I" for contribution.**

> "We had three days and the integration was failing. I took the parser, isolated the encoding bug and rewrote the import step, then walked the others through it so they could handle the same problem later."

That describes the team accurately and is clear about what you did. It is not boasting; it is answering the question.

## Building the story bank

Six to eight stories, each usable for several competencies with different emphasis.

Cover:

1. Working with someone difficult
2. A genuine failure of yours, and what changed after
3. Leading without authority
4. A hard analytical or technical problem
5. Competing deadlines and how you prioritised
6. Something you started that nobody asked for
7. Difficult feedback you received
8. Adapting when circumstances changed

Student material is entirely legitimate — group projects, part-time work, societies, sport, volunteering, tutoring, family responsibility. Interviewers hiring graduates expect graduate-scale examples and are assessing your thinking, not the size of the setting.

The same stories carry through the whole process, which is why building them properly once is efficient. The [US approach to story banks](/blog/usa-behavioral-interview-star-campus) is the same discipline with a slightly different delivery register.

## The failure question

"Tell me about a time you failed" is asked to test self-awareness. The disguised-strength answer — "I'm too much of a perfectionist" — is transparent and lands badly.

What works: a real failure, a real consequence, and a specific change you made afterwards. The change is the part being scored.

> "I under-communicated on a group project and a teammate duplicated two days of work. Since then I post a short summary at the end of every working session, which I carried into my placement year and still do."

Short, honest, and it demonstrates the reflection the question exists to find.

## Commercial awareness

A distinctively British emphasis, particularly in finance, law and consulting. It means: do you understand the business, the sector and what affects it.

Preparation is reading, with a view. Being able to name something happening in the sector and say why it matters to this employer is what separates candidates. Generic "the industry is changing rapidly" answers are heard constantly.

Pair this with [proper company research](/blog/how-to-research-company-before-interview) — the two questions employers use to separate similar candidates are "why us" and "what's happening in our market".

## Common Mistakes

- **Preparing the answer but not the follow-ups.** The probe is the assessment.
- **Long situations.** Half the answer spent on background.
- **No concrete result.** "It went well" is not an outcome.
- **Disappearing behind "we".** Leaves nothing to score.
- **A fake failure.** Transparent, and it wastes a question you could have won.
- **Ignoring the published competency framework.** It is the marking scheme, and it is usually public.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should an answer be?

Ninety seconds to two minutes for the initial answer, leaving room for follow-ups. Four-minute answers get interrupted.

### Can I use the same example twice?

Once more, with clearly different emphasis, is acceptable. Repeatedly returning to one story suggests a thin bank.

### What if I have no work experience?

Group projects, societies, part-time work and volunteering are all valid. Graduate interviewers expect graduate-scale examples.

### Should I write my answers out?

Write bullet points. Full scripts produce recited delivery and collapse when the question is phrased differently.

### What is commercial awareness really?

Understanding what the business does, how it makes money, and what is currently affecting its sector — with an actual opinion rather than a summary.

### How do I find the competency framework?

Check the employer's graduate careers pages, which frequently publish values or competencies. Where they do not, the job description's requirements are the next best proxy.
