## Key Takeaways

- New graduate hiring recruits for potential and trains generalists; mid-career hiring buys a specific skill for a specific role.
- New grads enter a cohort with structured training, rotation and a defined progression path.
- Mid-career hires are placed into an existing structure and receive far less development investment.
- Foreign professionals overwhelmingly enter mid-career, which shapes what to expect.
- Understanding which system you are in explains most of what feels confusing about Japanese employment.

Japan effectively runs two hiring systems that barely overlap. **Shinsotsu saiyo** recruits new graduates in a synchronised annual cycle, hiring for potential and training them for years. **Chuto saiyo** recruits mid-career professionals year-round, hiring for a specific capability that is needed now.

Almost every foreign professional enters through the second, and much of what feels strange about Japanese employment makes sense once you know which system you are inside.

This guide compares them and explains what each means for your career.

## New graduate hiring

**Synchronised.** An entire cohort applies simultaneously on a national calendar and starts together in April — the shukatsu process, covered in [the Japan job hunting timeline](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-job-hunting-timeline-shukatsu).

**Hired for potential, not skills.** Companies recruit generalists and assign departments afterwards. Your degree subject matters far less than in most countries, which is why liberal arts graduates routinely end up in technical functions.

**Extensive training.** New graduates receive months of structured induction, followed by years of rotation across departments. The investment is substantial and deliberate — the company is building a person, not filling a seat.

**Cohort identity persists.** Your intake year is a lasting professional identity, and progression is partly benchmarked against it — which connects directly to [nenko seniority](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-nenkou-seniority-explained).

**Pay starts low** and rises on a predictable schedule.

**Available to foreign students** who studied in Japan and participate in shukatsu, generally requiring N2 or above.

## Mid-career hiring

**Year-round and role-specific.** You are hired to do a defined job, starting soon, because a gap exists.

**Skills are the basis.** Your track record is what you are being hired for, which is why the shokumu keirekisho carries the weight — see [the shokumu keirekisho guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/shokumu-keirekisho-guide).

**Much less training.** You are expected to contribute quickly. Companies invest far less in developing mid-career hires than in developing their graduate cohort.

**Placed into an existing structure.** You join at a grade, and how generously your external experience is credited varies enormously between companies.

**Higher starting pay** than a new graduate, but potentially slower long-term progression at traditional firms where the cohort system dominates senior appointments.

**Agents dominate the process** — see [the Japan job change tenshoku guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-job-change-tenshoku-guide).

## What this means practically

**If you are a foreign professional hired mid-career:**

**Do not expect extensive onboarding.** The structured training that Japanese employment is known for is largely aimed at the graduate cohort. You will likely be pointed at work and expected to deliver.

**Your specialist value is why you were hired.** Lean into it rather than trying to become a generalist.

**Ask about development explicitly.** Training budget, language support and rotation opportunities are worth raising at offer stage, because they will not be assumed for you.

**Understand the cohort dynamic.** At traditional companies, senior positions frequently go to people who joined as graduates. That is not necessarily a formal rule and it is worth asking about directly — see whether mid-career hires and foreign employees hold senior grades.

**Gaishikei and startups largely lack this distinction**, hiring mid-career as the norm — see [gaishikei vs domestic](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-gaishikei-vs-domestic) and [the Japan startup jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-startup-jobs-guide).

## If you can choose

Some foreign students in Japan can genuinely choose between entering shukatsu or waiting and entering mid-career later.

**Shinsotsu suits you if:** you want the training investment, you plan a long career in Japan at a domestic company, your Japanese is at N2 or above, and you can commit to the timing.

**Mid-career entry suits you if:** you already have specialist experience, you want higher starting pay, your Japanese is below shukatsu requirements, or you want to enter through a gaishikei.

**The training difference is the real trade.** New graduate entry buys you years of structured development that mid-career entry simply does not offer, at the cost of lower early pay and a slower start.

## Common Mistakes

**Expecting graduate-level onboarding as a mid-career hire.** The structured training Japan is known for is aimed at the annual cohort, not at you.

**Not asking about development at offer stage.** Training budget, language support and rotation are assumed for graduates and negotiated for everyone else.

**Assuming your external experience maps to an equivalent grade.** Companies vary widely in how much they credit, and it directly determines your pay.

**Not checking whether mid-career hires reach senior roles.** At traditional firms senior appointments frequently favour the graduate cohort, and the absence of counterexamples is informative.

**Trying to be a generalist as a mid-career hire.** You were hired for a specific capability; the generalist path belongs to the graduate track.

**Applying to shukatsu below N2.** Standard new graduate hiring generally expects it, and applying below that threshold produces very low response rates.

**Ignoring the cohort identity.** Intake year is a lasting professional marker at domestic companies and shapes how progression is benchmarked.

**Assuming gaishikei work the same way.** They largely hire mid-career as the norm and lack this two-system split entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is shinsotsu saiyo?

New graduate hiring — a synchronised annual process recruiting students for potential rather than specific skills, with extensive training and rotation afterwards.

### What is chuto saiyo?

Mid-career hiring — year-round recruitment for a specific role based on your existing track record, with far less development investment.

### Which do foreign professionals usually enter?

Mid-career, overwhelmingly. New graduate hiring requires participating in the shukatsu cycle and generally N2 or above.

### Do mid-career hires get training?

Considerably less. The structured programmes Japan is known for are aimed at the graduate cohort, so raise development explicitly at offer stage.

### Is mid-career pay higher?

Starting pay is generally higher than a new graduate's, though long-term progression at traditional firms can be slower where senior roles favour the graduate cohort.

### Can foreign students do shukatsu?

Yes, if they studied in Japan and can meet the timing and language expectations — generally N2 or above.

### Does this distinction exist everywhere?

Largely not at foreign-owned companies and startups, which hire mid-career as the norm and lack the two-system split.

## Make This Practical

Work out which system you are entering, because it explains most of what will otherwise feel confusing. As a mid-career hire you are being bought for a specific capability, not developed as a generalist — so lean into your specialism rather than waiting for the training that belongs to the graduate cohort.

Then negotiate the things that will not be assumed for you. Training budget, Japanese language support, rotation opportunities and grade placement are all defaults for new graduates and negotiable items for everyone else — raise them at offer stage rather than after joining.

Finally, ask the question that reveals the ceiling. Whether mid-career hires and foreign employees hold senior grades at that company tells you more about your long-term prospects than any stated policy, and it is a reasonable thing to ask directly.
