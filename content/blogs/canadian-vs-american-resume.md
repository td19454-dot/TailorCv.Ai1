Canada and the United States share a border, a language, and many workplace norms — so it's easy to assume a resume that works in one will work in the other. Mostly, that's true: both favour concise, achievement-focused, ATS-friendly resumes. But there are real differences in spelling, terminology, and a few conventions that can subtly mark you as "not local" if you get them wrong.

This guide breaks down the Canadian vs American resume in 2026: what's the same, what's different, and exactly how to adapt your resume when you apply across the border — so you always send the right version for the right market.

## Key Takeaways

- Canadian and US resumes are **broadly similar**: 1–2 pages, achievement-focused, ATS-friendly, no photo or personal details.
- The main differences are **spelling** (Canadian vs American English), some **terminology**, and minor conventions.
- Both markets screen with an **ATS**, so formatting and keywords matter equally.
- Adapting the details — spelling, metrics, and local terms — signals you fit the market.
- When applying across the border, tailor a dedicated version rather than reusing one blindly.

## What's the Same

Before the differences, note how much overlaps — because it means adapting is quick:

- **Length:** one page for early-career; up to two for experienced professionals.
- **No photo or personal details:** neither Canadian nor US resumes include a photo, age, marital status, or (in the US) SSN / (in Canada) SIN.
- **Achievement-focused:** both prioritise quantified results over duty lists.
- **Reverse-chronological work history.**
- **ATS-friendly formatting:** single column, standard headings, text-based PDF, keyword-matched.
- **A dedicated skills section.**

So a strong resume in one country is 90% of the way to a strong resume in the other. The remaining 10% is what this guide covers.

## Key Difference 1: Spelling

The most visible difference. Canadian English generally follows British spelling conventions, while American English does not:

- Canadian: **colour, labour, organisation/organization, centre, cheque, licence (noun)**
- American: **color, labor, organization, center, check, license**

(Note: Canadian usage is somewhat mixed — "organization" with a "z" is common in Canada too — but "-our" endings like "colour" and "labour" and "centre" are distinctly Canadian.) Match the spelling to the country you're applying in. It's a small detail recruiters notice.

## Key Difference 2: Terminology

Some terms differ:

- **Grades/education:** US resumes may cite GPA on a 4.0 scale; Canadian resumes sometimes reference percentage or letter grades, and GPA is less universally expected.
- **"Resume" vs "CV":** in both countries, "resume" is standard for most jobs, and a "CV" is the long academic document. (This differs from the UK and many other regions where "CV" means the everyday resume.)
- **Certifications and licences:** name the country-specific credential (e.g. CPA designation exists in both but through different bodies; P.Eng in Canada vs PE in the US for engineers).

## Key Difference 3: Measurements and Localisation

- **Metric vs imperial:** Canada uses metric; the US uses imperial. Where units appear (rare on resumes, but possible for trades or logistics roles), match the local system.
- **Phone and location format:** use the local convention — city and province (Canada) or city and state (US). A local phone number reassures employers you're reachable.
- **Bilingualism:** in Canada, English–French bilingualism is a notable asset, especially for federal and Quebec roles. In the US, Spanish or other languages may be valued depending on the role and region.

## Key Difference 4: What Employers Expect Culturally

The differences here are subtle but real:

- **Canada** often values evidence of collaboration, community involvement, and (for newcomers) local volunteer experience.
- **The US** can lean slightly more toward bold self-promotion and standout individual achievement.

Neither is dramatic — quantified results win in both — but tone and emphasis can be nudged to fit.

## How to Adapt Your Resume Across the Border

When you apply in the other country, create a dedicated version:

1. **Switch spelling** to match the target country.
2. **Adjust terminology** (grades, credentials, "resume" conventions).
3. **Localise details** (location format, phone number, metric/imperial where relevant).
4. **Re-check keywords** against the specific posting — the ATS still rules on both sides.
5. **Add local signals** (Canadian volunteer experience; US-relevant certifications).

## Common Cross-Border Mistakes

- **Wrong spelling.** American spelling on a Canadian resume (or vice versa) is an easy tell.
- **Reusing one version blindly.** Localise details for each market.
- **Ignoring the ATS.** Both countries screen with software — formatting and keywords matter.
- **Missing local signals.** Canadian volunteer experience or relevant local certifications strengthen fit.
- **Not tailoring to the posting.** The biggest miss in either country.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use my American resume in Canada?**
Almost — the structure is the same, but switch to Canadian spelling, localise details, adjust terminology, and re-check keywords for the specific posting.

**Is the resume format different in Canada and the US?**
The core format (concise, achievement-focused, ATS-friendly, no photo) is the same. The differences are spelling, some terminology, and minor localisation.

**Do Canadian resumes use GPA like American ones?**
GPA is less universally expected in Canada; you may cite percentage or letter grades. Follow the norm in your field and what the posting asks for.

**Does spelling really matter on a resume?**
Yes — matching Canadian or American spelling to the target country is a small but noticeable signal of fit.
