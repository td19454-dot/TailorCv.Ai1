## Key Takeaways

- Bali is one of the world's **most established remote-work destinations**, with infrastructure and community that few places match.
- The legal position was **unclear for years**, with many people working on tourist visas — which was and is unlawful.
- Indonesia introduced **remote worker provisions** and a second home visa, changing the picture, and the position continues to evolve.
- **Bali is not an Indonesian job market** for foreigners in the way Jakarta is — it is tourism, hospitality and remote workers.
- **Tax residency is a separate question** from visa status and it is the dimension people most often ignore.

## Why Bali became what it is

The reasons are specific and they explain both the appeal and the problems.

**Cost.** A Western income goes a very long way, which is the foundation of the entire phenomenon.

**Infrastructure that arrived early.** Coworking spaces, reliable internet and services aimed at remote workers developed in Bali before almost anywhere else.

**Community.** The critical mass of remote workers makes arriving socially easy, which is self-reinforcing.

**Lifestyle.** Climate, surf, food and an environment people genuinely want to be in.

**And a tolerant historical approach**, where large numbers of people worked on tourist visas for years without much enforcement.

**The consequences are also real.** Canggu and Seminyak have changed enormously, [housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide) costs for locals have risen, traffic has worsened, and the relationship between the remote-worker population and local communities is a genuine and much-discussed issue.

## The legal position

Stated plainly, because a great deal of casual advice is wrong.

**Working on a tourist visa is not permitted**, whatever the arrangement and however many people do it. This has always been the legal position.

**Enforcement varied historically** and has become more active, with cases of deportation and blacklisting reported.

**Indonesia signalled and introduced provisions** aimed at remote workers, and a second home visa was created for longer stays with financial requirements.

**The remote worker route** has conditions attached including income and employment with a foreign entity, and the detail should be verified currently since this area has moved.

**A KITAS through an Indonesian sponsor** is the conventional route for anyone actually employed locally.

**Investor and retirement routes** exist for those who qualify.

**And the practical advice** is straightforward: use a route that actually fits your situation. The gap between what many people do and what is lawful is the single biggest issue with the Bali remote-work scene, and it is now more resolvable than it was.

## Tax, which people ignore

The dimension that causes the most eventual trouble.

**Indonesian tax residency** is generally triggered by presence beyond a defined period, or by intent to reside.

**Residents are taxed on worldwide income** in principle, which is a significant consideration for someone earning foreign income while living there.

**Visa status and tax residency are separate questions.** Holding a particular visa does not determine your tax position, and people conflate them constantly.

**Your home country may also tax you**, depending on its rules on residence and citizenship.

**Double taxation agreements** may apply and should be checked.

**And the practical consequence** is that someone spending years in Bali on rolling tourist visas with undeclared foreign income has an accumulating problem in two jurisdictions rather than none.

**Take professional advice** on your specific position. This is one of the clearest cases in this series where the cost of advice is small relative to the cost of getting it wrong.

## Practical life in Bali

The realities beyond the [marketing](/blog/resume-matching-marketing).

**Internet is generally good** in the main areas and coworking spaces are plentiful and inexpensive.

**Traffic in the south has become severe**, particularly around Canggu and Seminyak, and getting anywhere takes considerably longer than a map suggests.

**Housing ranges enormously**, from inexpensive local accommodation to villa rentals at Western prices, and costs have risen substantially.

**[Healthcare](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist)** has decent private clinics and international-standard facilities are limited, with serious cases frequently evacuated to Singapore — which makes evacuation cover genuinely important rather than optional.

**Scooters are the default transport** and accidents involving foreign visitors are common, so licence, helmet and insurance all matter.

**The community is large and transient**, which makes arriving easy and long friendships harder.

**And the different areas suit different people** — Canggu for the young remote-work scene, Ubud for a quieter and more wellness-oriented crowd, Sanur and Uluwatu for other preferences again.

**The honest note:** Bali is genuinely wonderful and it is also considerably more crowded, expensive and complicated than the version people imagine before arriving.

## Bali compared to other remote-work hubs

Where it sits.

**Against Chiang Mai.** Both are long-established. Chiang Mai is cheaper with a seasonal air quality problem; Bali has better weather year-round and higher costs.

**Against Lisbon.** Lisbon offers EU access, a path toward residency and better infrastructure; Bali is cheaper with better weather and no settlement path.

**Against Mexico City and Medellín.** Similar cost profiles, better time zones for American clients, and comparable community scale.

**Against Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City.** Vietnam is cheaper with a less clear legal position for remote workers.

**Against Georgia and the eastern European options.** Those offer easier visa positions and better European time zones.

**Bali's distinctive advantages** are the size and maturity of the community, the lifestyle and the year-round climate.

**Its distinctive disadvantages** are the crowding in the popular areas, the legal position having been unclear for years, and time zones that work poorly for American clients.

**And the honest closing point:** the version of Bali that made it famous — cheap, uncrowded, easy — has changed substantially, and arriving with expectations set by content from several years ago is the most common source of disappointment.

## Being a good guest

A section that belongs in an honest guide to this subject.

**Housing pressure is real.** Rising rents in popular areas affect local residents directly, and the connection between the foreign population and that pressure is well documented.

**Water and infrastructure** are under genuine strain in the south of the island.

**Respect matters practically as well as ethically.** Bali has strong cultural and religious traditions, temple etiquette is not decorative, and visible disregard for it has generated real local resentment.

**Traffic and behaviour** — particularly scooter riding — are a persistent source of friction and of accidents.

**Contributing locally** through where you spend, who you employ and how you engage makes a genuine difference at the margin.

**Learning some Indonesian** is easy relative to most Asian languages and it changes interactions substantially.

**And paying your taxes somewhere** is part of this too. A large population earning foreign income, using local infrastructure and contributing nothing to it is a legitimate criticism, and the newer visa routes at least make it possible to do better.

## Making the decision

An honest framework for anyone considering it.

**Check your visa route first.** If none of the legitimate options fit your situation, that is the answer rather than something to work around.

**Get tax advice** on your specific position before committing, since this is where the eventual problems concentrate.

**Visit before committing.** A few weeks tells you whether the reality matches the expectation, and for a great many people it does not.

**Consider the time zone seriously** if your work involves live collaboration, particularly with American colleagues.

**Choose your area deliberately.** Canggu, Ubud, Sanur and Uluwatu attract genuinely different populations and suit different people.

**Have a horizon.** Bali is not a settlement destination, and people who arrive without a sense of how long tend to drift.

**And weigh the honest downsides** — crowding, traffic, healthcare limits, the transience of the community — against the genuine upsides, which are also real.

## Alternatives within Indonesia

If Bali does not fit.

**Jakarta** for anyone who actually wants to work in the Indonesian economy rather than remotely, with the professional market and infrastructure.

**Yogyakarta** is considerably cheaper with a large student population, a strong cultural scene and a small but growing remote-worker presence.

**Bandung** has a cooler climate, [universities](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume) and a creative scene, within reach of Jakarta.

**Lombok** offers something closer to Bali twenty years ago, with less infrastructure and a much smaller community.

**Canggu alternatives within Bali** — Amed, Sidemen and the north and east of the island are dramatically quieter and cheaper than the southern hotspots.

**And the wider region.** If the Bali reality does not match the expectation, Chiang Mai, Da Nang, Kuala Lumpur and George Town all offer comparable propositions with different trade-offs.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are employed by a foreign company

Check Indonesia's remote worker provisions and the second home visa against your income and circumstances. A legitimate route is now more available than it was and it is worth using.

### Scenario 2: You have been on rolling tourist visas for years

Regularise it and get tax advice. Enforcement has increased, the tax position accumulates, and there are now routes that did not exist when the arrangement started.

### Scenario 3: You want to work for an Indonesian employer in Bali

That is a conventional KITAS situation with the usual sponsorship, restricted position and counterpart rules — and the Bali job market for foreigners is genuinely small outside tourism and hospitality.

> **Tip:** Treat visa status and tax residency as two separate questions. People in Bali routinely assume that sorting the visa settles the tax position, and it does not — presence beyond a defined period can create Indonesian tax obligations regardless of which visa you hold.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Use a route that fits your actual situation
- Check current remote worker provisions
- Get professional tax advice
- Arrange proper health insurance
- Consider your impact on local communities
- Verify conditions directly rather than from forums

### Don't

- Work on a tourist visa
- Assume visa status settles your tax position
- Rely on what other people are doing
- Ignore your home country's tax rules
- Assume Bali has a local professional job market
- Use outdated information about the legal position

## Common Mistakes

- **Working on tourist visas.** Unlawful, increasingly enforced, and now avoidable.
- **Confusing visa with tax residency.** Two separate questions people routinely conflate.
- **Ignoring home country obligations.** Tax follows you regardless of where you sit.
- **Treating forum advice as authoritative.** The legal position has changed and much of it is stale.
- **Expecting a local job market.** Bali is tourism, hospitality and remote workers.
- **Ignoring the local dimension.** Housing costs and community relations are genuine issues worth being thoughtful about.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I work remotely from Bali on a tourist visa?

No. It has never been permitted, and enforcement has become more active with reported deportations and blacklisting. Use a route that fits your situation.

### Does Indonesia have a digital nomad visa?

Indonesia introduced provisions aimed at remote workers and a second home visa for longer stays with financial requirements. The detail has moved, so verify the current position directly.

### Do I have to pay Indonesian tax?

Possibly. Tax residency is generally triggered by presence beyond a defined period and residents are taxed on worldwide income in principle. It is a separate question from your visa status.

### Is there a job market for foreigners in Bali?

Small, and concentrated in tourism and hospitality. Indonesia's professional market for foreigners is Jakarta, not Bali.

### Where do remote workers live in Bali?

Canggu is the established hub, with Ubud, Sanur and Uluwatu each attracting different communities. Canggu has changed substantially as the population grew.

### What about the impact on local communities?

It is a genuine and much-discussed issue, including housing costs and infrastructure pressure. Being thoughtful about it is reasonable rather than optional.
### What is practical life in Bali actually like?

Good internet and plentiful coworking, severe traffic in the south, housing costs that have risen substantially, limited international-standard healthcare making evacuation cover important, and scooters as default transport with real accident risk.
### How does Bali compare to other remote-work destinations?

It has the largest and most mature community and year-round climate, against crowding in popular areas, a legal position that was unclear for years, and time zones that work poorly for American clients.
### What should I be aware of as a foreigner living in Bali?

Housing pressure on local residents, infrastructure strain in the south, the practical importance of respecting cultural and religious traditions, traffic behaviour, and contributing through local spending and paying tax somewhere.
