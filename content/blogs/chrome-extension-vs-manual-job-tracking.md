## Key Takeaways

- Manual trackers fail for a predictable reason: **logging happens after the application, when the motivation has already been spent**.
- The extension **logs applications automatically** and saves each tailored resume against the job it was written for.
- The thing a spreadsheet cannot store is **the actual document you sent** — which is what you need when the call comes.
- Tracking matters most **six to twelve weeks in**, exactly when manual discipline has usually collapsed.
- A tracker's real job is not organisation. It is **making your own search legible to you** so you can improve it.

## Why spreadsheets get abandoned

Almost everyone starts one. Almost nobody has it two months later.

**The logging happens at the wrong moment.** You finish an application, you feel the small relief of having sent it, and the last thing you want is to open a spreadsheet and type the company name into it. So you tell yourself you will do it later, and later is a batch of eleven applications you can no longer reconstruct.

**It gets stale silently.** A tracker with three weeks missing is worse than no tracker, because you trust it and it is wrong.

**It stores the wrong things.** Company, role, date, status. What it does not store is the resume you actually sent, which is the thing you need when someone rings about a role from a month ago.

**And it is one more chore** in a process that is already generating more chores than motivation.

## What automatic logging changes

**It happens at the right moment — during, not after.** The application is logged as part of doing it, so there is no separate discipline to maintain.

**It stores the document.** Every tailored resume is saved to your account on the My Resumes page, against the job it was generated for. That is the piece a spreadsheet structurally cannot hold.

**It survives your motivation.** In week nine, when you are applying without enthusiasm, the record still gets kept, because it does not depend on you feeling like keeping it.

**It gives you data about your own search.** Which kinds of role responded, which did not, how your response rate changed after you rewrote your base resume. This is the information that turns a search from a slog into something you can actually improve.

## What still needs you

Automatic logging is not the whole tracker, and pretending otherwise is unhelpful.

**Outcomes.** Whether a company replied, whether you got an interview, what happened at it — these are events outside the application and they need a note.

**Follow-up dates.** A system can record that you applied; deciding to chase in ten days is your call.

**Contacts.** The recruiter's name, the person you spoke to, what they said. This is the highest-value information in any search and it lives nowhere automatic.

**Your notes on fit.** Why you wanted the job, what worried you about it, what you would ask if they called. Five sentences written at the time is worth more than an hour of reconstruction later.

**The practical setup:** let the system hold the applications and the documents, and keep a very light personal note against the ones that progress. That division of labour survives a long search in a way a full manual tracker does not.

## When tracking actually matters

Early on it feels unnecessary, which is why people skip it, which is why it fails later.

**Weeks one to three.** You remember everything. Tracking feels like overhead.

**Weeks four to eight.** The applications blur. You are no longer certain whether you applied to a company, and you have started occasionally applying twice.

**Weeks six to twelve.** Responses start arriving for applications made a month ago. This is when you need to know what you sent, and this is exactly when a manual tracker has usually gone stale.

**Beyond three months.** Patterns become visible if you have the data — which sectors respond, which application sources are wasting your time, whether a change you made helped. Without data, three months of search teaches you almost nothing.

## Mini checklist

- [ ] Extension installed and logged in
- [ ] My Resumes page checked once to see what it records
- [ ] A light personal note kept for applications that progress
- [ ] Follow-up dates set on a fixed weekly day
- [ ] Contact names recorded when you get them
- [ ] Response patterns reviewed monthly
- [ ] Duplicate applications checked before sending
- [ ] Base resume revisited if response rates are flat

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: A recruiter calls about a role from three weeks ago

Open My Resumes, find the version you sent, and read it before you speak. This is the single most concrete argument for keeping the documents rather than just the dates.

### Scenario 2: You cannot remember whether you applied somewhere

Check the record before sending again. Applying twice to the same role is a small, avoidable embarrassment that happens constantly in long searches.

### Scenario 3: Three months in with nothing to show

Look at the pattern rather than adding volume. Which sources produced responses, which sectors ignored you, whether anything changed after you updated your resume. Without a record there is nothing to look at.

> **Tip:** Keep the automatic log for applications and one short note per application that progresses. The full manual tracker is the thing that always gets abandoned; this split survives a six-month search.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Let the system log applications automatically
- Keep light notes on anything that progresses
- Record contact names when you get them
- Review response patterns monthly
- Check for duplicates before applying
- Read the sent version before a call

### Don't

- Rely on remembering
- Maintain a heavy manual tracker you will abandon
- Track dates without keeping the documents
- Skip tracking early because it feels unnecessary
- Add volume when the pattern says the resume is the problem
- Trust a tracker with three weeks missing

## Common Mistakes

- **Logging after the fact.** The motivation is spent by then, which is why spreadsheets die.
- **Storing dates but not documents.** The document is what you need on the call.
- **Skipping it early.** It feels unnecessary in week two and essential in week eight.
- **Trusting a stale tracker.** Partial data is worse than none.
- **Applying twice.** Common, avoidable, and slightly embarrassing.
- **Never reviewing patterns.** Three months without data teaches you nothing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do job search spreadsheets fail?

Because the logging happens after the application, when the motivation is already spent. Three weeks of missing entries makes the whole thing untrustworthy.

### What does the extension record?

Each tailored resume is saved to your TailorCV account against the job it was generated for, and applications are logged as you make them.

### What does a spreadsheet miss that this catches?

The actual document you sent. Dates and company names are easy to reconstruct; the specific tailored resume is not, and it is what you need when someone calls.

### Do I still need my own notes?

Yes, lightly. Outcomes, follow-up dates, contact names and your own view on fit all live outside the application and are worth five sentences each.

### When does tracking start to matter?

Around week six, when responses begin arriving for applications made a month earlier — which is exactly when manual trackers have usually gone stale.

Review monthly. Which sources responded, which sectors ignored you, and whether response rates changed after you revised your resume.
