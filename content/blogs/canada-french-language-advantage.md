## Key Takeaways

- French-language Express Entry draws have had markedly lower cutoffs than general draws, sometimes by a very wide margin.
- Additional CRS points are available for French proficiency even if English is your stronger language.
- Federal bilingual-designated positions require French and some attract a bilingualism bonus.
- Several provinces run francophone immigration streams outside Quebec with lower thresholds.
- Moderate French can be enough — you do not need fluency to benefit.

French is the most underexploited advantage in Canadian immigration and public sector employment. Candidates spend months trying to add CRS points through work experience or credential assessments while ignoring a route that can lower the threshold they need to hit in the first place.

It is not a shortcut — learning a language to a testable standard takes real time. But for anyone with school French, prior exposure, or the ability to commit a year, the return is unusually high.

This guide covers what French is actually worth and how it is assessed.

## Category-based French draws

The single largest effect.

**IRCC holds category-based Express Entry draws targeting French-language proficiency**, alongside categories for specific occupations. These draws have frequently had **substantially lower CRS cutoffs than general draws** — sometimes by a very large margin.

**What that means practically:** a candidate whose score falls well short of the general cutoff can be invited through a French-language draw. It changes eligibility rather than just adding points.

**Cutoffs and category definitions change**, so verify the current position with IRCC rather than relying on figures you read anywhere, including here.

---

## CRS points for French

Separately from category draws, French adds points directly.

**If French is your first official language**, you claim the standard language points for it.

**If English is your first**, French as a second official language adds additional points at qualifying levels — and this applies even to candidates whose English is already strong.

**Your spouse's French** also contributes, which is frequently unclaimed.

**The threshold matters.** Points accrue at defined proficiency levels, so reaching the next band is worth more than incremental improvement within one. Find out exactly which level triggers points before setting a study target.

## Francophone provincial streams

Several provinces outside Quebec run **francophone immigration streams** as part of a federal objective to support French-speaking communities outside Quebec.

**These streams frequently have lower thresholds** than the general provincial streams, because the candidate pool is smaller.

**They exist across multiple provinces**, including Ontario, the Atlantic provinces, Manitoba and others — see [the Canada provincial nominee guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-provincial-nominee-guide).

**Combined with a provincial nomination's very large CRS addition**, a francophone stream is one of the most reliable routes available to a candidate with moderate French and a below-average score.

## How French is tested

**TEF Canada and TCF Canada** are the approved tests for immigration purposes. Both assess reading, writing, listening and speaking.

**Results map to Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) equivalents**, which is what IRCC actually scores.

**Practical notes:**

- **Book early** — test availability varies by city
- **The speaking component** is where candidates most often underperform relative to their reading ability, so practise it specifically
- **Retaking is normal** and frequently worthwhile, since crossing a band threshold changes your points meaningfully
- **Preparation matters** — these are testable formats and familiarity improves scores

## French for employment

Beyond immigration, French has direct labour market value.

**Federal public service.** Many positions are designated bilingual, requiring specified French levels, and some attract a bilingualism bonus. This measurably widens the roles available and can increase pay — see [the Ottawa jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ottawa-jobs-guide).

**Quebec.** French is the working language for most employers, and the legal framework reinforces it — see [the Montreal jobs and French guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/montreal-jobs-french-guide).

**New Brunswick** is officially bilingual, with bilingual requirements across public and private employment.

**Customer-facing national roles.** Companies serving customers across Canada frequently need bilingual staff, in service, sales and support functions.

**Put it on your resume with a level**, not an adjective. "French — CLB 7 (TEF Canada, 2026)" is concrete; "conversational French" is not — see [the resume for newcomers to Canada guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/resume-for-newcomers-to-canada).

## Is it worth learning?

An honest assessment.

**Strongly worth it if:** you have school French or prior exposure to build on, your CRS is short of the general cutoff, you can commit six to twelve months of consistent study, or you are targeting federal public service work.

**Marginal if:** you are starting from nothing, your CRS is already comfortably above cutoffs, and you are targeting private sector roles in Toronto, Calgary or Vancouver where French carries little weight.

**The realistic timeline** from beginner to a testable qualifying level is many months of consistent effort — not a weekend project. But compared with the alternatives for raising a CRS score, which are largely fixed once your education and experience are set, it is one of the few genuinely controllable variables alongside English test improvement.

## Common Mistakes

**Ignoring French draws entirely because you assume you need fluency.** Qualifying levels are defined, and moderate French can meet them.

**Not checking which CLB level triggers points.** Points accrue at band thresholds, so studying without a target level wastes effort inside a band.

**Overlooking francophone provincial streams.** They frequently have lower thresholds than general streams because the candidate pool is smaller.

**Forgetting a spouse's French.** It contributes points and is routinely unclaimed.

**Underpreparing for the speaking component.** Candidates consistently underperform there relative to reading, and it is testable and practisable.

**Writing "conversational French" on a resume.** State a CLB level and the test — vague adjectives are ignored by the people screening for it.

**Treating one test result as final.** Retaking to cross a band threshold is normal and often changes your position materially.

**Assuming French only matters in Quebec.** Federal bilingual positions, New Brunswick and national customer-facing roles all value it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do French-language Express Entry draws really have lower cutoffs?

They have frequently been substantially lower than general draws. Cutoffs vary by draw and change over time, so check the current position with IRCC.

### Do I get points for French if English is my main language?

Yes. French as a second official language adds points at qualifying levels, even when your English is already strong.

### Which French test do I need?

TEF Canada or TCF Canada for immigration purposes. Results map to Canadian Language Benchmark levels, which is what IRCC scores.

### How much French do I need?

Enough to reach the defined qualifying benchmark — moderate proficiency rather than fluency. Identify the specific CLB level that triggers points before setting a study plan.

### Are there francophone immigration streams outside Quebec?

Yes, in several provinces, supporting French-speaking communities outside Quebec. They frequently have lower thresholds than general streams.

### Does French help with jobs, not just immigration?

Yes — federal bilingual-designated positions, roles in Quebec and New Brunswick, and national customer-facing positions all value or require it, and some federal roles pay a bilingualism bonus.

### How long does it take to learn enough?

Many months of consistent study from a low base, less if you have school French to revive. It is one of the few genuinely controllable variables in a CRS score.

## Make This Practical

Find out the exact CLB level that triggers CRS points before studying anything. Points accrue at band thresholds rather than continuously, so a study plan aimed at a specific benchmark is worth far more than general improvement.

Then check whether French changes your eligibility rather than just your score. Category-based French draws have had markedly lower cutoffs than general ones, and francophone provincial streams outside Quebec carry lower thresholds because fewer candidates target them — either can matter more than months of other CRS work.

Finally, treat it as employment value too, not just immigration. Book TEF or TCF early, practise the speaking component specifically since that is where candidates underperform, and state the result on your resume as a CLB level with the test and year rather than as a vague adjective.
