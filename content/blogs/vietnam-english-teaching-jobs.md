## Key Takeaways

- Vietnam has a **very large English teaching market** driven by genuine national demand for English proficiency.
- **Requirements are modest** — typically a degree in any subject, a TEFL or equivalent certificate, and native or near-native English.
- **The employer handles the work permit**, which makes this the most accessible legal route into the country.
- **Pay is modest in absolute terms and comfortable locally**, with enough left over to save if you live sensibly.
- It is a **separate track from professional work** and does not by itself build credentials for a corporate permit.

## The market

Why demand is as large as it is.

**English proficiency is a national priority.** Vietnam's integration into global supply chains and services created substantial demand for English at every level.

**Parents invest heavily** in children's English education, and private language centres are a large and competitive industry.

**Public schools** employ foreign teachers alongside Vietnamese staff, frequently through programmes or partner organisations.

**[Universities](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume)** hire for English and for English-medium subject teaching.

**Corporate training** exists for companies upskilling staff.

**And online teaching** to Vietnamese students is a further market, though it operates differently.

**The [employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) vary enormously.** Large reputable chains with proper contracts sit alongside small centres with poor practices, and the difference in [experience](/blog/resume-matching-experienced-professionals) is substantial.

## Requirements and what they actually mean

The practical bar.

**A bachelor's degree in any subject** is generally required for the work permit, not just for the job. This is a legal requirement rather than an employer preference.

**A TEFL, TESOL or CELTA [certificate](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist).** CELTA is the most respected and the most demanding; a 120-hour TEFL is the common minimum.

**Native or near-native English.** Passport nationality frequently matters to employers and to permit processing, which is a real feature of this market whether or not it is fair.

**A clean criminal record certificate**, legalised.

**A health check.**

**Teaching experience helps** but is not usually essential for entry-level centre work.

**And the same document [legalisation](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide) chain** applies as for any Vietnamese work permit, which is the slowest part.

## What it pays and what it is like

An honest account.

**Pay is quoted hourly or monthly** depending on the employer, and language centre work is frequently hourly with evening and weekend hours.

**In absolute terms it is modest**; against Vietnamese [living costs](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) it supports a comfortable life with saving possible.

**Hours are unsocial** at language centres — evenings and weekends, when students are free.

**Public school hours are more conventional** but pay is frequently lower.

**Contracts vary in quality.** Reputable employers provide proper contracts, handle the permit properly and pay on time; poor ones do none of these.

**[Housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide) is your own responsibility** in most cases, though some employers assist.

**And the work is genuinely demanding.** Teaching well is a skill, classes can be large, and the difference between someone who takes it seriously and someone treating it as a visa is visible to everyone including the students.

## Choosing between employer types

The practical comparison.

**Large language centre chains.** Established processes, proper contracts, reliable permit handling and pay on time. Hours are evenings and weekends, class sizes vary, and the teaching is often to a set curriculum.

**Small independent centres.** Variable quality, ranging from excellent small operations to genuinely poor employers. Research individually and never assume.

**Public schools.** More conventional hours, generally lower pay, larger classes, and frequently arranged through a placement organisation or programme.

**International schools.** The best-paid and most demanding, requiring a genuine teaching qualification such as a PGCE or equivalent plus experience — this is a professional teaching [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide) rather than an entry route.

**Universities.** Reasonable hours, moderate pay, and frequently requiring a master's degree.

**Corporate training.** Daytime hours, business-focused, and generally requiring some professional background alongside teaching capability.

**And online teaching**, which is a different arrangement entirely with its own visa implications if you are physically in Vietnam.

## Making it a good year

Practical advice from people who did it well.

**Learn Vietnamese.** Even basic ability changes your daily life and how you are received completely.

**Take the teaching seriously.** Preparation shows, students respond, and a good reputation opens better positions.

**Save deliberately.** The cost base makes saving genuinely possible on a teaching salary, and people who set a target achieve it.

**Build a network beyond teachers.** The expatriate teaching community is welcoming and it is easy to spend a year entirely inside it, which limits what you get from being there.

**Travel the country.** It is inexpensive and Vietnam is genuinely worth seeing beyond the city you live in.

**And decide what comes next before the year ends.** The people who drift from a teaching year into a second and a third without a plan are frequently the ones who look back with mixed feelings.

## Contracts and what to check

Before you sign anything.

**Whether the employer sponsors the work permit** and who pays for it. Some centres expect teachers to work on tourist visas, which is unlawful and should end the conversation.

**Guaranteed hours** versus hours offered. Hourly contracts with no minimum leave you exposed if student numbers drop.

**The pay rate and when it is paid.** Late payment is a known problem at weaker employers.

**Notice periods** on both sides.

**What happens to your permit if you resign**, since it is tied to the employer.

**Whether there is a contract completion [bonus](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email)** and its conditions.

**Holiday entitlement**, including whether the centre closes for Tet and whether that period is paid.

**Class sizes and age groups**, since teaching young children and teaching adults are entirely different jobs.

**And whether the contract is in English as well as Vietnamese**, with both versions consistent.

## Life as a teacher in Vietnam

The honest picture.

**The teaching community is large and welcoming**, particularly in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi.

**The hours suit some people and not others.** Evenings and weekends free your daytimes, which some find excellent and others find isolating.

**Costs are low enough** that a modest salary supports a good standard of living with saving possible.

**Travel is inexpensive** and Vietnam plus the wider region is accessible.

**Vietnamese is difficult** and even modest ability transforms daily life.

**The work is genuinely tiring** — teaching well takes energy and a full schedule of classes is demanding.

**And a year passes quickly.** The people who get most from it arrive with an intention beyond simply being there, whether that is saving, language learning, travel or working out what comes next.

## Where to look and how to apply

The practical channels.

**Direct to school and centre websites**, which is the most reliable route and lets you research the employer.

**Teaching-specific [job boards](/blog/best-job-search-websites-2026)** covering Vietnam and Southeast Asia.

**Facebook groups** are unusually significant in this market — teacher communities in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi share vacancies and, importantly, warnings about poor employers.

**[Recruitment agencies](/blog/how-to-work-with-recruitment-agencies)** specialising in ESL placement, some good and some not.

**Placement programmes** for public school positions, which handle logistics in exchange for a lower salary.

**And in person.** A substantial number of teachers find their best positions after arriving, once they have a network and can visit schools directly. This carries risk if you arrive without a plan, and it works for people who arrive with savings and patience.

**Ask current teachers.** The single most useful research you can do is speak to someone who works at the school you are considering, and the community is open enough that this is usually possible.

## After the teaching year

What people actually do next.

**Stay and progress within teaching.** Moving from a language centre to an international school, or into teacher training and management, is a genuine career path with a real qualification behind it.

**Move into corporate training** or educational management, where teaching experience plus business capability is valued.

**Transition into another sector**, which requires building the [credentials](/blog/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume) separately — study, remote work in your field, or a transfer from a company at home.

**Move to another country** and teach there, since a year of experience plus a qualification travels across the whole ESL market.

**Return home** with savings, language exposure and a genuinely broadening experience.

**Or start something**, since a number of foreign-founded businesses in Vietnam began with someone who arrived to teach and stayed.

**The people who look back most positively** are those who chose one of these deliberately rather than renewing a contract by default for a third and fourth year without deciding.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You want to live in Vietnam and have a degree

This is the most accessible legal route, and it works. Get a CELTA or a good TEFL, choose a reputable employer, and treat the teaching itself seriously.

### Scenario 2: You want to move into corporate work later

Be deliberate about it. Teaching gets you into the country and gives you time to learn Vietnamese and build a network, but the credentials for a professional permit have to come from elsewhere — study, remote work in your field, or a transfer.

### Scenario 3: You are offered a job by a centre you cannot find much about

Research harder before accepting. The quality gap between employers in this market is very large, and a poor employer means late pay, permit problems and an unpleasant year.

> **Tip:** Choose the employer far more carefully than the location. The gap between a reputable school with proper contracts and permit handling and a poor one is the single biggest determinant of whether your year in Vietnam is good or miserable.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Get a proper teaching qualification
- Research specific employers thoroughly
- Read the contract fully
- Confirm the employer handles the permit
- Legalise documents early
- Take the teaching itself seriously

### Don't

- Accept a role without a written contract
- Assume all centres are equivalent
- Work on a tourist visa
- Treat it as an automatic route into corporate work
- Underestimate the unsocial hours
- Skip the qualification

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing by location not employer.** The employer quality gap is the biggest variable.
- **Working without a proper permit.** Some centres suggest it; it is unlawful and risky.
- **Assuming it leads into business.** It provides presence and time, not corporate credentials.
- **No written contract.** A common problem at smaller centres and entirely avoidable.
- **Underestimating the hours.** Language centre work is evenings and weekends.
- **Not taking the teaching seriously.** It is a real job and doing it badly is visible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do I need to teach English in Vietnam?

Generally a bachelor's degree in any subject, a TEFL, TESOL or CELTA certificate, native or near-native English, a legalised criminal record certificate and a health check.

### Is a degree really required?

Yes, for the work permit rather than merely as an employer preference. It is a legal requirement of the permit process.

### What does teaching pay in Vietnam?

Modest in absolute terms and comfortable against local costs, with saving possible. Language centre work is frequently hourly with evening and weekend hours.

### Does teaching lead into corporate work?

It provides legal presence, time to learn Vietnamese and a network, but it does not build the credentials a professional work permit requires. Treat it as a deliberate step rather than a ladder.

### How do I avoid a bad employer?

Research specific schools rather than the sector, insist on a written contract, confirm they handle the work permit properly, and speak to current or former teachers where you can.

### Which qualification should I get?

CELTA is the most respected and most demanding; a 120-hour TEFL is the common minimum. A better qualification opens better employers.
### Which type of English teaching employer is best in Vietnam?

Large chains offer reliable contracts and permit handling with unsocial hours; public schools have conventional hours and lower pay; international schools pay best but require a genuine teaching qualification.

### How do I make a teaching year in Vietnam worthwhile?

Learn Vietnamese, take the teaching seriously, save deliberately since the cost base makes it possible, build a network beyond other teachers, and decide what comes next before the year ends.
### What should I check in a Vietnamese teaching contract?

Whether the employer sponsors the work permit and pays for it, guaranteed versus offered hours, the pay date, notice periods, what happens to your permit if you resign, and whether Tet closure is paid.
### Where should I look for teaching jobs in Vietnam?

School and centre websites directly, ESL job boards, and Facebook teacher communities which are unusually significant here for both vacancies and warnings about poor employers.
### What comes after teaching English in Vietnam?

Progression within teaching toward international schools or management, corporate training, transitioning to another sector with separately built credentials, teaching elsewhere, returning home with savings, or starting a business.
