## Key Takeaways

- The French CV is **one page** for a graduate — considerably shorter than the German or Swiss convention.
- The **lettre de motivation** is still standard and genuinely read, and a generic one actively damages an otherwise good application.
- **A photo is common but optional**, and increasingly omitted by large and international employers.
- **Education carries unusual weight**, and where you studied does real work on a French CV.
- **Write in French** unless the advert is in English — and a poor French letter is worse than a strong English one.

## The one-page rule

French [recruiters](/blog/how-recruiters-read-resumes) expect concision, and a graduate CV running to [two pages](/blog/ats-resume-length-guide) signals an inability to prioritise.

**One page, always, for anyone under about ten years of experience.** Senior professionals may use two.

**État civil** at the top: name, address, phone, email, and often age or date of birth. Nationality is common, and if you need a work permit, stating your status is genuinely helpful.

**A title.** French CVs frequently carry a short title under the name stating the target role — "Ingénieur Data — Recherche CDI". It orients the reader immediately and its absence is noticeable.

**Formation (education)** often comes before experience for a recent graduate, which reverses the Anglo-American order. Institution, qualification, year, and any distinction.

**Expérience professionnelle**, [reverse chronological](/blog/chronological-vs-functional-resume), including stages and alternance, which are counted as real experience in France rather than discounted as student work.

**Compétences** — technical skills, languages with levels, and software.

**[Centres d'intérêt](/blog/hobbies-and-interests-on-resume).** French CVs conventionally include a short interests line, and it is genuinely read as a signal of personality. Keep it specific — "marathon, photographie argentique" says more than "sport, cinéma".

## The photo question

**It is common and it is optional.**

Traditional French practice includes a small professional photo. Large employers and international companies increasingly discourage it on anonymised-recruitment grounds, and some ATS-driven processes strip it anyway.

**The safe position:** include it for traditional French employers and smaller companies, omit it for large corporates, international firms and anywhere the advert mentions anonymised recruitment. If you include one, make it a plain professional headshot.

## The lettre de motivation

Unlike the UK, where [cover letters](/blog/cover-letter-generator-by-country) are frequently skimmed, the French letter is a real part of the assessment.

**One page. Three or four paragraphs.**

**The classical structure** is sometimes described as *vous, moi, nous* — you, me, us. Open with the company and why it interests you specifically; continue with what you bring and the evidence for it; close with what the two of you would do together.

**Address a named person** where possible, with the correct formula. "Madame, Monsieur," is the safe default when you genuinely cannot find a name.

**The closing formula is formal and fixed.** "Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées" or a close variant. This is not optional flourish — it is the convention, and getting it wrong marks you out immediately.

**Do not restate the CV.** The letter connects your specific background to their specific need.

**Write it in French for a French-language advert.** If your French will not support a professional letter unaided, that is meaningful information about whether the role suits you. A [tailored draft](/cover-letter) built against the actual posting solves the blank page, but the final language should be yours.

## Stages and alternance on a CV

This is where an international CV most often undersells itself in France.

**[Internships count](/blog/internship-resume-guide) as experience.** A six-month stage at a recognised company is real professional experience in French eyes, and it belongs in the experience section described with outcomes, not in a student activities footnote.

**Alternance is highly regarded.** An apprenticeship contract combining study and work signals both academic capability and professional maturity, and French recruiters read it very positively.

**Describe them properly.** Employer, dates, team, what you actually did, and what came of it. "Stage de fin d'études" with no description wastes the strongest thing on many graduate CVs.

**Long-term casual work counts too.** Sustained part-time employment alongside studies is read as evidence of seriousness rather than as filler.

## Automated screening and French employers

Large French employers screen with software, and the design instincts the market rewards can work against you here.

**Keep the layout single-column.** Two-column CVs are visually common in France and they [parse badly](/blog/ats-resume-parser-friendly-format), which means a document that looks polished to a human can arrive at the recruiter as scrambled text.

**Use conventional section headings** — Formation, Expérience professionnelle, Compétences, Langues. A creatively named section is one that does not get extracted.

**Avoid text inside images or icons.** Icon-heavy contact blocks are a French design habit that costs you information in a parser.

**Mirror the advert's vocabulary exactly**, in French. Matching is literal, and a synonym does not count.

**[Export as PDF](/blog/ats-file-format-guide)** with a sensible filename — "Dupont_Marie_CV.pdf".

**Check it parses** with an [ATS scan](/ats-analysis) before you send it anywhere. It takes a minute and it removes a whole category of silent [rejection](/blog/how-to-handle-job-rejection).

## Tailoring, and the details that get noticed

French readers are attentive to form, and small errors carry disproportionate weight.

**Accents matter.** A CV in French with missing accents reads as careless at best and as a weak language claim at worst.

**Be consistent with dates and formatting.** French documents are read as formal records.

**Claim [language levels](/blog/bilingual-language-skills-on-resume) honestly**, using CEFR. If you write C1 French, part of the interview will be in French.

**Adapt the [title line](/blog/how-to-write-resume-headline) per application.** It takes ten seconds and it makes each CV look aimed rather than broadcast.

**Have a native speaker read the letter.** The lettre de motivation is the document where errors are least forgiven, because the whole point of it is that you wrote it for them.

**Keep an English version current.** Paris technology, research institutions and international companies will want one, and maintaining both means you can respond to either the day a posting appears.

## Adapting an international CV for France

Three shifts that most international candidates do not make.

**Reorder for a French reader.** Education first for a graduate, a title line under the name, and the interests section that would look odd elsewhere. These are conventions, and following them signals familiarity with the market.

**Translate your qualification into recognisable terms.** French readers calibrate carefully on education, and a degree title with no French equivalent needs one clarifying line — the level, the duration, and the field.

**Convert achievements into French professional register.** Anglo-American CVs use strong claiming verbs; French CVs are somewhat more measured. Describe what you did precisely and let the results carry the emphasis rather than the adjectives.

**Include the stage properly.** This is worth saying twice because it is the most commonly wasted asset on an international CV in France.

**Keep the English version too.** Paris technology and research run in English, and having both ready means you can respond to either the day it appears.

## Following up in France

The etiquette is specific and mostly unwritten.

**One [follow-up after](/blog/how-to-follow-up-after-job-application) ten days to two weeks** is normal and welcome. Use a formal register.

**Use the correct formulas** even in a short email. The opening ("Madame, Monsieur," or a named form) and the closing formula matter as much in an email as in the letter.

**Do not chase repeatedly.** French hiring is not fast, particularly at large employers where multiple approvals are involved, and impatience is read as poor judgement.

**[Networking](/blog/networking-tips-for-job-search) works, quietly.** French professional networks — school alumni associations especially — are effective, and a *cooptation* [referral](/blog/networking-messages-for-referrals) from an employee carries real weight and often a bonus for the referrer.

**Salon and forum recruitment events** are a genuine channel in France, including school-specific forums that international candidates rarely think to attend.

## Interviewing in France

The document gets you the *entretien*; the interview has its own conventions.

**Formality first.** Vouvoiement, a handshake, business dress at most employers. Wait to be invited to sit.

**Expect intellectual questions.** French interviews often probe how you think rather than only what you did — a question about a trade-off, a judgement, or an opinion on something in your field. Answering with a considered position and its reasoning lands better than a rehearsed story.

**Your education will come up.** Where you studied, what you specialised in, and why. Have a clear account of it.

**Case and technical exercises** are common in consulting, finance and engineering.

**Prepare a *présentation de votre parcours*.** A two-minute account of your career path is a standard opening request, and a rambling one sets a poor tone.

**Ask about the role, the team and the company's direction.** Curiosity is expected, and a candidate with no questions reads as a candidate with no interest.

**Salary comes up later** than in the Netherlands or the UK, and is usually discussed in gross annual terms.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are applying to a large French corporate

Omit the photo, keep to one page, place education prominently, and write a formal French letter with the correct closing formula. Convention here is the message.

### Scenario 2: You are applying to a Paris startup with an English advert

Apply in English, keep it to one page, and lead with what you have built. Station F-adjacent companies are the most internationally normal part of the French market.

### Scenario 3: Your only experience is internships

Describe them as jobs. In France a six-month stage is genuine professional experience, and burying it under a student heading gives away the strongest material you have.

> **Tip:** Put a short title line under your name — "Développeuse Full-Stack — Recherche CDI". French recruiters use it to place you within two seconds, and CVs without one make the reader do work that a competitor's CV did for them.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Keep it to one page
- Add a title line stating your target role
- Place education prominently
- Describe stages and alternance as real experience
- Use the correct formal closing in the letter
- Include a specific interests line

### Don't

- Run to two pages as a graduate
- Skip the lettre de motivation
- Use an informal or invented closing formula
- Bury internships under student activities
- Include a photo without considering the employer
- Write in French beyond your actual level

## Common Mistakes

- **Two pages.** For a graduate it signals an inability to prioritise.
- **A generic cover letter.** The French letter is genuinely read, so a generic one is worse than none.
- **Wrong closing formula.** The conventions are fixed and errors are conspicuous.
- **Underselling internships.** A stage is real experience in France.
- **Omitting the title line.** It costs nothing and orients the reader instantly.
- **Skipping centres d'intérêt.** It is a genuine convention, not filler.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a French CV be?

One page for a graduate and for anyone under roughly ten years of experience. Two pages is for senior professionals only.

### Should I include a photo?

It is common but optional. Include it for traditional and smaller French employers; omit it for large corporates, international firms, and anywhere anonymised recruitment is mentioned.

### Is the lettre de motivation really read?

Yes, genuinely, unlike cover letters in some markets. A generic one damages an otherwise strong application.

### Do internships count as experience in France?

Very much so. A six-month stage is real professional experience, and alternance is regarded highly. Describe both properly in the experience section.

### What is the correct closing formula?

A formal set phrase such as "Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées". Improvised closings are conspicuous.

### Should I include a centres d'intérêt section?

Yes — it is a genuine French convention and it is read. Keep it specific rather than listing generic categories.
### Do French employers use automated screening?

Larger ones do. Keep the layout single-column, use conventional section headings, avoid icons and text in images, and check the document parses before sending.

### Do accents really matter on a French CV?

Yes. Missing accents read as careless and undermine any language level you have claimed elsewhere on the document.
### How should I follow up on a French application?

One formal follow-up after ten days to two weeks, using proper opening and closing formulas. Repeated chasing is read as poor judgement.

### Does referral work in France?

Yes. Cooptation — employee referral — carries real weight and often a bonus for the referrer, and school alumni networks are effective channels.
