## Key Takeaways

- A high score confirms legibility and keyword relevance. It cannot confirm that your resume is persuasive, because no scanner assesses whether your work was any good.
- Five causes account for nearly all of this: duty-statement bullets, a buried lead, a seniority mismatch, wrong targeting, and applicant volume.
- Duties-instead-of-results is the most common and the most fixable, and it usually takes an hour to correct across a whole resume.
- Your response pattern tells you which cause you have - no responses at all means something different from screens without manager rounds.
- Past a workable score, more keyword work returns almost nothing. Bullet quality returns a great deal.

You scan at 85%. The missing-keyword list is nearly empty. And nobody calls.

This is a genuinely different problem from a low score, and continuing to optimise keywords will not touch it. The scanner stage is solved. What follows is a human spending a few seconds deciding whether to spend ten minutes more.

Here is what they are deciding on, and the five things that usually go wrong.

## What a Score Cannot Measure

A scanner can confirm that "stakeholder management" appears in your resume. It cannot assess whether you managed two stakeholders or twenty, whether the outcome was good, whether you led the work or attended the meetings, or whether the sentence would impress anyone who does that job for a living.

Those are exactly the judgments a hiring manager makes in the moment after the scan stops mattering.

So a high score with weak content means: **legible, relevant, unconvincing.** That combination produces the specific pattern of never being rejected outright and never being called — which is what makes it so hard to diagnose from the outside.

## Cause 1: Your Bullets List Duties, Not Results

This is the most common cause by a wide margin, and a scanner cannot detect it.

"Responsible for managing the reporting process" contains good keywords and tells a hiring manager nothing about whether you are any good at it.

> **Scores well, persuades nobody:** Responsible for managing weekly sales reporting and communicating results to stakeholders.
>
> **Scores the same, persuades:** Rebuilt weekly sales reporting from four manual spreadsheets into one automated dashboard, cutting turnaround from two days to 40 minutes and removing a recurring source of month-end errors.

The second version contains the same keywords. It also contains a before, an after, and a consequence — which is what the reader is actually looking for.

### The test

Read each bullet and ask **"so what?"** If the answer is not in the bullet, it is a duty, not an achievement.

Apply this to your top five bullets first. Those are the ones that get read; the rest are skimmed. [Weak bullets behind a good score](/blog/weak-bullets-behind-good-score) covers the four patterns this takes.

### What to do when you have no metrics

Plenty of real work is never measured. The fix is not invention — it is stating the change precisely.

- "Removed a recurring source of month-end errors" — a real outcome, no metric
- "Cut the approval step from three signatures to one" — concrete without a percentage
- "Became the person the team asked when the pipeline broke" — scope, honestly stated

What does not work is adjectives. "Significantly improved efficiency" is what readers discount automatically.

## Cause 2: Your Best Work Is on Page Two

A recruiter's first pass covers the top third of page one: your summary, your most recent title, and the first two or three bullets under it.

If your strongest, most relevant achievement is the fourth bullet of your second job, it is effectively invisible during the decision that matters. Since [recruiters decide in about six seconds](/blog/how-recruiters-read-resume-6-seconds), position is not a minor detail.

**The fix is ordering, not writing.** For each application, make sure the single most relevant thing you have done appears in the top third. That may mean reordering bullets within a role, or naming the achievement in your summary so it registers early even when the detail sits lower.

This is the cheapest fix on this page. It takes about a minute and requires no new content — see [which three bullets to rewrite](/blog/which-bullets-to-rewrite) for how to choose.

## Cause 3: You Are Reading as the Wrong Level

Scanners match terms. Humans read seniority signals — scope, autonomy, who you influenced, what you owned.

There are two failure modes, and they need opposite fixes.

| Failure | What the resume shows | What the reader concludes | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading junior | Tasks completed, tools used | "Executes well, needs direction" | Foreground ownership, ambiguity, decisions |
| Reading senior | Strategy, oversight, headcount | "Won't do the hands-on work" | Keep specifics of what you personally built |

**Reading junior for a senior role** is the more common of the two. Your bullets describe tasks rather than outcomes you owned, and nothing indicates you set direction or worked across teams.

**Reading senior for a hands-on role** catches experienced people applying to smaller companies. Everything is strategy, and a hiring manager who needs someone to do the work cannot see evidence you still do it.

The fix in both cases is matching scope language to the level, which [tailoring for a stretch role](/blog/executive-resume-tailoring-guide) covers in more depth.

## Cause 4: You Are Applying to the Wrong Jobs, Well

A high score is achievable on a posting you are not competitive for. Keyword overlap is not the same as candidacy.

If you are consistently three or more years short of the stated experience, missing the central credential, or crossing an industry boundary with no bridging evidence, the resume is doing its job and the targeting is the problem.

### How to check

Look at your last twenty applications and sort them honestly:

| Category | Healthy mix |
|---|---|
| Roles you clearly match | 50–60% |
| Slight stretches (1–2 years, one missing tool) | 30–40% |
| Genuine long shots | 10% or less |

If most were stretches, the answer is not another resume edit. It is a more realistic band of roles, plus a few stretches rather than only stretches.

Reading the posting properly helps here — [how to read a job description like a recruiter wrote it](/blog/read-a-jd-like-a-recruiter) shows which requirements actually decide the shortlist.

## Cause 5: Volume and Timing

Some of this is not about you, and it is worth naming so you do not over-correct on a document that is already fine.

- A posting open three weeks with six hundred applicants gives even strong resumes poor odds
- Roles filled internally get posted anyway for compliance
- Hiring freezes arrive mid-process
- Some postings are collecting a pipeline rather than hiring now

**What you can control:** apply earlier where possible, prioritise postings under a week old, and treat referral routes as worth more than additional cold applications. A referred application is read differently regardless of score.

## Diagnosing Which One You Have

Your response pattern is the most reliable signal available:

| What you see | Most likely cause | Where to work |
|---|---|---|
| No responses at all, 20+ applications | Evidence quality or targeting | Causes 1 and 4 |
| Occasional recruiter screens, no manager rounds | Seniority or evidence depth | Causes 1 and 3 |
| Manager rounds but no offers | Resume is working | Interview prep, not the resume |
| Responses only from smaller companies | Brand or scope signal | Cause 3 |
| Responses only from agencies | Targeting or level mismatch | Causes 3 and 4 |

The third row matters most. If you are reaching manager rounds, your resume has done everything a resume can do, and further edits are wasted effort.

## Rewriting Your Top Five Bullets: A Worked Pass

Cause 1 is the one to fix first, so here is the actual mechanics rather than the principle.

### Step 1: Find the five that get read

Your first two or three bullets under your current role, plus your single strongest achievement wherever it sits. Nothing else is read closely on a first pass.

### Step 2: Mark each as duty or outcome

Read each and ask "so what?" If the bullet ends without answering that, mark it.

> - Responsible for weekly sales reporting ← **duty**
> - Managed relationships with key accounts ← **duty**
> - Reduced reporting turnaround from two days to 40 minutes ← **outcome**

### Step 3: Add the missing half

A duty describes what you were assigned. An outcome describes what changed. Most duty bullets already contain the first half — you are adding the second.

| Duty statement | What is missing | Rewritten |
|---|---|---|
| Responsible for weekly reporting | What improved | Rebuilt weekly reporting into an automated dashboard, cutting turnaround from two days to 40 minutes |
| Managed key accounts | Scale and result | Managed 12 enterprise accounts worth £2.4m, lifting renewal rate from 78% to 91% |
| Supported the migration | Your role and effect | Led data validation for the CRM migration, catching 400 duplicate records before cutover |
| Handled customer escalations | Volume and outcome | Resolved ~30 escalations monthly, reducing repeat contacts by a third |

### Step 4: Check the verb

*Responsible for*, *supported*, *assisted with* and *helped* all signal proximity to work rather than ownership of it. Where you genuinely owned something, say *led*, *built*, *owned*, *ran*.

Where you genuinely did support, keep the honest verb — [claiming work accurately](/blog/tailoring-without-lying) matters more than sounding senior, and interviewers probe inflated verbs first.

### Step 5: Re-scan once and expect a small drop

Stronger bullets are usually longer and more specific, which dilutes keyword density slightly. A two or three point fall here is the correct trade and should be kept — the reasoning is in [why your score drops after editing](/blog/ats-score-dropped-after-edit).

## Common Mistakes at This Stage

**Continuing to optimise keywords.** Past a good range this returns nothing and starts damaging readability — [what counts as a good score](/blog/good-ats-score-when-to-stop) covers where the curve flattens.

**Rewriting the whole resume.** The top five bullets carry the decision. Rewriting bullets under a job from six years ago changes nothing.

**Adding more content.** A longer resume dilutes relevance density. Usually the fix is cutting, not adding — [what to delete from a resume](/blog/weak-resume-bullets-fix) covers what goes first.

**Redesigning it.** Recruiters read content. Design matters only when it obstructs.

**Over-tailoring.** A resume that mirrors the posting too closely reads as generic and reveals nothing distinctive about you. [Tailoring without lying](/blog/tailoring-without-lying) covers where the line sits.

**Blaming the ATS.** At 85% the scanner has already passed you. The problem is downstream, and [ATS score versus recruiter judgment](/blog/ats-score-vs-recruiter-judgment) explains why those are different gates.

**Ignoring how the resume is read on screen.** Recruiters skim in a predictable pattern, and [how recruiters actually read resumes](/blog/how-recruiters-read-resumes) is worth knowing before you decide where your evidence should sit.

## A Worked Fix

A marketing analyst, scanning at 84%, forty applications, two screens, no manager rounds.

Her top three bullets read:

> - Produced weekly and monthly campaign performance reports
> - Managed the marketing calendar across three channels
> - Ran A/B tests on email subject lines and landing pages

Every keyword the posting wanted was present. Nothing said whether she was good.

Rewritten, same facts:

> - Ran 24 A/B tests across email and landing pages; the winning onboarding variant lifted week-one retention from 41% to 58%
> - Rebuilt campaign reporting into a self-serve dashboard, ending a weekly two-hour manual process
> - Owned the content calendar across three channels, coordinating four freelancers to deadline

Her score moved from 84% to 81% — slightly down, because the bullets are longer and more specific. She had three manager rounds in the following month.

That trade is the whole point. The score was never the binding constraint.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I keep optimising keywords if my score is already high?

No. Past a good range, further keyword work adds nothing measurable and usually costs readability. Move to evidence quality — that is where the remaining gains are.

### How do I know if my bullets are strong enough?

Ask whether each one contains a result a stranger would find impressive without knowing your company. If it needs context you have not given, rewrite it.

### Is my resume design the problem?

Rarely, if it is clean and parses. Recruiters read content. Design matters mainly when it obstructs — dense text, tiny type, or a layout that hides the top third.

### Could my resume be too tailored?

Yes. A resume that mirrors the posting too closely reads as generic and reveals nothing distinctive. Tailoring should surface your relevant evidence, not replace your voice.

### How many applications before I conclude something is wrong?

Twenty to thirty well-matched applications with no responses is a real signal. Fewer than ten tells you very little — variance at that sample size is enormous.

### Does a referral actually change anything at this stage?

Substantially. A referred application is usually read by a human first, which bypasses the ranking stage entirely and changes who decides whether you progress.

### My score is high and I get screens but never offers. Is it the resume?

No. If you are reaching interviews, the resume has done its job. The problem has moved to interview performance, and no resume edit addresses that.

### Should I shorten my resume?

Often yes, if it is over two pages and the extra length is older roles. Cutting increases relevance density and pushes your best work higher. The [resume length guide](/blog/ats-resume-length-guide) covers what to cut first.

### How long should fixing the bullets take?

About an hour for your top five, and it is the highest-return hour in a job search — those rewrites carry across every future application rather than being redone each time.

### Does this change if I am applying through referrals?

The resume matters just as much, but it is read differently — usually by a human first, with more attention and more benefit of the doubt. Weak bullets still cost you, they just cost you later in the process.

### My bullets have numbers and I still get no calls. What then?

Check that the numbers mean something to an outsider. "Processed 400 tickets" is a volume, not an achievement. "Cut average resolution time from 3 days to 8 hours" is the same work stated as a result.

### Can I fix all five causes at once?

Do them in order: bullets, then placement, then level framing, then targeting. Volume is outside your control, so address it last by applying earlier rather than editing more.

## What to Do Next

Start with cause 1, because it is the most common and the fix carries across every future application. Take your top five bullets and rewrite any that do not answer "so what?" That is an hour of work that improves every application you send from now on.

Then check placement — a minute per application, reordering so your most relevant evidence sits in the top third.

Only after those two should you question your targeting. Most people jump straight there and conclude the market is broken, when the resume was losing applications it should have won.

If you want to confirm the scanner stage is genuinely handled before you move on, [scan your resume against a posting](/ats-analysis) — then close the report and spend your time on whether each of your top bullets would convince someone who does this job.
