## Key Takeaways

- Job Bank publishes official wage data by occupation and region, which is the strongest free source available anywhere in Canada.
- Pay transparency rules are expanding across provinces, so more postings now carry ranges.
- Provincial differences are large — the same role pays very differently in Toronto, Calgary and Halifax, and costs differ more.
- Newcomers routinely accept below market because they lack a local benchmark; Job Bank fixes that.
- Compare total compensation including pension, health benefits and vacation, not base salary alone.

Canadian salary research is genuinely easier than in most countries, because the federal government publishes official wage data by occupation and region. Almost nobody uses it.

That matters most for newcomers, who arrive without a local reference point, are anxious about the Canadian experience objection, and accept the first number offered — a gap that then compounds through every subsequent raise and move.

This guide covers the reliable sources and how to turn them into a defensible number.

## Job Bank wage data

The best free salary source in Canada, and the starting point for everything.

**What it gives you:** low, median and high wage figures for a given occupation in a given province or region, drawn from official labour market data.

**Why it is powerful:**

- **It is official**, not crowdsourced, so citing it in a negotiation carries weight
- **It is specific to your NOC code**, so you are comparing the right occupation
- **It is regional**, so you get Calgary figures rather than a national average
- **It is free** and requires no account

**How to use it:** find your NOC code, look up the wage data for your target province, and note the median as your anchor rather than the low figure.

It also carries job outlook data by region, which is useful when choosing where to target — see [the Canada Job Bank guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-job-bank-guide).

## Pay transparency

**Rules are expanding.** Several provinces have introduced or legislated requirements for salary ranges in job postings, with British Columbia and Ontario among those moving in this direction. Specifics and timing vary by province.

**Where ranges are published**, negotiation shifts from guessing to positioning within a stated band — a much better position.

**Read twenty postings** for your title in your target city regardless. Even where ranges are not mandated, enough employers publish voluntarily to establish a pattern.

## Other sources

**Recruitment agency salary guides.** Several major agencies publish annual Canadian guides by sector and city, based on placements made. Useful for technology, finance and professional roles.

**Professional associations** publish member salary surveys, particularly for engineering, accounting and IT.

**Public sector and union scales** are published outright. Government, healthcare and education pay grids tell you exactly what a comparable public role pays, which is a useful anchor even if you are not applying — see [the Canada healthcare jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-healthcare-jobs-guide).

**Crowdsourced sites.** Directionally useful, unreliable for precision — self-reported and often stale.

**Recruiters directly.** Ask what the range is. They know, and a placement is in their interest.

## Provincial differences

Larger than people expect, and they run in both directions.

**Salaries vary substantially** between provinces for the same role — Toronto and Vancouver typically highest for technology, Alberta strong in energy-adjacent roles, Atlantic provinces generally lower.

**Costs vary more.** Toronto and Vancouver housing is dramatically more expensive than Calgary, Ottawa, Winnipeg or Halifax.

**Provincial income tax differs**, sometimes meaningfully, so the same gross produces different take-home by province.

**Which means the comparison is:**

> (Provincial salary − provincial housing − provincial tax) for each option

That calculation frequently favours somewhere other than the highest-paying city. ## Total compensation

Base salary alone misreads Canadian offers.

**Pension or RRSP matching.** Varies enormously — from nothing to a substantial matched contribution. Public sector and large employers frequently offer defined benefit or generous matched plans, worth thousands annually.

**Health and dental benefits.** Provincial health insurance covers core medical care, but prescriptions, dental, vision, physiotherapy and mental health support come through employer plans. The gap between a good plan and a minimal one is real money.

**Vacation.** Statutory minimums are set provincially, commonly two weeks initially. Many employers offer three or four, and extra weeks are often easier to negotiate than salary.

**Bonus.** Ask what it has actually paid, not the maximum.

**Professional fees and training**, particularly relevant for regulated professions — see [the Canada regulated professions guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-regulated-professions-guide).

## Newcomers specifically

**Do not anchor on your foreign salary.** Different market, currency and cost base. You are not obliged to disclose it, and if you do it caps you.

**Do not accept low because of the Canadian experience objection.** It is a real dynamic and not a reason to take substantially below market. Job Bank data is official, free and specific to your occupation and province — it is a credible basis for a counter regardless of where your experience was earned. See [the Canadian experience problem](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canadian-experience-problem).

**Understand the deductions.** Gross is not take-home — federal and provincial income tax, Canada Pension Plan contributions and Employment Insurance premiums all come off.

## Common Mistakes

**Not using Job Bank.** It is official, free, occupation-specific and regional — the strongest salary source in the country and most candidates have never opened it.

**Anchoring on the low figure rather than the median.** The median is the defensible anchor; the low end is where inexperienced candidates land themselves.

**Comparing provinces on salary alone.** Housing and provincial tax differ enough that the highest-paying city frequently is not the best outcome.

**Newcomers accepting the first offer.** The Canadian experience objection is real but is not a reason to take substantially below documented market rate.

**Disclosing a foreign salary.** Different market and currency, no obligation to share it, and doing so caps your offer.

**Ignoring pension and health benefits.** The spread between a generous plan and a minimal one is worth thousands annually.

**Taking a stated maximum bonus at face value.** Ask what it has actually paid recently.

**Forgetting vacation is negotiable.** Extra weeks above the provincial minimum are frequently easier to obtain than salary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best source for Canadian salary data?

Job Bank, which publishes official low, median and high wage figures by occupation and region. It is free, specific to your NOC code, and credible to cite in a negotiation.

### Do Canadian job postings show salary?

Increasingly. Several provinces have introduced pay transparency requirements, and many employers publish voluntarily even where not required.

### How much do salaries differ between provinces?

Substantially, and costs differ more. Toronto and Vancouver typically pay highest for technology but have far higher housing costs than Calgary, Ottawa or Halifax.

### Should newcomers negotiate?

Yes. Accepting substantially below market compounds through every subsequent raise, and Job Bank data gives you an official benchmark regardless of where your experience was gained.

### Do I have to disclose my previous salary?

No, and if it was earned abroad it is not a meaningful benchmark for Canadian work. Redirect to the market rate for the role.

### What benefits matter most?

Pension or RRSP matching and the health and dental plan. Provincial healthcare does not cover prescriptions, dental or vision, so the employer plan carries real value.

### How much vacation should I expect?

Provincial minimums are commonly two weeks initially, with many employers offering three or four. Extra weeks are frequently easier to negotiate than base salary.

## Make This Practical

Look up your NOC code on Job Bank before any conversation about money. Official low, median and high wages for your occupation in your target province, free and citable — it is the strongest salary evidence available in Canada and the fastest fix for a newcomer with no local benchmark.

Then compare provinces on what remains rather than on gross. Subtract housing and provincial income tax for each option, because the highest-paying city frequently is not the one that leaves you better off.

Finally, negotiate on total compensation. Add pension matching, the health and dental plan, and vacation above the provincial minimum — then make one specific ask anchored on the Job Bank median, and never disclose a foreign salary that would cap you below it.
