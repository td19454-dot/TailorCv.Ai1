## Key Takeaways

- Thailand has one of Asia's **longest-established English teaching markets**, with a large and varied employer base.
- **A degree, a TEFL certificate and a teaching licence or waiver** are the standard requirements, and the licensing element is stricter than many people expect.
- **Employer quality varies enormously**, from well-run international schools to agencies with poor practices.
- **International schools are a professional career** requiring proper qualifications and paying substantially more than language centres.
- **Working without a proper permit is unlawful** and enforcement has become more active.

## The market

Its shape and scale.

**Government schools** employ a very large number of foreign teachers, frequently through agencies that place teachers into schools — an arrangement worth understanding because the agency, not the school, is your [employer](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes).

**Private and bilingual schools** offer better conditions and pay than government placements.

**International schools** are the top of the market, requiring genuine teaching qualifications and paying substantially more, with proper contracts and [benefits](/blog/questions-to-ask-in-an-interview).

**Language centres** teach children and adults outside school hours, with evening and weekend schedules.

**[Universities](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume)** hire for English and English-medium teaching, frequently requiring a master's degree.

**Corporate training** for companies upskilling staff.

**And online teaching**, which operates separately with its own considerations.

**The quality spread is the defining feature.** A well-run international school and a poorly run agency placement are entirely different experiences of the same country.

## Requirements, properly

Stricter than the reputation suggests.

**A bachelor's degree** in any subject, required for the work permit.

**A TEFL, TESOL or CELTA [certificate](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist)**, with CELTA the most respected.

**A teacher licence or a waiver.** Thailand requires foreign teachers in formal education to hold a teaching licence from the Teachers Council, or a temporary waiver while working toward one. Waivers are limited in number and duration, which surprises people.

**A criminal record check**, legalised.

**A health certificate.**

**Degree verification and [legalisation](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide)**, which takes time.

**And for international schools**, a genuine teaching qualification — PGCE, a state teaching licence or equivalent — plus [experience](/blog/resume-matching-experienced-professionals). This is a professional teaching [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide), not an entry route.

**The licence requirement in particular** is the element most commonly misunderstood, and it has become more strictly applied over time.

## Pay, conditions and the honest picture

What to expect.

**Government school placements** pay the least, with agency placements paying less than direct school employment.

**Private and bilingual schools** pay more.

**International schools** pay substantially more, comparable to or exceeding teaching [salaries](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) in some Western countries when [housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide) and benefits are included.

**Language centres** pay hourly with unsocial hours.

**Against Thai [living costs](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist)**, even modest teaching pay supports a comfortable life outside the expensive expatriate areas.

**Saving is possible** but limited at the lower end of the market.

**Contracts vary in quality.** Reputable employers provide proper contracts, handle the permit and licence properly, and pay reliably; poor ones do not.

**And agencies deserve particular scrutiny.** Some are professional; others take a substantial cut, provide little support and handle documentation poorly.

## Where to look and how to choose

The practical channels and what to check.

**School and international school websites directly**, which is the most reliable route.

**Teaching-specific [job boards](/blog/best-job-search-websites-2026)** covering Thailand and the region.

**Facebook teacher communities** in Bangkok and Chiang Mai, which share both vacancies and warnings.

**[Recruitment agencies](/blog/how-to-work-with-recruitment-agencies)**, with the caveat about quality.

**International school recruitment fairs**, which run on an annual cycle and are the main channel for qualified teachers.

**And in person**, which some teachers do successfully after arriving with savings and time.

**What to check about any employer:** whether they handle the work permit and licence properly, whether the contract is written and in English, what the actual teaching hours and class sizes are, whether pay has been reliable for current staff, and whether they are the school or an agency placing you into one.

**Speak to a current teacher.** It is the single most useful piece of research available and the community is generally open to it.

## Making a teaching year work

Practical advice.

**Learn some Thai.** It transforms daily life and how you are received, and it is achievable to a functional level in a year.

**Take the teaching seriously.** It is a real job, students respond to preparation, and a good reputation opens better positions.

**Understand the school calendar.** Thai academic terms and the long breaks affect pay at some employers, particularly agency placements, and clarifying whether breaks are paid is important.

**Budget realistically.** Teaching salaries support a comfortable life outside expensive areas and limited saving.

**Build a network beyond teachers**, since it is easy to spend a year entirely inside the teaching community.

**Travel.** Thailand and the region are inexpensive and genuinely worth seeing.

**And decide what comes next.** The people who look back most positively treated it as a deliberate period with a purpose rather than renewing by default.

## After teaching

Where it leads.

**Progression within teaching**, toward international schools with a proper qualification, or into teacher training and management.

**Corporate training or educational management**, where teaching experience plus business capability is valued.

**Another country**, since a qualification plus experience travels across the whole ESL market.

**Another sector**, which requires building [credentials](/blog/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume) separately.

**Or home**, with savings, experience and a broadened view.

**The route into professional work in Thailand specifically** is difficult from teaching, since the restricted occupation list and employer sponsorship conditions apply regardless of your time in the country.

## Realistic expectations

An honest summary for anyone considering it.

**It is a real job.** Teaching a class of thirty children with limited English, five days a week, is demanding work that requires preparation and energy.

**The pay is modest** and supports a comfortable life rather than an affluent one.

**The employer determines the experience** more than the location does.

**The legal requirements are real** and have become stricter — degree, certificate, licence or waiver, proper permit.

**The country is genuinely wonderful to live in**, which is why the market exists and why so many people stay.

**And it is a defined period for most people**, which is fine provided you treat it as one rather than drifting through several years without deciding.

**The people who do it well** arrive qualified, choose their employer carefully, take the teaching seriously, learn some Thai, save deliberately and decide what comes next before the contract ends.

## Beyond Bangkok

Where teaching jobs are and how they differ.

**Bangkok** has the most positions, the widest quality range and the highest costs.

**Chiang Mai** is popular with teachers for lifestyle, with lower pay and a substantial competing supply of applicants.

**Provincial towns and the northeast** offer government school positions with lower pay, much lower costs and a genuinely different Thai experience — and frequently the warmest reception.

**Resort areas** — Phuket, Samui, Hua Hin — have positions serving both local and international communities.

**The Eastern Seaboard** has demand connected to the industrial and expatriate communities there.

**Rural placements** are the least contested and the most immersive, and they suit people who want the experience rather than the city.

**The trade-off is consistent:** the more desirable the location, the more competition and the lower the relative pay, since employers know people want to be there.

## The financial reality over a year

What teaching in Thailand actually leaves you with.

**Monthly pay** at government and agency placements is the lowest tier; private and bilingual schools pay more; international schools substantially more.

**Rent outside expensive areas** is modest, and teachers commonly live comfortably on local terms.

**Food and transport are inexpensive**, particularly outside central Bangkok.

**Saving is possible** at private and international schools and difficult at the bottom of the market.

**Unpaid school breaks** are the trap at some employers, particularly agency placements, and clarifying whether the long April break is paid materially changes your annual income.

**Visa and permit costs** may or may not be covered by the employer.

**Flights home** are rarely included outside international schools.

**And the honest arithmetic:** teaching in Thailand funds a good life in Thailand. It does not usually fund saving at a rate that changes your financial position materially, unless you are at an international school.

## A final word on expectations

The honest summary for anyone weighing this.

**Teaching in Thailand is a genuine experience** and it is not, for most people, a long-term career with compounding financial returns.

**It works best as a defined chapter** — a year or two, entered deliberately, with a plan for what follows.

**People who treat it as a career** generally need to move into international schools with proper qualifications, or into management, or accept a plateau.

**People who treat it as a gap** and then return to their field frequently find it was worth doing and cost them less than they feared.

**And people who drift** — extending indefinitely without a plan — are the ones who most often regret it, usually around year five when the savings position becomes clear.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are a qualified teacher

Target international schools. The pay, conditions and professional standing are entirely different from the language centre market, and your qualification is what unlocks them.

### Scenario 2: You have a degree and want to live in Thailand

The standard route is a TEFL plus a government or private school position. Research the employer or agency thoroughly, understand the licence requirement, and insist on proper documentation.

### Scenario 3: You are offered a role by an agency you cannot verify

Be cautious. Agency quality varies enormously, and a poor one means a lower salary, weak support and potentially improper documentation. Speak to teachers currently placed by them.

> **Tip:** Understand the teacher licence requirement before you arrive. Thailand requires foreign teachers in formal education to hold a licence or a limited waiver, it is more strictly applied than it used to be, and a lot of casual advice about Thai teaching predates that.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Get a proper teaching qualification
- Understand the licence and waiver position
- Research the specific employer or agency
- Read the contract fully
- Target international schools if qualified
- Legalise documents early

### Don't

- Work without a proper permit
- Assume all agencies are equivalent
- Ignore the teacher licence requirement
- Treat international school teaching as an entry route
- Accept a role without written terms
- Rely on advice that predates the licensing rules

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring the licence requirement.** More strictly applied than older advice suggests.
- **Not distinguishing agency from school employment.** The agency is your employer and its quality determines your experience.
- **Assuming all teaching jobs are similar.** International schools and language centres are entirely different markets.
- **Working without a permit.** Unlawful and increasingly enforced.
- **No written contract.** A recurring problem at the weaker end of the market.
- **Late document legalisation.** It gates the whole process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do I need to teach English in Thailand?

A bachelor's degree, a TEFL or equivalent certificate, a teacher licence or waiver from the Teachers Council, a legalised criminal record check and a health certificate.

### What is the teacher licence requirement?

Foreign teachers in formal education must hold a licence from the Teachers Council or a temporary waiver while working toward one. Waivers are limited in number and duration.

### How much does teaching pay in Thailand?

Government and agency placements pay least, private and bilingual schools more, and international schools substantially more — the last requiring a genuine teaching qualification.

### Should I work through an agency?

Agency quality varies enormously. Some are professional; others take a large cut and handle documentation poorly. Research the specific agency and speak to teachers they have placed.

### Is international school teaching an entry route?

No. It requires a genuine teaching qualification such as a PGCE or state licence plus experience, and it is a professional career rather than a way into the country.

### Can I teach on a tourist visa?

No. It is unlawful and enforcement has become more active. Insist on a proper Non-B visa and work permit through your employer.
### How do I choose a teaching employer in Thailand?

Check whether they handle the permit and licence properly, whether the contract is written, the real teaching hours and class sizes, whether pay has been reliable, and whether they are the school or an agency placing you into one.
### What comes after teaching English in Thailand?

Progression toward international schools with a proper qualification, corporate training, teaching in another country, or returning home. Moving into professional work in Thailand itself is difficult given the sponsorship and restricted occupation rules.
### Where in Thailand should I teach?

Bangkok has the most positions and highest costs; Chiang Mai attracts teachers for lifestyle with more competition; provincial and northeastern placements offer lower pay, much lower costs and the warmest reception.
### Can I save money teaching in Thailand?

At private and international schools, yes. At government and agency placements it is difficult, particularly if the long April break is unpaid — which is worth clarifying before signing.
