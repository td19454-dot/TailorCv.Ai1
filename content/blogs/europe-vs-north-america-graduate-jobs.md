## Key Takeaways

- **North America pays substantially more** in technology, [finance](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs) and medicine; the gap narrows considerably in most other fields once benefits and protections are counted.
- **Immigration is the decisive variable.** The US [H-1B](/blog/h1b-visa-job-search-guide) lottery is genuinely a lottery; most European countries have deterministic post-study routes.
- **Europe offers stronger statutory protections** — holiday, pa[rental](/blog/canada-major-city-job-markets) leave, dismissal protection, [healthcare](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist) — that do not appear in a salary comparison but are worth real money.
- **Canada sits between the two**, with North American salaries somewhat below the US and immigration that is far more predictable.
- The right answer depends on **your field, your passport and your risk tolerance**, not on which continent is generally better.

## The pay question, honestly

The salary gap is real and it is not uniform.

**In [software engineer](/blog/resume-matching-software-engineer)ing, it is enormous.** A senior engineer at a large US technology company can earn a multiple of the European equivalent, driven substantially by equity compensation that barely exists in most of Europe.

**In finance**, New York pays above London, and London pays well above continental Europe.

**In medicine**, US physician compensation is far above European levels.

**In most other fields the gap is much smaller.** [Marketing](/blog/resume-matching-marketing), operations, general management, engineering outside software, [public sector](/blog/canada-government-jobs-for-students), academia — the difference narrows considerably and in some cases reverses once you account for what you pay for separately in the US.

**And the comparison has to be net of what Europe includes.** Healthcare, university education, childcare and [pension](/blog/first-90-days-new-job) provision are funded through [taxation](/blog/canada-first-job-taxes-and-benefits) in most of Europe and paid for privately in the US. A European gross salary buys things an American gross salary does not.

**The honest summary:** if you are a software engineer or a doctor, North America pays more, sometimes dramatically. If you are almost anything else, run the comparison carefully before assuming.

## Immigration, which usually decides it

For an international graduate this is the factor that overrides the others.

| | Route after graduating | Predictability |
|---|---|---|
| **United States** | OPT, then H-1B via lottery | Poor — the lottery is genuinely random |
| **Canada** | PGWP, then Express Entry | Good — points-based and transparent |
| **UK** | Graduate Route, then Skilled Worker | Good — deterministic if you meet criteria |
| **Ireland** | Third Level Graduate Scheme | Good |
| **Netherlands** | Orientation year | Good, and open to some foreign graduates |
| **Germany** | Job-seeker permit, EU Blue Card | Good |
| **France** | RECE, then Talent Passport | Good |
| **Australia** | 485, then points test | Moderate — points competition is real |

**The H-1B lottery is the single biggest structural difference.** An excellent candidate with an excellent offer at an excellent company can simply not be selected, repeatedly. No amount of merit changes the odds, and this is not true anywhere else on this list.

**Europe's routes are deterministic.** Meet the criteria and you get the permit. That is a fundamentally different kind of risk to carry through your twenties.

**Canada is the compromise** many people land on: North American work culture and salary bands somewhat below the US, with an immigration system that tells you in advance what you need to do.

## What Europe gives you that does not appear in a salary

Worth pricing rather than dismissing.

**Statutory paid holiday** of four to six weeks, universally, and taken. US norms are two weeks and frequently not fully used.

**Parental leave** measured in months to years across most of Europe, shared between parents, versus a US federal entitlement to unpaid leave only.

**Healthcare that does not depend on your employer.** This is the structural difference people underweight — losing a job in the US can mean losing coverage, which changes what a job means.

**Dismissal protection.** Most of Europe requires cause and notice; most US employment is at-will.

**Pension provision** through mandatory or near-universal schemes in most of Europe.

**The counterargument is real too.** Higher European taxes, slower salary progression, less equity upside, and in some countries a rigid labour market that makes entry harder even as it protects those already inside.

## Work culture, which people underestimate

The daily experience differs more than the numbers suggest.

**Hours and expectations.** North American professional norms run longer, with shorter holidays actually taken and a stronger expectation of availability. European norms vary — Paris finance and London law are demanding; Dutch, Nordic and German workplaces are protective of hours in a way that is legally enforced rather than merely encouraged.

**Job mobility.** Changing employer every two or three years is normal and rewarded in North America. In much of continental Europe it is read as instability, and tenure carries more signal.

**Feedback and directness** vary in both directions. Dutch and German directness surprises Americans; American positivity surprises Germans. Neither is a universal standard.

**Titles and progression.** North American titles inflate faster and progression is quicker in nominal terms. European progression is slower and the underlying responsibility often matches the title more closely.

**At-will employment changes the relationship.** In the US, either side can end it at any time for almost any reason. In most of Europe, dismissal requires cause and process. This shapes how people behave, how much they invest in an employer, and how much an employer invests in them.

**Neither is objectively better.** The question is which set of trade-offs you want to live inside for the next decade.

## Making the decision

A practical way to think about it rather than a general one.

**Start with your passport.** It determines which of these [opt](/blog/opt-cpt-resume-guide)ions are actually available, and there is no point optimising between two things when only one is open to you.

**Then your field.** The salary gap is field-specific and the difference between software and everything else is enormous.

**Then your five-year plan.** If you want to be permanently settled somewhere in five years, deterministic immigration matters more than salary. If you want to earn hard for a defined period and go home, the calculation reverses.

**Then your dependants.** Partner work rights, childcare, schooling and healthcare all differ enough to change the answer entirely for a family versus a single person.

**And then run one honest spreadsheet.** Net pay, real rent, healthcare, childcare, pension contribution, and the value of the holiday you would actually take. Most people who do this find the answer is less obvious than they assumed — in both directions.

## Long-term residency, which nobody compares

The ten-year picture differs more than the five-year one.

**The US green card queue is nationality-dependent** and, for applicants born in some countries, measured in many years. This is the most consequential fact in the whole comparison for a large share of graduates and it is almost never mentioned in salary discussions.

**Canada's [Express Entry](/blog/how-to-get-a-job-in-canada)** is points-based, transparent, and delivers permanent residence in a timeframe you can plan around, followed by citizenship after a qualifying residence period.

**European routes to long-term residence** typically run through several years of continuous legal residence, with EU long-term resident status available in most member states and national citizenship after a longer period. Requirements often include a language test and a civics test.

**The UK's settlement route** runs through a qualifying period on eligible visas, with the [Graduate Route](/blog/how-to-get-a-job-in-uk-foreigner) itself generally not counting toward it — a detail that catches people out.

**Dual citizenship rules vary** and matter if you would have to renounce an existing nationality.

**The practical instruction:** if you intend to settle rather than to work abroad for a defined period, map the whole path before choosing the first step. A higher salary attached to a decade-long queue is a different offer from a lower one attached to permanent residence in three years.

## Getting hired in each, practically

The mechanics of applying differ enough to matter.

**Resume conventions.** North America uses a one-page resume with no photo and no personal details. Europe varies enormously — [one page](/blog/ats-resume-length-guide) in France, two in the Netherlands and Ireland, a full dossier with photo in Switzerland and Germany. Sending a US-format resume to a Swiss employer signals unfamiliarity with the market.

**[Cover letters](/blog/cover-letter-generator-by-country).** Largely skimmed in the US and UK; genuinely read in France, the Netherlands, Germany and Switzerland. Where they are read, a generic one is worse than none.

**Interview style.** North American processes lean on [behavioural questions](/blog/mock-interview-for-sponsorship-roles) and, in technology, algorithmic problems. Dutch, Nordic and German processes weight practical assessment and take-home work more heavily.

**Salary conversations.** Asked early and directly in the Netherlands and Nordics; later and more delicately in France; often prohibited as a history question in parts of the US.

**[References](/blog/resume-references-guide).** A formality in much of the US; genuinely called in Switzerland, New Zealand and Ireland.

**[Automated screening](/blog/ats-resume-checker-how-it-works) is universal.** The one constant across every market in this comparison is that large employers screen documents with software first. Running your base document through an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) once is worth doing wherever you are applying.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are a software engineer with no immigration constraint

North America pays more, substantially, particularly at the large technology companies where equity dominates total compensation. If money is the objective, this is not a close comparison.

### Scenario 2: You need a visa and want predictability

Canada, Ireland, the Netherlands or Germany. All four have deterministic post-study routes, and none of them will tell you after two years of good work that a lottery went against you.

### Scenario 3: You are planning a family in the next five years

Europe, and specifically Northern Europe. Parental leave, childcare subsidy and healthcare that does not depend on your employer are worth a great deal of salary, and they are the things a gross comparison never shows.

> **Tip:** Decide the immigration question before the salary question. A higher American offer that depends on winning a lottery is not comparable to a lower European one that comes with a deterministic route to residency — and treating them as the same number is the most expensive mistake in this whole comparison.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Compare within your specific field
- Weigh immigration predictability heavily
- Include healthcare, pension and childcare in the comparison
- Consider Canada as a middle path
- Check your partner's work rights in each option
- Think about the ten-year residency picture

### Don't

- Compare gross salaries across systems
- Assume the US pays more in every field
- Underestimate the H-1B lottery risk
- Dismiss European protections as soft benefits
- Ignore language requirements in continental Europe
- Assume a European offer means slow career progression everywhere

## Common Mistakes

- **Comparing gross salaries.** The two systems fund completely different things through them.
- **Assuming the pay gap is universal.** It is enormous in software and medicine and modest elsewhere.
- **Underweighting the lottery.** No amount of merit affects H-1B odds.
- **Pricing benefits at zero.** Holiday, parental leave and healthcare are worth real money.
- **Ignoring Canada.** It is the compromise most people are actually looking for.
- **Forgetting the partner.** Work rights for a spouse differ enormously between these systems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does North America really pay more?

Substantially in software engineering, finance and medicine. In most other fields the gap narrows considerably once healthcare, pension and education costs are counted.

### What is the H-1B lottery and why does it matter?

The US skilled work visa is allocated by random selection when applications exceed the cap. An excellent candidate with an excellent offer can simply not be selected, repeatedly.

### Is Canada a good middle option?

For many people, yes — North American work culture, salaries somewhat below the US, and a transparent points-based immigration system that tells you in advance what you need.

### Are European benefits really worth the lower salary?

Depends on your circumstances. Four to six weeks of holiday, months of parental leave and employer-independent healthcare are worth a great deal to some people and little to others.

### Which is better for career progression?

North America tends to move faster and reward risk more; Europe tends to be steadier with better protection. Neither is universally better — it depends on what you want compounding.

### Do I need a second language for Europe?

For Ireland, the Netherlands and much of the technology sector, no. For most of the wider market in France, Germany, Switzerland and the Nordics, yes eventually.
### Is job-hopping viewed the same in Europe and North America?

No. Changing employer every two or three years is normal and rewarded in North America; in much of continental Europe it reads as instability and tenure carries more signal.

### How should I actually make the decision?

Passport first, since it determines what is available. Then your field, since the pay gap is field-specific. Then your five-year plan and your dependants. Then one honest net-of-everything spreadsheet.
### How do the routes to permanent residency compare?

Canada's Express Entry is the most predictable. European routes run through several years of continuous residence with language and civics requirements. The US green card queue is nationality-dependent and can be extremely long.

### Does the UK Graduate Route count toward settlement?

Generally not. Time on it usually does not count toward the qualifying period for settlement, which surprises people who assumed it would.
