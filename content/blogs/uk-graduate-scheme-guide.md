## Key Takeaways

- Applications typically open in early autumn for roles starting the following summer, and many schemes recruit on a rolling basis - so applying in October beats applying in January.
- Most schemes screen at a 2:1 minimum, though a growing number have moved to contextual or classification-blind recruitment.
- The process usually runs: online application, psychometric tests, video interview, assessment centre - each stage eliminating a large share of candidates.
- Aptitude tests are practisable, and practice measurably improves scores.
- Applying to six schemes properly beats applying to thirty generically, because the written answers are scored individually.

Graduate schemes are a distinctly British institution: structured two-year programmes with rotations, formal training, and a defined progression path, run by large employers on a fixed annual calendar.

They are also among the most competitive entry routes in the UK job market, with popular schemes drawing very large applicant numbers for a small intake. The process is long, multi-stage, and heavily standardised - which is good news, because it means it can be prepared for.

This guide covers the calendar, each stage, and where candidates lose.

## The Calendar

This is the single most useful thing to know, and it catches out international students and career changers constantly.

**September to November** - applications open for schemes starting the following summer. This is the main window.

**December to February** - assessment centres run; many schemes close as they fill.

**Spring** - some schemes still open, plus later rounds where intakes are not filled.

**Summer** - the previous year's successful applicants start.

**Critically, many schemes recruit on a rolling basis.** They assess as applications arrive and stop when the intake is full, which means a strong application in January can lose to an average one from October. If you are targeting graduate schemes, September and October are the months that matter.

**Summer internships** run on a similar but earlier cycle, and they are the highest-value route in - a large share of graduate scheme places go to former interns. If you are in your penultimate year, the internship application is more important than the graduate one. See the [internship resume guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/internship-resume-guide).

## Entry Requirements

**Degree classification.** A 2:1 minimum remains common, and some schemes state it as a hard filter. That said, a meaningful number of employers have moved to contextual recruitment - considering your grades relative to your circumstances and school - or have dropped the classification requirement entirely in favour of test performance. Check each scheme rather than assuming.

**UCAS points or A-level grades.** Some schemes still screen on these, particularly in finance and law.

**Degree subject.** Many schemes are open to any discipline. Others - engineering, actuarial, some technology - require a relevant degree.

**Right to work.** Larger schemes often sponsor, but not all do, and some explicitly exclude candidates requiring sponsorship. Check before investing hours in an application. The mechanics are in the [UK Skilled Worker visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-skilled-worker-visa-guide).

## Stage 1: The Online Application

Usually a form rather than a CV upload, with several free-text questions scored individually.

Typical questions:

- "Why this scheme, and why this organisation?"
- "Tell us about a time you demonstrated [named competency]."
- "What has been your greatest achievement?"

**These are scored, not skimmed.** Each answer is assessed against a rubric, which is why volume applications fail - a recycled answer that does not address the specific organisation scores near zero on the motivation question.

**Answer in STAR** for the competency questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result - with most of the space on Action. The structure is covered in [competency-based interview questions](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/competency-based-interview-questions-guide).

**Use the word count.** An answer at 40% of the limit reads as low effort.

**Be specific about the organisation.** Name their actual business, a recent development, or the specific rotation structure. Generic praise is transparent.

## Stage 2: Online Tests

Usually sat immediately or shortly after the form, and this is where the largest single cut happens.

**Numerical reasoning.** Data interpretation from tables and charts under time pressure. Not advanced maths - percentages, ratios, and reading data accurately at speed.

**Verbal reasoning.** True / false / cannot say against a passage. The discipline is answering only from the text, not from what you know.

**Logical or inductive reasoning.** Pattern sequences.

**Situational judgement.** Workplace scenarios where you rank responses. These test alignment with the employer's stated values, so read the organisation's competency framework first - the "right" answer differs between a consultancy and the civil service.

**Gamified assessments.** Increasingly common. Short tasks measuring risk appetite, attention, and processing speed.

**Practice works.** These are learnable formats, and familiarity with the question types and the time pressure measurably improves scores. Free practice tests are widely available, and most test providers publish their own. Doing twenty practice questions before your first real test is the highest-return hour in the whole process.

## Stage 3: Video Interview

Usually asynchronous - recorded answers to questions on a timer, with limited or no retakes.

It feels unnatural and most candidates underperform relative to a live conversation. What helps:

- **Practise recording yourself.** The discomfort fades quickly and it is the only fix.
- **Look at the camera**, not at your own image.
- **Structure answers in STAR** even without an interviewer reacting.
- **Neutral background, good lighting, tested connection.**
- **Have your prepared examples on a card** just off-camera.

More in [remote job interview preparation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/remote-job-interview-preparation-2026).

## Stage 4: The Assessment Centre

A half or full day, in person or virtual, combining several exercises. Each is scored separately, so a weak performance in one does not necessarily end it.

**Group exercise.** A team task with assessors observing. They are scoring how you contribute - bringing others in, building on ideas, managing time. Dominating scores badly; so does silence. Two substantive contributions beat constant talking.

**Presentation.** Often prepared on the day from a brief. Structure and keeping to time matter as much as content.

**Case study or written exercise.** Analysis under time pressure. Answer the question actually asked.

**Competency interview.** As described in [UK interview tips](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-interview-tips).

**Informal sessions** with current graduates. These are usually genuinely informal, but behave as though they are not.

## Where Candidates Lose

**Applying too late.** Rolling recruitment means October beats January.

**Volume over quality.** Thirty generic applications produce worse outcomes than six tailored ones, because the written answers are individually scored.

**No test practice.** The largest single cut, and the most easily prepared for.

**Generic motivation answers.** "I want to work for a prestigious firm in a fast-paced environment" scores near zero.

**Weak competency examples.** Vague, unquantified, or answered in the plural. "We" tells assessors nothing about you.

**Ignoring the competency framework.** Most schemes publish theirs. It is effectively the marking scheme.

**Only applying to the famous names.** Mid-size firms run excellent schemes with a fraction of the applicants.

**Giving up after rejections.** Rejection rates are high for everyone. If you are not placed, the alternatives are real - see [not placed, what next](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/not-placed-in-campus-what-next).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I apply for UK graduate schemes?

September and October for schemes starting the following summer. Many recruit on a rolling basis and close early when full, so applying in the first weeks materially improves your odds.

### Do I need a 2:1?

Many schemes still require it, but a growing number use contextual recruitment or have dropped the requirement in favour of test performance. Check each scheme individually rather than ruling yourself out.

### How competitive are graduate schemes?

Very - popular schemes attract large numbers of applicants for small intakes. This is why targeting fewer schemes properly, and practising the tests, matters more than application volume.

### Can international students apply?

Often yes, but not always - some schemes exclude candidates requiring sponsorship. If you are eligible for the Graduate visa, that removes the barrier for the first two years and is worth stating explicitly on your application.

### Are aptitude tests really practisable?

Yes. They test familiar formats under time pressure, and familiarity with the format is a large part of the score. Practice is the highest-return preparation in the whole process.

### What if I miss the autumn window?

Some schemes open later, and others reopen when intakes are not filled. Direct entry-level roles outside the scheme structure are also worth targeting, and they hire year-round.

Get the timing right first. Build your target list over the summer and apply in September and October, because rolling recruitment quietly penalises everyone who waits.

Then prepare the two stages that eliminate most candidates.
