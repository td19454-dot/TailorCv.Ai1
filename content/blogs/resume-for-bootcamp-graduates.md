## Key Takeaways

- **The bootcamp is not the qualification.** What you built with it is.
- **Lead with projects**, since they are the only evidence that distinguishes you from every other graduate of the same course.
- **Cohort projects look identical** to reviewers who have seen dozens of them, which is the core problem.
- **Prior experience is an asset**, not something to hide, particularly for career changers.
- **The first role is the hard one.** After it, the bootcamp stops mattering almost entirely.

## The screening problem

Why bootcamp resumes get filtered.

**Reviewers see many identical applications.** A cohort of thirty produces thirty resumes with the same course, the same stack and frequently the same projects.

**The tutorial project is recognisable.** Reviewers in this space have seen the standard clone applications hundreds of times and they signal nothing about capability.

**The course name carries little weight** on its own, since quality varies enormously and employers know it.

**Duration works against you.** A few months of study reads as less than a multi-year degree, fairly or not.

**And many applicants apply to the same roles**, since cohorts share job boards and advice, which concentrates the competition.

**None of this means bootcamp graduates do not get hired.** They do, consistently. But the ones who do are almost always the ones who built something the cohort did not.

## What actually differentiates you

The things worth your time.

| Signal | Weight |
|---|---|
| **An original project solving a real problem** | Highest |
| **Work with real users or data** | High |
| **Contributions to existing codebases** | High |
| **Depth in one area rather than breadth** | Moderate to high |
| **Prior domain experience** | High for relevant roles |
| **The bootcamp certificate itself** | Low |

**Build something nobody assigned you.** One original project, finished and deployed, outperforms four course projects by a wide margin.

**Solve a problem you actually have**, since projects rooted in genuine need are more interesting to discuss and demonstrate judgement rather than instruction-following.

**Real users change everything.** Even a handful of them means you have handled feedback, edge cases and the difference between working and usable.

**And prior experience is frequently your strongest card.** A former nurse building healthcare software, or an ex-accountant working on financial tools, has domain knowledge that a computer science graduate does not.

## Choosing what to build

The project that changes your application.

**Pick a problem you personally have**, because you will understand the requirements without guessing and you will stay interested long enough to finish.

**Keep the scope small enough to complete.** A finished modest tool beats an ambitious abandoned platform every time, and reviewers can tell the difference instantly.

**Aim for real users**, even a handful. Friends, a local group, a community you belong to — the moment someone else uses it, you encounter the problems that separate a demo from software.

**Deploy it properly** so it can be visited, since a project that requires a reviewer to clone and run it usually goes unexamined.

**Handle the unglamorous parts** — errors, empty states, bad input, basic security. These are what distinguish production thinking from tutorial thinking.

**Write down why you made your choices**, because the reasoning is what a technical interviewer will probe and what most [portfolios](/blog/how-to-build-professional-portfolio) never show.

**And keep working on it after launch**, since sustained maintenance over months is a signal almost no bootcamp portfolio carries.

## The first role and what follows

Setting expectations honestly.

**The first job is disproportionately hard** and the effort is not evenly distributed across your career. Expect it to take longer than you were told.

**Volume alone will not solve it.** If a hundred applications produced nothing, the hundred and first will not either, and the answer is differentiation.

**Smaller companies are frequently more accessible**, since they assess more pragmatically and have less rigid screening.

**Contract, internship and junior support roles** are legitimate entry points, and many careers start sideways rather than directly.

**Your prior career gives you an edge in its own industry**, which is consistently the most overlooked route into a first technical role.

**And after twelve to eighteen months of professional work**, the question of how you learned disappears almost entirely from hiring conversations.

**Which means the bootcamp is a bridge rather than a destination.** Its job is to get you to the first role, and after that your work speaks for itself.

## Mini checklist

- [ ] At least one original project, finished and deployed
- [ ] Tutorial and cohort projects de-emphasised or removed
- [ ] Live links to working examples
- [ ] Code publicly available and readable
- [ ] Prior experience presented as relevant, not hidden
- [ ] Depth shown in one area
- [ ] Contributions to existing projects where possible
- [ ] Bootcamp listed under education, briefly

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: All your projects are from the course

Build one more before applying widely. Two weeks on an original project changes your application more than two months of sending the cohort version.

### Scenario 2: You are changing careers into technology

Lead with the combination. Domain knowledge plus new technical skills is a genuinely differentiated profile, and roles in your former industry are the most realistic entry point.

### Scenario 3: You have applied everywhere with no response

The problem is almost certainly differentiation rather than volume. Stop applying, build one substantial thing, and restart with a materially different application.

> **Tip:** Reviewers can identify a course project within seconds. One finished original project — even a modest one solving a real problem for real people — moves you out of the cohort pile entirely, which is the only screening problem you actually have.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Build at least one original project
- Deploy it so it can be used
- Lead with projects over the course
- Use prior experience as an advantage
- Show depth in one area
- Keep code public and readable

### Don't

- Lead with the bootcamp name
- Rely on cohort projects alone
- Hide a previous career
- List every technology superficially
- Apply widely before differentiating
- Treat the certificate as the qualification

## Common Mistakes

- **Cohort projects only.** Indistinguishable from everyone else.
- **Leading with the course.** It is the weakest signal you have.
- **Hiding prior experience.** Frequently your strongest asset.
- **Breadth over depth.** Twelve shallow technologies persuade nobody.
- **Volume before differentiation.** It repeats the same failure.
- **Undeployed projects.** Code nobody can run is hard to assess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do employers respect bootcamps?

Some do and some do not, but it matters less than people think. What is assessed is what you can build, and the course name is a weak signal in either direction.

### What should a bootcamp graduate lead with?

Projects, specifically original ones. Cohort projects are recognisable to reviewers and signal nothing, while one finished original piece of work changes the application entirely.

### Should I hide my previous career?

No. For career changers it is frequently the strongest differentiator, particularly when applying to companies operating in your former industry.

### How many projects do I need?

One substantial original project matters more than several small ones. Depth, real users and finished deployment persuade far more than quantity.

### Should I list the bootcamp at all?

Yes, briefly, under education. It explains your timeline. It should not be the headline of your application.

### How long until the bootcamp stops mattering?

After your first role. Once you have professional experience, that becomes the thing employers assess and the course becomes a line in your education section.
### What project should a bootcamp graduate build?

One solving a problem you personally have, scoped small enough to finish, deployed so it can be visited, with real users and the unglamorous parts handled — errors, empty states and bad input.

### How long until the bootcamp stops mattering?

Twelve to eighteen months of professional work. After that, employers assess your experience and how you originally learned rarely comes up at all.
