## Key Takeaways

- Belgium has **three official languages and three regions**, and employment rules including work permits are **regionally administered** rather than national.
- **Brussels is the EU's administrative centre** and hosts an enormous international sector operating in English and French.
- **Flanders (Dutch-speaking) has the stronger private-sector economy**; Wallonia (French-speaking) is smaller and has higher unemployment.
- **Bilingualism is a genuine career asset** — French and Dutch together open a market that monolingual candidates cannot reach.
- The **single permit** combines work and residence authorisation and is applied for by the employer to the relevant region.

## Three regions, three job markets

Understanding Belgium's structure is not optional trivia — it determines where you apply and under which rules.

**Flanders** in the north, Dutch-speaking, with the stronger private-sector economy. Antwerp's port and chemical cluster is one of Europe's largest, and there is substantial technology, logistics, pharmaceuticals and manufacturing employment.

**Wallonia** in the south, French-speaking, historically industrial and economically weaker, with higher unemployment. There is real activity in aerospace, biotech around Liège and Charleroi, and Louvain-la-Neuve's research cluster.

**Brussels-Capital**, officially bilingual French and Dutch, and in practice substantially French-speaking with an enormous English-speaking international population.

**And a small German-speaking community** in the east.

**Why this matters practically:** [work permits](/blog/graduate-work-visa-comparison-usa-uk-canada-singapore) are administered by the regions, not the federal government. The rules, the shortage occupation lists and the processing differ between Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels. An [application](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day) for a job in Antwerp goes through a different system than one for a job in Namur.

## Brussels and the international sector

This is the single most distinctive thing about the Belgian labour market.

**The EU institutions.** The European Commission, Council, Parliament (partly), the External Action Service and dozens of agencies employ a very large number of people in Brussels. EPSO competitions are the main recruitment route for permanent staff, and contract agent and traineeship routes are more accessible starting points.

**NATO headquarters** is in Brussels, with its own civilian recruitment.

**The lobbying and representation ecosystem.** Trade associations, corporate EU affairs offices, law firms, [consultancies](/blog/case-interview-preparation-guide-2026) and NGOs — thousands of organisations exist to engage with the institutions, and they hire continuously.

**International schools, media and services** supporting that population.

**The consequence:** Brussels has an English-working professional labour market of a size that its population would not otherwise justify. For an English-speaking [graduate](/blog/first-resume-for-gen-z-2026) with an interest in policy, regulation, public affairs or international work, it is one of the best cities in Europe.

**A separate guide covers the EU institutions properly** — see [EU institution careers](/blog/belgium-eu-institutions-careers).

## Language, and the bilingual premium

Belgium's language situation is genuinely consequential for a [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide) here.

**Flanders operates in Dutch.** [Employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) in Antwerp, Ghent, Leuven and Bruges expect it for most roles, though international companies and technology firms often [work in English](/blog/english-speaking-jobs-across-europe).

**Wallonia operates in French.**

**Brussels is officially bilingual**, and public-sector roles frequently require both French and Dutch to a demonstrated standard. This is a hard requirement, not a preference.

**The international sector operates in English**, with French useful socially and Dutch less so in Brussels.

**The bilingual premium is real.** Candidates with genuine working French and Dutch are relatively scarce and can access the whole country plus the bilingual public sector. If you already have one and can build the other, it is one of the highest-return language investments available in Europe.

**Selor** administers language [certification](/blog/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume) for public-sector roles, and those [certificates](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist) are the currency in that market.

## The single permit and how it works

The mechanics for third-country nationals.

**EU, EEA and Swiss citizens** need no permit. Free movement applies, and you register with your commune after arriving.

**Everyone else needs a single permit** — a combined work and residence authorisation. The employer applies to the **regional** authority where the work will be performed, and the federal immigration office handles the residence component.

**Categories exist** for highly skilled workers, [EU Blue Card](/blog/germany-eu-blue-card-graduates) holders, [shortage occupations](/blog/choosing-country-to-study-abroad-for-jobs), researchers and others. Each region maintains its own shortage occupation list, and being on the relevant one materially simplifies the process.

**[Salary](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) [thresholds](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide) apply** to the highly skilled and Blue Card routes, set regionally and revised annually.

**Processing timelines** vary by region and category. Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels publish their own guidance and they do not match.

**Graduates of Belgian institutions** have a route to remain and search for work or start a business after completing their studies, with conditions set at federal level. Check the current duration, because it has been adjusted.

**EU institutions are outside all of this.** Officials and agents of the European institutions have their own status, which is why that route is covered separately.

## Setting up when you arrive

Belgian administration is famously bureaucratic and the sequence matters.

**Register at your commune** within the required period after arriving. This triggers a check that you actually live at the stated address — a police visit is normal and not a cause for alarm.

**Receive your residence card and national register number**, which unlocks essentially everything else.

**[Health insurance](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist) is compulsory** and provided through a mutuelle or ziekenfonds — a health insurance fund you choose and join. This is not automatic; you must enrol.

**Bank account**, and note that Belgian banks generally want the commune registration first.

**Register with the regional employment service** — VDAB in Flanders, Actiris in Brussels, Le Forem in Wallonia — which run listings, training and support for jobseekers.

**[Housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide)** is markedly cheaper than Amsterdam, Paris or Munich, which is one of Belgium's genuine and under-appreciated advantages.

## Money, contracts and what leaves your account

Belgian compensation is structured distinctively and the gross figure is misleading on its own.

**Belgium has among the highest labour taxation in Europe.** The gap between what an employer pays and what reaches your account is large, and a gross salary comparison against a UK or Irish offer will mislead you badly in both directions.

**Extra-legal benefits are how Belgium compensates.** Meal vouchers, eco-vouchers, a company car or mobility budget, group insurance and hospitalisation cover are widespread and are worth real money after tax. Two offers with the same gross can differ substantially once these are counted.

**The company car culture is genuine.** Historically a tax-efficient benefit, it remains common in professional roles, with mobility budget alternatives increasingly offered.

**Thirteenth month and holiday pay** are standard in most sectors.

**Indexation.** Belgian wages are automatically indexed to inflation under sectoral agreements — an unusual arrangement that means pay rises without negotiation when prices rise.

**Joint committees (paritair comité / commission paritaire)** govern pay and conditions by sector, similar to collective agreements elsewhere. Find out which one covers your role.

**Ask for a net simulation.** Belgian employers and payroll providers routinely produce these, and asking for one is normal — it is the only way to compare two offers meaningfully.

## Where roles are advertised

Belgium's channels are regional, like everything else.

**VDAB** in Flanders, **Actiris** in Brussels and **Le Forem** in Wallonia are the public employment services, each with its own listings and support programmes for jobseekers including internationals.

**StepStone Belgium and Jobat** carry broad private-sector volume.

**Références** (French) and **Vacature** (Dutch) are the established newspaper-linked job platforms in their respective regions.

**LinkedIn is strong in Brussels** because of the international population and thinner in Wallonia.

**EURACTIV and Politico's job boards** carry the EU affairs and policy roles that the institutions themselves do not advertise.

**Company career pages** for the large employers — the Antwerp chemical cluster, the banks, the pharmaceutical companies.

**Interim agencies are unusually significant** in Belgium. Temporary work through an agency is a mainstream route into permanent employment rather than a lesser option, and registering with two or three is standard practice.

## Working culture

Belgium sits between its neighbours in most respects.

**Less formal than France, more formal than the Netherlands.** Vous or u until invited otherwise, but the register warms faster than in Germany or Austria.

**Hierarchies exist but are not rigid.** Disagreement is acceptable if expressed with some diplomacy — the Dutch bluntness that works in Amsterdam can land badly here.

**Compromise is a genuine cultural value.** Belgium governs itself through elaborate negotiated settlements between communities, and that instinct shows up in how workplaces resolve disagreement.

**Hours are protected** and the statutory leave entitlement is generous, with long lunch breaks normal in many workplaces.

**Meetings can be multilingual.** In Brussels it is entirely normal for a meeting to run in English with side conversations in French and Dutch, and nobody finds this remarkable.

**Sector agreements shape a great deal** — pay, hours, leave and conditions all sit inside joint committee arrangements rather than individual contracts.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You speak English only

Brussels, and the international sector specifically — institutions, associations, NGOs, consultancies and the corporate EU affairs world. It is a large market and it genuinely runs in English.

### Scenario 2: You speak Dutch or are willing to learn it

Flanders, where the private-sector economy is strongest and competition from monolingual internationals is lower. Antwerp, Ghent and Leuven all have real technology, logistics and life sciences employment.

### Scenario 3: You have both French and Dutch

You are in an unusually strong position. The bilingual public sector, the whole private market, and the Brussels institutions are all open to you, and genuinely bilingual candidates are scarcer than you would expect.

> **Tip:** Check which region your target job sits in before anything else. Work permits in Belgium are administered regionally, with different rules and different shortage lists in Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels — advice about "Belgium" as a single system is misleading.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Identify the region before checking any rules
- Consider Flanders if you have or will learn Dutch
- Use the Brussels international sector if English-only
- Treat bilingualism as a serious career investment
- Check the regional shortage occupation list
- Start diploma equivalence early for regulated professions

### Don't

- Treat Belgium as a single national labour market
- Assume Brussels rules apply in Antwerp
- Expect English to work across Flanders and Wallonia
- Overlook the EU institutions' accessible entry routes
- Ignore Selor certification for public-sector ambitions
- Assume French alone covers Brussels public sector

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating Belgium as one system.** Permits, rules and lists are regional.
- **English-only outside Brussels.** Flanders works in Dutch and Wallonia in French.
- **Ignoring Flanders.** It has the stronger private-sector economy and less international competition.
- **Missing the institution entry routes.** Contract agent and traineeship posts are far more accessible than permanent competitions.
- **Underrating bilingualism.** Genuine French plus Dutch is scarce and valuable.
- **Late diploma equivalence.** Required for regulated professions and slow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to speak Dutch and French in Belgium?

It depends entirely on the region and sector. Flanders works in Dutch, Wallonia in French, Brussels officially in both, and the international sector in English. Genuine bilingualism is a scarce and valuable asset.

### Why do the rules differ across Belgium?

Employment and work permit administration is devolved to the regions. Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels each run their own system with their own shortage occupation lists.

### Can I work in Brussels with English only?

Yes, in the international sector — EU institutions, NATO, trade associations, NGOs, law firms and consultancies. It is a genuinely large English-working market.

### Which region has the strongest economy?

Flanders has the stronger private-sector economy, with Antwerp's port and chemical cluster, plus technology, logistics and life sciences employment.

### What is the single permit?

Belgium's combined work and residence authorisation, applied for by the employer to the relevant regional authority rather than to a national one.

### Is Selor certification worth getting?

If you want public-sector work in Belgium, yes. Selor language certificates are the recognised currency for demonstrating French and Dutch ability in that market.
### What is the Belgian single permit?

A combined work and residence authorisation for third-country nationals. The employer applies to the regional authority where the work is performed, with the federal immigration office handling residence.

### What do I do first on arriving in Belgium?

Register at your commune, expect a police visit to confirm your address, receive your residence card and national register number, then enrol in a health insurance fund — enrolment is compulsory and not automatic.
### Why is Belgian net pay so much lower than gross?

Belgium has among Europe's highest labour taxation. Ask for a net simulation, which employers routinely produce, because gross comparisons across borders are genuinely misleading here.

### What are extra-legal benefits?

Meal vouchers, eco-vouchers, a company car or mobility budget, group insurance and hospitalisation cover. They are widespread in Belgium, worth real money after tax, and can separate two offers with identical gross salaries.
### Where should I look for jobs in Belgium?

VDAB, Actiris or Le Forem depending on region, plus StepStone Belgium, Jobat, Références and Vacature. Interim agencies are unusually significant and are a mainstream route to permanent work.

### What is Belgian workplace culture like?

Between French formality and Dutch directness — vous or u initially, hierarchy that exists without being rigid, a genuine cultural preference for negotiated compromise, and frequently multilingual meetings.
