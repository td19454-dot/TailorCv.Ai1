## Key Takeaways

- One status of residence covers most foreign professionals in Japan, informally shortened to *gijinkoku*.
- The work must relate to your degree or documented experience — this matching rule causes most refusals.
- Ten years of relevant professional experience can substitute for a degree in some categories, three for translation and language work.
- Your employer applies for the Certificate of Eligibility before you apply for anything.
- The status covers a field of work rather than one employer, so changing jobs within the same field is straightforward.

Most foreign professionals working in Japan hold a single status of residence with an unwieldy name: **Engineer / Specialist in Humanities / International Services**, usually shortened in conversation to *gijinkoku* (技人国). Software engineers, marketers, designers, translators, consultants and analysts all sit under it.

The rule that catches people out is the matching requirement — your job must relate to what you studied or what you can document having done. Applications fail on this more than on anything else.

This guide covers what qualifies, what does not, and how to present it.

## What the status covers

The name is three categories in one:

**Engineer.** Work requiring knowledge of physical sciences, engineering or natural sciences. Software development, IT infrastructure, mechanical and electrical engineering, data work.

**Specialist in Humanities.** Work requiring knowledge of law, economics, sociology or other humanities. Marketing, accounting, planning, sales strategy, HR.

**International Services.** Work requiring thought or sensitivity based on foreign culture — translation, interpretation, language instruction outside formal schools, international business development, and some design work.

**What it does not cover:** manual labour, most service and hospitality work, and simple repetitive tasks. That distinction is enforced, and it is why a graduate cannot use this status to work in a convenience store or restaurant floor role.

## The matching rule

This is where applications fail.

**Your work must relate to your degree subject, or to documented professional experience.**

**Examples that work cleanly:**

- Computer science degree → software engineer
- Economics degree → marketing analyst
- Any degree → translation work using your native language, under International Services

**Examples that raise questions:**

- History degree → software engineer, with no professional programming background
- Engineering degree → general sales role with no technical element

**How to resolve a mismatch:**

**Document experience instead of education.** Ten years of relevant professional experience can substitute for a degree in the Engineer and Specialist in Humanities categories. For International Services work such as translation, three years is the common threshold.

**Emphasise the relevant element of the role.** A job description that foregrounds the technical or specialist content, rather than incidental duties, presents better.

**Provide certificates of employment** from previous employers stating dates, position and actual duties. These are the evidence that experience substitutes for a mismatched degree — and they take time to obtain, so request them early.

## The process

**Your employer applies first.** They submit for a **Certificate of Eligibility (CoE)** at their regional immigration bureau, providing both company documents and your personal documents. Approval typically takes one to three months.

**Then you apply** for the visa at a Japanese embassy or consulate with the CoE. This step is usually quick, often under a week, because the substantive assessment already happened.

**On arrival**, you receive a residence card at the airport, then register your address at your municipal office within 14 days and enrol in health insurance and pension.

## Changing jobs on this status

A genuine advantage over employer-tied visas elsewhere.

**The status covers a category of activity, not one employer.** Moving from one software engineering role to another generally requires no new status — though you must notify immigration within 14 days of the change.

**Where care is needed:** moving to work outside the category. A software engineer becoming a sales manager, or moving into teaching, may need a change of status. Confirm before resigning.

**A certificate of authorised employment** can be requested from immigration confirming your intended new role falls within your current status. Worth obtaining before accepting an offer if there is any doubt — it converts uncertainty into a document.

## Documents and consistency

Your employer handles company-side paperwork. You typically provide passport, photographs, degree certificate, certificates of employment, and your resume.

**Consistency matters more than people expect.** Immigration reads your resume, your certificates of employment and your contract together. Dates that do not match across documents generate queries and delay approval.

**Which means:** write your shokumu keirekisho with the same dates and duties your reference letters will state — see [the shokumu keirekisho guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/shokumu-keirekisho-guide) and [the rirekisho guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japanese-resume-rirekisho-guide).

## Common Mistakes

**Applying for the visa before the Certificate of Eligibility.** The CoE comes first and your employer applies for it in Japan; the visa step afterwards is largely administrative.

**Ignoring the degree-matching rule.** Work must relate to your degree subject or documented experience, and mismatches are the leading cause of refusal.

**Not documenting experience when the degree does not match.** Ten years of relevant professional experience can substitute in the main categories, three for translation work — but only with certificates of employment to prove it.

**Requesting employment certificates late.** Former employers abroad are slow, and these are what make an experience-based application work.

**Inconsistent dates across documents.** Immigration reads resume, certificates and contract together, and discrepancies trigger queries.

**Assuming the status covers any job.** Manual, service and simple repetitive work fall outside it, and this is enforced.

**Changing to work outside the category without checking.** Moving between software roles is fine; moving into sales or teaching may require a status change.

**Missing the 14-day notification windows.** Address registration on arrival and notification of a job change both have short deadlines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the gijinkoku visa?

Shorthand for the Engineer / Specialist in Humanities / International Services status of residence, which covers most white-collar foreign professionals in Japan.

### Do I need a degree?

Usually, in a field related to the work. Alternatively, ten years of documented relevant professional experience can substitute in the main categories, or three years for translation and language work.

### What if my degree does not match my job?

Document relevant professional experience with certificates of employment from former employers, and ensure the job description foregrounds the specialist content of the role.

### Can I change jobs on this status?

Yes, within the same category of work, with notification to immigration within 14 days. Moving outside the category may require a change of status.

### How long does the process take?

The Certificate of Eligibility typically takes one to three months; the visa application afterwards is usually quick because the assessment has already been done.

### Can I work part-time in a restaurant on this status?

No. Manual and simple service work falls outside the status, and this is enforced.

### How do I check a new job qualifies?

Request a certificate of authorised employment from immigration confirming the intended role falls within your current status, before accepting the offer.

## Make This Practical

Check the matching rule against your own situation before applying anywhere. If your degree subject and your intended work do not line up, the application will turn on documented professional experience — and that means requesting certificates of employment from former employers now, because they are slow and they are the evidence the whole case rests on.

Then keep every document consistent. Immigration reads your resume, your employment certificates and your contract together, so write your shokumu keirekisho with exactly the dates and duties your former employers will confirm.

Finally, use the flexibility the status gives you. It covers a field of work rather than one employer, so changing jobs within your category is straightforward — just notify immigration within 14 days, and if a new role sits near the boundary of your category, request a certificate of authorised employment before accepting rather than after.
