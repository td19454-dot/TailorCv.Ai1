## Key Takeaways

- "Missing" means the scan could not find evidence of the skill in your resume — not that you lack it.
- First question, always: do you actually have this skill? Be honest, then act on the honest answer.
- If you have it, the fix is naming it inside evidence, not adding it as a bare list item.
- If you genuinely lack it, three honest options exist — none of them is inventing the skill.
- Hard skills are more heavily weighted than soft skills in most matching, so this list deserves real attention.

A scan flags "missing hard skills" and hands you a list — Python, stakeholder reporting, Salesforce, whatever the posting named. Before doing anything with that list, answer one question for each item: do you actually have this?

## Start Here: The Honest Split

Take the missing list and sort every item into one of two piles.

**Pile 1 — I have done this.** You have used the tool, performed the task, held the responsibility — your resume just does not say so clearly.

**Pile 2 — I have not done this.** Genuinely absent from your experience.

Everything below depends on which pile an item is in. Do not skip this step or rush it — the fix is completely different for each pile, and conflating them is how resumes end up either under-selling real experience or over-claiming absent experience.

## Pile 1: You Have It, You Just Didn't Say So

This is usually the larger pile, and the good news is that it is fixable in minutes, honestly.

**The mistake that causes this:** people describe their work in their own words rather than the posting's words, and a system matching on specific terms cannot connect "built the process that pulled data from Salesforce nightly" to a posting asking for "ETL pipeline experience" unless the language overlaps somewhere.

**The fix:** find the bullet that already describes the work, and add the missing term into it directly.

> Before: Built the nightly process that pulled data from Salesforce into our warehouse.
> After: Built and maintained the ETL pipeline moving Salesforce data into our warehouse nightly.

Nothing was invented. The claim is identical. It is now matchable, both to a parser and to a human scanning for that exact phrase.

**Do this for every item in Pile 1.** It is the highest-return activity available when a scan comes back with gaps, and it typically accounts for most of the achievable improvement. See [which missing skills actually matter](/blog/which-missing-skills-actually-matter) for prioritising when the list is long.

## Pile 2: You Genuinely Lack It

This is the harder pile, and the wrong move is adding the word anyway. It works at the filter and fails at the interview — a worse outcome than the rejection you were trying to avoid. See [improving your score without lying](/blog/improve-score-without-lying).

Three honest responses, depending on the specifics:

**Option A — State genuine adjacent experience.** If the posting wants Snowflake and you have deep BigQuery experience, say exactly that:

> Data warehousing experience with BigQuery; comfortable learning adjacent platforms.

This is honest, it shows real relevant capability, and a reasonable hiring manager can evaluate the transfer themselves. What you should not write is "Snowflake" — that is a specific, checkable claim you cannot back up.

**Option B — Acknowledge the role may not be the right fit yet.** If the gap is substantial — years of experience, a required licence, a fundamentally different technical stack — the honest conclusion is that this specific posting is a stretch. That is useful information, not a failure. See [required vs preferred qualifications](/blog/matching-resume-required-vs-preferred-qualifications) to check whether the gap is actually a hard requirement or a wish-list item you can reasonably ignore.

**Option C — Close the gap for real, if time allows.** If you keep seeing the same missing skill across postings and you have weeks rather than hours, that is a genuine signal about what to learn next. A short course or a side project gives you something true to write, rather than something invented.

## A Worked Example

Scan result on a marketing analyst posting: missing **SQL**, **A/B testing**, **Looker**, **budget management**, **cross-functional collaboration**.

Honest audit:

- **SQL** — you write queries weekly but your resume just says "data analysis." **Pile 1.** Fix: name SQL in the bullet describing that work.
- **A/B testing** — you have run three experiments this year. **Pile 1.** Fix: name it in the relevant bullet with a result if you have one.
- **Looker** — you have never used it, though you have used Tableau extensively. **Pile 2, Option A.** State Tableau explicitly and let the transfer speak for itself.
- **Budget management** — you have never owned a budget. **Pile 2, Option B.** A real gap; if it is stated as required rather than preferred, this posting may not be the best use of your time tonight.
- **Cross-functional collaboration** — a soft skill dressed as a hard one. Low priority regardless of which pile — see [soft skills nobody can verify](/blog/soft-skills-nobody-can-verify).

Out of five missing items, two are quick honest fixes, one is a reasonable adjacent-experience statement, one is a real gap worth noting, and one barely matters. That is a five-minute plan instead of a vague sense of being underqualified.

## Why Hard Skills Get Weighted Heavily

Hard skills are specific, checkable and directly tied to whether you can do the work — a named tool, a certification, a technical method. That specificity is exactly why matching systems and human screeners both lean on them more than soft skills, which everyone claims and nobody can verify from a resume alone.

This means the effort you spend on Pile 1 for hard skills produces more return than almost anything else you could do to a resume in the same amount of time.

## What Not to Do

**Do not add a tool from a single tutorial and call it a skill.** There is a real difference between having used something and having watched a video about it — an interview will find that difference quickly.

**Do not pad the skills list with things unrelated to the posting**, hoping something sticks. It dilutes the items that actually matter — see [keyword stuffing](/blog/resume-too-many-keywords-stuffing).

**Do not ignore Pile 1 because Pile 2 feels discouraging.** The fixable items are usually the majority of the list, and fixing them is worth doing regardless of what else is missing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What if I'm not sure which pile something belongs in?**
Ask whether you could discuss it for two minutes under questioning. If yes, Pile 1. If you would be improvising, it is Pile 2.

**Should I list a skill I used briefly years ago?**
Yes, honestly labelled with context — "prior experience with X" reads differently from implying current daily use.

**Is it ever okay to list a skill I'm currently learning?**
State it accurately: "currently studying X" or "X (in progress)." Do not present it as an established skill.

**How many Pile 2 items are too many to apply anyway?**
No fixed number — check whether they are required or preferred. Several missing preferred items is normal; one missing required item is a real signal.

**Does fixing Pile 1 guarantee an interview?**
No, but it removes an avoidable failure. Everything after that — competition, level fit, timing — is a separate question.
