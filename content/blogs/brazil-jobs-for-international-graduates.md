## Key Takeaways

- Brazil is **Latin America's largest economy** with a substantial domestic market and a genuinely significant technology sector.
- **Portuguese is essential** for almost all professional work — this is stricter than in many markets and English alone is rarely sufficient.
- **São Paulo dominates** professional employment, with Rio, Belo Horizonte, Porto Alegre and Florianópolis holding real sectors.
- **Fintech is a genuine Brazilian strength**, with Nubank among the world's largest digital banks.
- **CLT employment carries substantial statutory protections**, and understanding the contract type is essential.

## The economy

Scale and structure.

**Agribusiness** is enormous and technically sophisticated — Brazil is a leading global producer across several commodities, with the technology and logistics sectors that implies.

**Mining and resources**, including iron ore and increasingly critical minerals.

**Oil and gas**, with substantial offshore production and the associated services sector.

**Manufacturing**, particularly automotive and aerospace — Embraer is a globally significant aircraft manufacturer.

**Financial services**, with a large and sophisticated [banking](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs) sector.

**Technology**, which is the growth story and covered in more detail below.

**And a very large domestic consumer market**, which is what makes Brazilian technology companies viable at scale without needing to internationalise immediately.

## Technology and fintech

Brazil's most internationally visible sector.

**Nubank** became one of the world's largest digital banks, serving an enormous customer base across Brazil and beyond.

**Fintech generally is a Brazilian strength**, driven by historically high banking fees, a large underbanked population and the success of Pix, the instant payment system, which transformed the payments landscape.

**iFood, Rappi's Brazilian operations, Mercado Livre's Brazilian presence** and the wider e-commerce and delivery sector.

**Enterprise software** including TOTVS and a substantial B2B sector.

**Agritech**, applying technology to Brazil's largest industry.

**And international companies** with Brazilian engineering operations, drawn by the talent pool and the market.

**For an engineer**, Brazilian technology offers genuine scale problems and a strong local ecosystem — and the local talent pool is large and good, which sets the bar for foreign hiring.

## Language and the practical reality

The constraint that shapes everything.

**Portuguese is required** for almost all professional employment. This is not a soft preference — Brazilian professional life operates in Portuguese and English-only roles are genuinely rare outside a narrow band of international companies.

**English proficiency in Brazil is lower** than in many comparable markets, which means the working environment does not default to it.

**Spanish is not a substitute**, and assuming it is causes real problems. The languages are related and mutually partly intelligible in writing, and Brazilian professional life happens in Portuguese.

**Portuguese is learnable**, particularly for Spanish or other Romance language speakers, and it opens a market of considerable size.

**Which means:** Brazil is a poor destination for someone unwilling to learn the language, and a genuinely rewarding one for someone who will.

## Cities beyond Sao Paulo

The map is wider than most people assume.

**Sao Paulo** is the commercial and financial capital and holds the great majority of professional employment, technology included. It is enormous, expensive by Brazilian standards, and where the opportunity concentrates.

**Rio de Janeiro** for oil and gas, energy, media and a growing technology presence, with a considerably different quality of life and a reputation for higher [living costs](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) in the desirable areas.

**Belo Horizonte** for mining, engineering and a genuine technology scene, at meaningfully lower cost.

**Porto Alegre** in the south, with a real technology sector, strong [universities](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume) and a European-influenced culture.

**Florianopolis** has become a genuine technology hub, frequently described as one of the better places in Brazil to combine software work with quality of life.

**Campinas** for research, technology and a strong university presence within reach of Sao Paulo.

**Curitiba** for manufacturing, automotive and planning-led urban quality.

**Brasilia** for government, public sector and the associated [consulting](/blog/case-interview-preparation-guide-2026).

**And Recife**, whose Porto Digital has produced a real technology cluster in the northeast.

## Employment structure

The distinction that catches foreigners.

**CLT** is the formal employment regime under the Consolidation of Labour Laws. It carries statutory protections including paid holiday with a holiday [bonus](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email), thirteenth salary, FGTS severance fund contributions, notice requirements and unemployment protections.

**PJ** — pessoa juridica — means being engaged as a company rather than an employee. Headline pay is higher and the statutory protections do not apply, with tax and social contributions falling to you.

**The gap is substantial**, and a PJ figure that looks better than a CLT one frequently is not once the [benefits](/blog/questions-to-ask-in-an-interview) are priced in.

**Both are common** in the technology sector particularly, and the choice is a genuine negotiation point rather than a formality.

**Cooperativas and intermediary arrangements** also exist and warrant scrutiny.

**And labour law is enforced.** Brazil has an active labour court system and disputes are common, which cuts both ways but does mean CLT protections are real rather than nominal.

## Documents and administration

The bureaucracy is genuine and worth preparing for.

**CPF** — the taxpayer registration number — underpins almost everything from bank accounts to phone contracts. Get it early.

**RNM** — the national migration registry card — for foreign residents.

**Carteira de Trabalho**, the work record, now largely digital.

**Diploma revalidation** through a Brazilian university for regulated professions, which is a genuine process rather than a formality and takes time.

**Sworn translation** by a registered public translator for official [documents](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist), which is a specific legal category rather than any translation service.

**[Apostille](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide)** for documents from Convention countries.

**And patience.** Brazilian administration has a reputation and it is partly earned, though digitalisation has genuinely improved several processes in recent years.

## Finding work

The practical channels.

**[LinkedIn](/blog/linkedin-profile-optimization-guide) is dominant** in Brazil and genuinely well used across the professional market.

**Gupy** is a widely used Brazilian recruitment platform, particularly for larger [employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes).

**Vagas.com** carries substantial domestic volume.

**Catho** and **InfoJobs Brasil** for broader coverage.

**Programathor** and **Geekhunter** for technology roles specifically.

**Company [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide) pages** for the larger employers and multinationals.

**[Referrals](/blog/networking-messages-for-referrals) matter considerably**, and the Brazilian professional network is relationship-driven in a way that rewards genuine contact over volume [applications](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day).

**Your application should be in Portuguese** unless the posting is explicitly in English, and a Portuguese [CV](/blog/resume-optimization-guide) that reads naturally does far more than a translated one that does not.

**Run it through an [ATS check](/ats-analysis)** before submitting, since Gupy and the larger employers screen at volume.

**And a [portfolio site](/portfolio)** helps in technology and creative fields, where reviewers will look.

## Salary and cost of living

An honest financial picture.

**Salaries are low by Western standards** and the currency has been volatile, which affects any comparison made in dollars or euros.

**Technology pays well relative to the Brazilian market**, and the gap between technology and other professions is wide.

**Sao Paulo is expensive by Brazilian standards** and cheap by New York or London standards, with housing in the desirable central neighbourhoods being the main cost.

**The thirteenth salary and holiday bonus** are genuine additions under CLT and should be included in any comparison.

**Taxes are meaningful** and the CLT versus PJ distinction changes the calculation substantially.

**Private health insurance** is standard as an employment benefit and effectively necessary, since the public system is universal and heavily strained.

**Security costs** are real in the larger cities, whether in housing choice, transport habits or insurance.

**And the exchange rate question** matters enormously for anyone with obligations in another currency, and has caught out plenty of people who calculated their move at one rate and lived it at another.

## Working culture

Calibrating expectations.

**Relationships come first.** Brazilian business runs on personal connection to a degree that surprises people from more transactional cultures, and time invested in relationships is not wasted time.

**Communication is warm and indirect**, with a preference for softening bad news and avoiding blunt refusal.

**"Jeitinho"** — the informal workaround — is a real and much-discussed cultural feature, describing the capacity to find a way around obstacles.

**Hierarchy exists** and is generally less rigid than in Asia, with a friendly manner across levels.

**Punctuality is flexible** socially and more observed professionally, particularly in Sao Paulo and in multinational environments.

**Meetings run long** and the social preamble is part of the meeting rather than a delay to it.

**Physical warmth is normal** — greetings are close, and the personal distance is smaller than northern Europeans or East Asians expect.

**And the working day** frequently starts and finishes later than in northern Europe, with a proper lunch break being a genuine institution.

## Security and quality of life

Worth an honest treatment rather than avoidance.

**Crime rates vary enormously by city and neighbourhood**, and headline national statistics are close to useless for judging any specific situation.

**Sao Paulo and Rio have very safe areas and genuinely dangerous ones**, sometimes close together, and local knowledge is what protects people rather than general caution.

**Practical habits matter** — phone use in public, transport choices, awareness at traffic lights — and expatriates adapt to these quickly.

**Southern cities** including Florianopolis, Curitiba and Porto Alegre generally report better safety outcomes.

**Quality of life is genuinely high** in many respects — food, social life, natural environment, climate and a culture with real warmth.

**Healthcare is good privately** and strained publicly, which is why private insurance is standard.

**And most people who move there report enjoying it considerably more than the outside reputation suggests**, which is worth weighing against media coverage that concentrates on the worst of it.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You speak Portuguese or will learn it

Brazil becomes a genuine option with a large market, a real technology sector and considerable opportunity. The language is the gate and passing it changes everything.

### Scenario 2: You speak only English

Your market is a narrow band of international companies, primarily in São Paulo. It exists and it is small, and Portuguese study should begin immediately.

### Scenario 3: You work in fintech

Brazil is one of the more interesting fintech markets globally, with Pix having transformed payments and a large population that moved rapidly into digital banking. It is a genuine reason to choose this market.

> **Tip:** Treat Portuguese as a prerequisite rather than an advantage. Brazil's professional market operates in Portuguese to a degree that surprises people from more English-accommodating countries, and Spanish is genuinely not a substitute.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Learn Portuguese seriously
- Consider fintech and agritech specifically
- Get a CPF early
- Understand CLT versus PJ contracts
- Check diploma revalidation if regulated
- Compare costs against real São Paulo housing

### Don't

- Assume Spanish will suffice
- Expect English-only roles outside a narrow band
- Underestimate the local talent pool
- Ignore the contract type in an offer
- Skip document apostille and translation
- Dismiss cities outside São Paulo

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming Spanish works.** It does not, and the assumption causes real problems.
- **Underestimating the language requirement.** Brazil is stricter than most markets on this.
- **Ignoring contract type.** CLT and PJ carry entirely different rights and obligations.
- **Overlooking fintech.** Brazil's most distinctive technology opportunity.
- **São Paulo tunnel vision.** Florianópolis, Porto Alegre and Belo Horizonte have real sectors.
- **Skipping revalidation.** Regulated professions require formal diploma recognition.
