## Key Takeaways

- Most etiquette rules reduce to one thing: demonstrating that you are reliable and considerate of others' position.
- Keigo — business Japanese — matters more than general fluency in how you are perceived.
- Punctuality means early, and lateness carries more weight than in most Western workplaces.
- Reporting proactively (*horenso*) is the workplace habit foreign professionals most often underestimate.
- Nobody expects perfection from a foreigner, but visible effort is noticed and rewarded.

Japanese business etiquette has a reputation for being an elaborate system of rituals that foreigners inevitably violate. The reality is more forgiving and more practical: most of the conventions exist to signal reliability and to avoid putting other people in an awkward position, and once you understand that, the specific rules make sense rather than requiring memorisation.

Nobody expects a foreign professional to be flawless. Visible effort counts for a great deal.

## Horenso — the habit that matters most

**報連相** — *hokoku* (report), *renraku* (inform), *sodan* (consult). The single most important workplace convention and the one foreign professionals most often underestimate.

**What it means in practice:** you report progress proactively, you keep relevant people informed before they have to ask, and you consult before making decisions that affect others.

**Why it matters here more than elsewhere:** Japanese workplaces run on shared awareness. A manager who is surprised by something you were working on has been failed, even if the outcome was fine.

**What foreign professionals get wrong:** working independently and presenting finished results. In many Western workplaces that reads as autonomy. Here it frequently reads as having excluded people who should have been informed.

**Practical version:** update more often than feels necessary, flag problems early rather than solving them silently, and check before deciding anything with wider impact. This single habit affects how competent you are perceived to be more than your actual output does.

## Keigo

Business Japanese — the honorific and humble forms — is a separate skill from conversational fluency, and it is not tested by the JLPT.

**Why it matters:** using casual forms with a client or a senior colleague is noticeable in a way that a grammatical error is not. It reads as carelessness rather than as a language gap.

**The realistic approach for a foreign professional:**

- **Learn the standard set phrases** for meetings, emails and phone calls. A relatively small number cover most situations.
- **Default to polite forms** (です/ます) universally until you know the workplace. Over-politeness is a much smaller error than under-politeness.
- **Ask a colleague to check important emails** early on. Most people are happy to.

**Nobody expects native-level keigo from a foreigner**, but visible effort is noticed and appreciated. See [the JLPT for jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-jlpt-for-jobs-guide).

## Meetings and punctuality

**Early is on time.** Arriving five to ten minutes before a meeting is standard. Arriving exactly on time is arriving late by local convention, and actual lateness carries more weight than in most Western workplaces.

**Seating follows seniority** in formal settings — the seat furthest from the door is generally the most senior position. You will not be expected to know this perfectly, but following others' lead rather than sitting first is a safe habit.

**Meetings frequently confirm decisions** rather than making them. Much of the actual deciding happens beforehand in informal consultation — *nemawashi*. If you want a proposal accepted, discussing it with key people before the meeting is far more effective than arguing well during it.

**Silence is not disagreement.** Space in conversation is normal, and filling every pause is a common foreign habit that reads as impatience.

## Business cards

Less ceremonial than it is often described, but the basics matter.

**Present and receive with both hands**, with the card facing the recipient.

**Read it before putting it away.** Glancing at the card is the point — it acknowledges the person.

**Do not write on it** or stuff it in a pocket in front of them. Place received cards on the table during the meeting, in front of you.

**Have your own**, in Japanese on one side if possible.

This matters most in client meetings and first introductions rather than internally.

## Email and communication

**More formal than most Western business email.** Set phrases open and close messages, and skipping them reads as brusque.

**Keep a template.** Save the standard opening and closing constructions and reuse them — this is entirely normal and everyone does it.

**Copy people appropriately.** Under-copying is a horenso failure; over-copying is rarely criticised.

**Respond promptly**, even if only to acknowledge receipt and give a timeline.

## What matters less than expected

**Perfect bowing.** A slight nod is fine for a foreign professional in most situations. Nobody is scoring the angle.

**Drinking.** After-work socialising remains common but attendance expectations have relaxed considerably, and declining alcohol is unremarkable now.

**Extreme formality with close colleagues.** Workplaces vary, and many modern companies are considerably more casual internally than the stereotype.

**Being flawless.** Effort and consideration matter far more than precision. Colleagues generally appreciate a foreign professional trying rather than expecting them to have mastered it.

## Common Mistakes

**Working independently and presenting finished results.** It reads as excluding people who should have been informed, not as autonomy — horenso is the habit that most shapes how you are perceived.

**Solving problems silently.** Flagging early is expected; discovering a problem after it was resolved still counts as having been kept in the dark.

**Using casual Japanese with seniors or clients.** It reads as carelessness rather than as a language gap, which is why keigo matters more than general fluency.

**Arriving exactly on time.** Five to ten minutes early is the convention, and arriving on the hour is effectively late.

**Trying to win a decision during the meeting.** Much of the deciding happens beforehand through informal consultation, so persuading people in advance is far more effective.

**Filling every silence.** Pauses are normal and rushing to fill them reads as impatience.

**Pocketing a business card without reading it.** Glancing at it is the whole point of the exchange.

**Assuming you must be perfect.** Visible effort counts for far more than precision, and colleagues generally respond well to a foreigner clearly trying.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is horenso?

Report, inform, consult — the convention of proactively updating colleagues, flagging issues early and consulting before decisions with wider impact. It is the workplace habit foreign professionals most often underestimate.

### Do I need perfect keigo?

No. Learn the standard set phrases, default to polite forms, and ask a colleague to check important emails early on. Visible effort is appreciated.

### How early should I arrive?

Five to ten minutes before a meeting. Arriving exactly on time is effectively arriving late by local convention.

### Is business card etiquette really that important?

The basics matter in client meetings and first introductions — both hands, read it, place it on the table rather than pocketing it. Internally it matters less.

### Do I have to attend after-work drinking?

Expectations have relaxed considerably. Attending occasionally builds relationships, but declining is unremarkable now and declining alcohol while attending is entirely normal.

### What is nemawashi?

Informal consultation before a meeting to build agreement. Meetings frequently confirm decisions rather than making them, so persuading people beforehand is more effective than arguing during.

### Will colleagues be offended if I get things wrong?

Rarely. Nobody expects a foreign professional to be flawless, and effort is generally met with patience and appreciation.

## Make This Practical

Adopt horenso deliberately from your first week — report progress before being asked, flag problems while they are still small, and consult before deciding anything that affects other people. It is the single habit that most determines how competent Japanese colleagues consider you, and working independently to present polished results actively works against you here.

Then learn the set phrases rather than the whole system. A relatively small number of keigo constructions cover most meetings, emails and calls, so save templates and default to polite forms universally until you know the workplace.

Finally, redirect your effort from ritual to substance. Bowing angles and card ceremony matter far less than arriving early, responding promptly, copying the right people, and building agreement before meetings rather than during them.
