## Key Takeaways

- Vietnam has been among **Asia's fastest-growing economies** for years, driven by manufacturing, exports and a young workforce.
- **Manufacturing and supply chain** is the largest driver, with substantial international investment relocating production there.
- **Technology and software outsourcing** has grown into a genuine sector, with Vietnamese firms serving global clients.
- **English teaching remains the most accessible entry** for native speakers, and it is a separate track from professional work.
- **Foreign workers need a work permit**, and the employer must demonstrate the role cannot be filled locally.

## The economy

Vietnam's growth story is real and it has a specific shape.

**Manufacturing is the engine.** Electronics, textiles, footwear and increasingly higher-value assembly. Global companies moved substantial production capacity into Vietnam over the past decade, and the supply chain around it employs enormous numbers.

**Electronics in particular.** Samsung's Vietnamese operations are very large, and the components ecosystem around consumer electronics manufacturing is substantial.

**Software and IT outsourcing.** FPT, TMA, NashTech and a growing tier of firms serve international clients, and Vietnam has built a reputation for engineering quality at competitive rates.

**A domestic technology sector** including VNG, MoMo, Tiki and a startup ecosystem, primarily in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi.

**Finance and banking**, growing with the economy.

**Tourism and hospitality**, substantial and recovered.

**Agriculture and agri-processing**, still a large employer and increasingly technical.

**And education**, including a very large English-teaching market.

## What foreigners actually do there

The honest map of foreign employment.

**English teaching** is the largest category by headcount and the most accessible. Requirements are typically a degree, a TEFL certificate and native or near-native English, with the employer handling the work permit.

**Management and technical roles at foreign-invested manufacturers.** Quality, production, supply chain and engineering positions at the international companies operating plants there.

**Senior and specialist roles** at Vietnamese companies where specific expertise is not locally available.

**Technology roles**, though the local talent pool is strong and growing, which means a foreign hire needs to bring something genuinely additional.

**Hospitality management** at international hotel groups.

**Consulting, legal and financial services** serving foreign investors.

**Entrepreneurship**, with a visible foreign-founded business community particularly in Ho Chi Minh City.

**The pattern:** foreign professional employment concentrates where international companies operate or where a specific expertise gap exists. Vietnam has a large, capable and inexpensive domestic workforce, which means the bar for hiring a foreigner into a general role is high.

## Language and daily life

**Vietnamese is required for most domestic roles** and it is a tonal language that is genuinely difficult for most learners.

**English works** at foreign-invested companies, international schools, hotels and within the expatriate business community.

**Ho Chi Minh City has the largest international community**, with Hanoi second and Da Nang growing.

**Costs are low** by international standards, which means even a modest foreign salary supports a comfortable life.

**And the country is young and fast-changing**, which shows up in the pace of development and in how quickly things shift.

## Money and cost of living

The financial picture.

**Foreign professional salaries vary enormously** by sector and employer — an expatriate package at an international manufacturer differs greatly from a local-contract role.

**Local salaries are low** by international standards, and a foreigner on a local contract will earn accordingly.

**Expatriate packages** at foreign-invested companies may include housing, schooling and flights, and these are what make Vietnam financially attractive for senior roles.

**Teaching pay is modest** but goes a long way given local costs, and it is generally enough to live comfortably and save a little.

**Personal income tax is progressive** and residents are taxed on worldwide income, with non-residents taxed at a flat rate on Vietnam-sourced income. Residency depends on days present and other factors.

**Costs are genuinely low.** Food, transport, rent outside the premium expatriate areas and daily life all cost a fraction of Western equivalents.

**Healthcare** is mixed — public facilities are basic and international clinics and hospitals are good and私 expensive, which is why insurance matters.

**And the arithmetic works well** for someone on a foreign-linked salary and poorly for someone on a purely local one.

## Setting up

The administrative sequence.

**Work permit first**, obtained by your employer before you can work legally.

**Temporary residence card or a work-permit-linked visa**, which follows and determines how long you can stay.

**Criminal record certificate** from your home country and from Vietnam if you have been there, both authenticated.

**Health check** at an approved facility.

**Degree and experience documents**, legalised and consularised — the slowest element.

**Health insurance**, since international care is the practical option and it is expensive without cover.

**Bank account**, which requires your residence documentation.

**And expect the process to be employer-driven.** Vietnam's work permit system runs through the employer, and the quality of their HR function largely determines how smoothly it goes.

## Where roles are advertised

Vietnam's channels.

**VietnamWorks** is the largest professional job platform in the country.

**LinkedIn works** for the international and foreign-invested sector, which is where most foreign hiring happens.

**ITviec** covers technology roles specifically and is well used by the local engineering market.

**Company career pages** for the large foreign-invested manufacturers and international employers.

**Recruitment agencies** are active in executive and specialist search, particularly for the manufacturing and industrial sector.

**Teaching-specific channels** and school websites for the English teaching market, which operates separately.

**Chambers of commerce** — the European, American, British and other bilateral chambers — run networking and job listings and are genuinely useful for foreign professionals.

**And referrals**, which in a market where foreign hiring is relatively concentrated carry real weight.

## Working culture

Worth calibrating before arriving.

**Hierarchies are respected** and seniority carries weight, more so than in Western workplaces.

**Relationships underpin business** and time spent building them is not preamble.

**Indirect communication is normal.** Disagreement is expressed carefully, and a direct Western style can land poorly.

**Face matters.** Public criticism is genuinely damaging and correction is best handled privately.

**The pace is fast** in a rapidly growing economy, and things change quickly.

**Hours can be long**, particularly in manufacturing and at local companies.

**Tet — the lunar new year — stops the country** for a substantial period, and business plans around it.

**And Vietnamese colleagues are notably welcoming** to foreigners who make an effort with the language and the customs, which is a genuine and low-cost advantage.

## Building a career from Vietnam

Where the market leads.

**Manufacturing and supply chain experience is highly portable.** Vietnam sits inside global supply chains, and experience running quality, production or sourcing there transfers to Thailand, Malaysia, Mexico, India and beyond.

**Regional roles are a natural progression.** Many international companies use Vietnam as a base for wider Southeast Asian responsibility.

**Language matters over time.** Vietnamese is difficult and it materially changes your standing with local teams and partners.

**The economy is growing quickly**, which means responsibility arrives earlier than it would in a mature market — a genuine advantage for someone early in a career.

**And the international community is well connected.** The bilateral chambers, the industry associations and the expatriate business network are small enough to be genuinely useful.

**The common pattern** is a few years building regional or manufacturing expertise, then either a regional role, a move to another Southeast Asian market, or a return home with genuinely differentiated experience.

## Common misreadings of the Vietnamese market

Five beliefs that lead people astray.

**"It is cheap so anyone can move there."** Living costs are low and work authorisation is not easy. The permit rests on expertise the local market lacks.

**"Teaching leads into business."** It puts you in the country legally, which matters. It does not build the credentials a professional permit requires.

**"The local workforce is unskilled."** It is young, growing rapidly in capability and inexpensive. Assuming otherwise leads to applications that go nowhere.

**"I can work remotely on a tourist visa."** Not lawfully, and enforcement has tightened.

**"It is basically like Thailand."** The economies differ substantially — Vietnam is manufacturing-led and Thailand more services and tourism-led, and the foreign employment patterns follow that.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are a native English speaker with a degree

Teaching is the most accessible entry, and it puts you in the country with a legal work permit while you build a network. Treat it as a deliberate step rather than assuming it leads into corporate work by itself.

### Scenario 2: You have manufacturing, quality or supply chain experience

This is where foreign professional demand genuinely is. The foreign-invested manufacturing sector needs experienced people, and the expertise argument for a work permit is straightforward.

### Scenario 3: You are a software engineer

Be realistic. Vietnam has a strong and inexpensive local engineering pool, so a foreign hire needs to bring something specific — a niche specialism, client-facing capability in a foreign language, or leadership experience.

> **Tip:** Understand that Vietnam has a large, capable and inexpensive domestic workforce. Foreign hiring happens where there is a genuine expertise gap or an international company's own requirement — so lead with what you bring that is not locally available.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Target foreign-invested companies and international employers
- Lead with genuinely scarce expertise
- Start document legalisation early
- Consider teaching as a legitimate entry
- Compare salary against very low local costs
- Learn some Vietnamese for daily life

### Don't

- Assume a general professional role will justify a permit
- Expect to work without employer sponsorship
- Treat teaching as an automatic route into corporate work
- Underestimate the local talent pool
- Leave document authentication late
- Assume English works outside international contexts

## Common Mistakes

- **Underestimating local talent.** Vietnam's workforce is capable and inexpensive, so the bar for foreign hiring is high.
- **Expecting a general role to qualify.** Permits rest on expertise not locally available.
- **Treating teaching as a stepping stone.** It gets you into the country; it does not build corporate credentials.
- **Late document preparation.** Legalisation and authentication take weeks.
- **Ignoring manufacturing.** It is where foreign professional demand genuinely concentrates.
- **Assuming English suffices.** It works in international contexts and not in the domestic market.
