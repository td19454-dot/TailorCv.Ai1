## Key Takeaways

- Agencies place a substantial share of UK roles, considerably more than in the US, so ignoring them removes a real part of the market.
- Most work on contingency — paid only on placement — which explains both their responsiveness and their pushiness.
- Register with three or four specialists in your field, not fifteen generalists.
- Never let an agency submit you without naming the client first; duplicate submissions get candidates rejected outright.
- Agencies reformat CVs onto their own template, which is why clean structure matters more than design.

The UK job market runs through recruitment agencies far more than the American one does. A large share of professional vacancies never appear on a company's own careers page — they go to two or three agencies, and the agencies find the candidates.

Treating that as an annoyance means competing for a smaller slice of the market than exists. Treating it as a channel, and understanding how consultants are paid, makes them work for you.

This guide covers the model, the etiquette, and the traps.

## How they get paid, and why it matters

**Contingency.** Most agencies are paid only when a placement is made, typically a percentage of first-year salary. No placement, no fee.

Three consequences follow directly from that:

**They are motivated to place you.** A consultant with a live role you fit will work hard, because you are their income.

**They are motivated to place you anywhere.** Including roles that suit them more than you. This is where the pushiness comes from — it is structural, not personal.

**Speed matters to them.** Several agencies may be working the same vacancy, and only the one whose candidate is hired gets paid.

**Retained search** is the other model, used for senior and executive roles. The client pays regardless of outcome, so the consultant works exclusively and more thoroughly.

**Understanding the incentive lets you read the behaviour.** A consultant pushing you toward a role that does not fit is not being dishonest; they are responding to their pay structure. Push back and they usually move on.

## Choosing agencies

**Three or four, not fifteen.** More is actively harmful, for reasons below.

**Specialists over generalists.** An agency that only places data professionals knows the market, the clients and the salary bands. A general agency covering everything from warehouse staff to CTOs knows none of it well.

**Ask what they actually cover.** "Which clients do you work with regularly, and what have you placed at my level recently?" A consultant who cannot answer specifically is unlikely to have relevant roles.

**Check they hold real relationships.** Some agencies advertise roles they do not have, to collect CVs. If a consultant is vague about which company the role is with even after you press, treat it sceptically.

## The rule that matters most

**Never allow your CV to be sent anywhere without knowing the client.**

If two agencies submit you to the same employer, the employer frequently rejects you outright rather than resolve which agency is owed the fee. It is a commercial dispute they will not entertain, and you lose the opportunity through no fault of your own.

**So the etiquette is:**

> Happy for you to put me forward — can you confirm the client name first so I can check I have not already applied?

Any legitimate consultant accepts this. One who refuses is planning to spray your CV, which is precisely the behaviour that gets you rejected.

**Keep your own record** of every company you have applied to, directly or through an agency, and the date. This is the single most useful admin habit in a UK search.

## Working with consultants effectively

**Be direct about your position in the first call.** Salary expectations, notice period, location and right to work. A good consultant will immediately tell you whether their clients match — see [UK right to work explained](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-right-to-work-explained) and [UK salary negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-salary-negotiation-guide).

**Give them something to sell.** Consultants pitch you verbally to clients. Two or three specific, quantified achievements they can repeat are far more useful than a general description.

**Respond quickly.** Contingency means speed, and a consultant will put forward the candidate who replied first.

**Ask for interview feedback.** Agencies get feedback clients would never give a candidate directly. This is genuinely one of the biggest advantages of the channel — use it.

**Stay in touch periodically** without being a nuisance. Consultants work on what is in front of them, and a check-in every few weeks keeps you there.

**Tell them when you accept something.** Basic courtesy, and consultants remember who behaved well.

## The CV they want

**Agencies reformat.** Most put your CV onto their own branded template before sending it to a client. Which means:

**Structure matters more than design.** A visually elaborate CV will be stripped anyway. Clean headings and clear sections survive reformatting; multi-column layouts and text inside graphics do not — see [the UK CV format guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-cv-format-guide-2026) and [ATS resume formatting mistakes](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ats-resume-formatting-mistakes).

**Some ask for Word rather than PDF.** That is a genuine convention here, not incompetence — it is easier to reformat. Send it.

## Common Mistakes

**Registering with a dozen agencies.** It multiplies the chance of duplicate submission, which gets you rejected by clients outright.

**Letting a CV go without knowing the client.** Two agencies submitting you to one employer usually means rejection rather than a resolved dispute.

**Not keeping a record of where you have applied.** Without it you cannot check for duplicates, which is the whole defence against the previous problem.

**Choosing generalists over specialists.** A specialist knows the clients, the market and the bands; a generalist covering every sector knows none of them.

**Being vague about salary and notice.** Consultants can only match you to suitable roles if they know your actual constraints.

**Sending a heavily designed CV.** It gets reformatted onto their template anyway, and elaborate layouts survive that process badly.

**Not asking for interview feedback.** Agencies receive candid feedback that clients would never give you directly, and most candidates never ask.

**Going quiet after accepting a role elsewhere.** Consultants remember, and the UK market is smaller than it looks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I pay recruitment agencies?

No. Legitimate agencies are paid by the employer. Anyone asking a candidate for a fee to find them work should be treated with serious suspicion.

### How many agencies should I register with?

Three or four specialists in your field. More increases the risk of duplicate submissions, which frequently gets candidates rejected outright.

### Why do agencies want my CV in Word?

Because they reformat it onto their own template before sending it to clients. It is a normal convention here rather than a red flag.

### Can two agencies submit me to the same company?

They should not, and if it happens the employer often rejects you rather than resolve who is owed the fee. Always confirm the client name before agreeing to a submission.

### Why is my consultant pushing an unsuitable role?

Contingency pay means they earn only on placement, so there is pressure to fill whatever is live. Decline clearly and they generally move on.

### Are agencies worth using in the UK?

Yes — they place a substantial share of professional roles, considerably more than in the US, and ignoring them shrinks the market you are competing in.

### Should I tell an agency about my other applications?

Tell them which companies you have already applied to, so they do not duplicate. You do not need to share the details of other processes.

## Make This Practical

Pick three or four specialist agencies in your field rather than registering broadly, and ask each consultant which clients they place with at your level. A specific answer tells you they have real relationships; a vague one tells you they are collecting CVs.

Then protect yourself with one rule: never agree to a submission without the client's name first, and keep your own dated record of every company you have applied to. Duplicate submissions get candidates rejected outright, and this habit is the only defence.

Finally, make consultants effective for you. Be direct about salary, notice and right to work in the first call, give them two or three quantified achievements they can repeat verbally, reply fast because contingency rewards speed, and always ask for interview feedback — it is candid in a way clients never are directly.
