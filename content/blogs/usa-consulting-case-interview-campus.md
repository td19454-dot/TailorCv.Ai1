## Key Takeaways

- Cases assess structured thinking under pressure, not business knowledge. You are not expected to know the industry.
- Memorised frameworks are visible and score badly. A structure built from the specific question scores well.
- Arithmetic is done aloud. Stating your approach before calculating protects you when you make an error.
- The fit interview is not a warm-up — for close calls it frequently decides the outcome.
- Consulting recruits earliest of any campus industry, so the timeline matters more than in most sectors.

## What a case is actually testing

An interviewer gives you an ambiguous business problem: a client's profits are falling, should they enter a market, is this acquisition sensible. You have twenty to thirty minutes.

They are scoring four things:

**Structure.** Can you break a vague problem into parts that are separate and that together cover the question?

**Prioritisation.** Having built a structure, do you know which branch matters most, and can you say why?

**Quantitative comfort.** Can you set up and execute arithmetic without a calculator, and sanity-check the result?

**Communication.** Can you be followed? Do you signpost where you are? Do you say what the number means?

Notice that business knowledge is not on the list. Cases are set in industries you have never worked in, deliberately. If you find yourself thinking you cannot answer because you do not know the airline industry, you have misread the exercise.

## Why memorised frameworks fail

Every case prep book contains frameworks — profitability trees, market entry checklists, the four Ps. Students memorise them and deploy them regardless of the question.

Interviewers see this constantly and it scores poorly, for a straightforward reason: a generic framework applied to a specific problem produces branches that are obviously irrelevant. Reciting "customer, company, competition, context" for a question about a factory's production costs signals that you are pattern-matching rather than thinking.

What works instead is building a structure **from this question**, out loud:

> "To work out whether profits are falling because of revenue or costs, I'd want to split it. On revenue, I'd look at volume and price separately, since those have different causes. On costs, I'd separate fixed from variable, because a volume drop affects those differently. Can I start with revenue — do we know whether volume has moved?"

That is a framework. It just was not memorised. It is also visibly derived from the problem, which is what earns the score.

Learn the standard structures to understand the *shapes* problems take. Then build fresh each time.

## Handling the maths

Case arithmetic is not hard maths. It is simple arithmetic done aloud, under observation, with no calculator — which is a different skill.

**State the approach before you calculate.**

> "So I want annual revenue. That's units per month, times twelve, times average price. Units are 40,000, so 480,000 a year, times $25 — call it $12 million."

If you make an arithmetic slip, the interviewer has seen that your method was right and will usually just correct the number. If you calculate silently and produce a wrong figure, they have nothing to credit.

**Round aggressively and say you are doing it.** "I'll use 500,000 rather than 483,000 to keep it clean" is fine and expected.

**Sanity-check out loud.** "That gives a 40% margin, which seems high for retail — worth checking whether I've missed a cost line." Noticing an implausible number is a strong signal.

**Practise mental arithmetic separately.** Percentages, ratios, and multiplying and dividing large round numbers. Ten minutes a day for a few weeks removes most of the difficulty.

## Structuring the whole case

**Clarify first.** Repeat the question in your own words and confirm the objective. "So we want to know whether to enter the German market, and success means profitable within three years — is that right?" This takes twenty seconds and prevents answering the wrong question.

**Ask for time.** Thirty to sixty seconds to structure is normal and expected. Say you are taking it.

**Signpost constantly.** "I've looked at revenue and it seems stable, so I'd like to move to costs." The interviewer should always know where you are.

**Drive.** Cases are collaborative but you are expected to lead. Waiting to be told what to examine next scores badly.

**Finish with a recommendation.** Not a summary — a recommendation, with the reason, the main risk, and what you would check next. Even when you are not certain, commit. "I'd recommend entering, primarily because the margin structure holds at half the volume we assumed. The main risk is the regulatory timeline, and I'd want to pressure-test that before committing capital."

Trailing off without an answer is one of the most common ways strong analysis produces a rejection.

## The fit interview

Consulting fit interviews are more demanding than most students expect. They are looking for leadership, resilience, working with difficult people, and persuading without authority — and they probe hard.

Expect follow-ups: why did you do that, what did the other person say, what would you do differently, how did you know it worked. A story that holds up for ninety seconds but collapses under three follow-ups is a common failure.

The preparation is the same as any [behavioral story bank](/blog/usa-behavioral-interview-star-campus), with more depth per story. Six well-examined stories beat twelve shallow ones.

## Timing and process

Consulting recruits earliest of any industry on campus. Applications frequently open in **July and August** for the following year, with offers out by autumn. Some firms run sophomore programmes, meaning the pipeline begins in your second year.

If consulting is your target, the [autumn window](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline) is not advice, it is the entire game. Missing it usually means waiting a full year.

The process typically runs: resume screen, sometimes an online test, first round of one or two cases plus fit, then a final round of two or three more with senior staff.

The resume screen is real and unforgiving — these firms receive enormous volume and cut hard on it. Your document needs to [survive automated parsing](/ats-analysis) and read as achievement-dense rather than duty-dense. [Tailoring it to the firm](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) matters, and so does having something concrete on it beyond coursework.

## How to practise

**Do live cases with a partner.** Cases cannot be practised alone — the interaction is the exercise. Case clubs exist at most universities for exactly this reason.

**Do fewer, deeper.** Thirty cases with real feedback beats eighty rushed ones.

**Get feedback on communication, not just conclusions.** Ask your partner whether they could follow you, not whether you were right.

**Record one.** Uncomfortable, and the fastest way to notice that you mumble through arithmetic or never signpost.

## Common Mistakes

- **Deploying a memorised framework.** Visible, and it produces irrelevant branches.
- **Silent arithmetic.** Removes the interviewer's ability to credit your method.
- **Not driving the case.** Waiting for direction scores as passivity.
- **No recommendation at the end.** Analysis without a conclusion reads as an inability to commit.
- **Ignoring fit preparation.** Frequently the deciding factor between similar candidates.
- **Starting in the spring.** Consulting closes earlier than any other campus industry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a business degree?

No. Firms hire across disciplines deliberately, and engineers, scientists and humanities students are all recruited. Business knowledge is not what is being tested.

### How many practice cases should I do?

Enough that structuring stops feeling like a scramble — commonly twenty to forty with live partners. Depth of feedback matters more than the count.

### What if I get stuck mid-case?

Say so and reason aloud. "I'm not sure which of these matters more — let me think about which would move the number most." Interviewers help. Silence is what hurts.

### Is mental maths really that important?

Yes, and it is the most trainable part. Percentages, ratios and large-number arithmetic, practised briefly and daily, remove a whole category of avoidable failure.

### Does my school matter?

It affects access to on-campus recruiting more than it affects assessment. Where firms do not visit your campus, referrals and direct applications replace that channel — [cold outreach](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students) does real work here.

### Should I write while I think?

Yes. Structure your notes so you can glance down and see where you are. Interviewers expect note-taking and often look at your page.
