## Key Takeaways

- Sector is the dominant variable in UK graduate pay, with a wide gap between the highest and lowest paying fields.
- London salaries are higher and London costs are higher still, which makes the real comparison more complicated than the headline figure.
- Structured graduate schemes usually pay a fixed cohort rate that is not individually negotiable.
- What is often negotiable sits outside base pay — start date, signing bonus, and sometimes location.
- Researching a realistic figure before being asked is the single most useful thing a graduate can do before an offer conversation.

## What actually varies

**Sector is the largest factor by a wide margin.** Investment banking and some technology roles sit well above the graduate median; law and consulting sit above it too, particularly at the largest firms. Retail, hospitality, charity and some public sector roles sit below it. The gap between the highest and lowest paying sectors for a graduate is substantial, and comparing yourself against a single national average tells you very little about your own offer.

**City matters, and less simply than it first appears.** London pay is meaningfully higher than regional pay for the same role. London cost of living, particularly housing, is higher again, often by a larger margin than the salary premium. The [regional jobs comparison](/blog/uk-regional-jobs-outside-london) works through this arithmetic properly, and it frequently favours the regional offer once real costs are counted.

**Employer type matters within a sector.** The largest, most recognisable employers in a field often pay at or near the top of the range; smaller and regional firms in the same field often pay less but with materially lower competition for the role.

**Degree classification and subject have some effect** at the margin, mostly through which employers and schemes you can access rather than through pay for an identical role, the [classification guide](/blog/uk-degree-classification-and-employers) covers this properly.

## Researching a realistic figure

Read published graduate salary surveys for your sector, which give you a genuine median and a range rather than a single headline number that blends every industry together.

Check the salary stated on the specific posting, where one is given, increasingly common and the most reliable single data point available to you.

Ask people doing the actual role. A short conversation with someone one or two years into a similar job, found through your university alumni network, gives you a far more accurate picture than any survey.

Build a range, not a point figure. "Based on what I have seen for graduate analyst roles in this sector, I am expecting somewhere around X" is a stronger and more defensible position than a single number.

## What is actually negotiable

**Structured graduate scheme base pay is usually fixed across the cohort.** Everyone in the intake is paid the same, and the recruiter typically has no authority to vary it for an individual candidate. Pushing here is the most common wasted effort in a UK graduate negotiation.

**Start date is often flexible**, and it is the easiest thing to ask for and the least often asked.

**A signing bonus, where one exists**, is sometimes more flexible than base pay, because it is a one-off cost rather than an ongoing commitment.

**Location, at employers with multiple offices, is worth raising early** rather than after placement decisions are made.

**What is not usually worth pushing on:** base salary at a structured scheme, unless you have a genuinely competing offer and are prepared to name it.

## Comparing the whole package

Base salary is the headline and not the whole story. Pension contributions, private healthcare, a season ticket loan, professional qualification funding and bonus structure all add real value that a bare salary comparison misses entirely.

Ask what a typical bonus actually was last year, not what the target figure is, the two are frequently different.

Factor the real cost of the city into any comparison between two offers in different locations, using the [same approach that applies anywhere](/blog/usa-relocation-and-cost-of-living-first-job), real rent for somewhere you would actually live, not a headline average.

## Building your own realistic figure properly

**Cross-reference at least two independent sources before settling on a target range**, a published sector salary survey and a direct conversation with someone in a comparable role, relying on a single source risks anchoring to an outlier figure that does not genuinely represent the current market.

**Adjust any general sector figure for the specific size and type of employer you are targeting**, a well-known large employer in a given sector often pays differently from a smaller specialist firm in the same broad sector, a single sector-wide average blends both together in a way that can mislead for either extreme.

## What the number actually depends on

UK graduate pay varies more by sector and location than by how well you negotiate, which is why research beats technique here.

| Factor | How much it moves the figure |
|---|---|
| Sector | The largest single variable — law, banking and tech sit well above the graduate median |
| London vs regional | A London figure carries a weighting that regional roles do not, and rent absorbs much of it |
| Scheme vs direct hire | Schemes publish a fixed cohort rate; direct hires are more variable |
| Employer size | Large schemes pay to a published band; smaller employers negotiate individually |

Most large UK schemes publish the salary on the advert, which makes it the single most reliable data point available — more so than any national average, which blends sectors that have nothing to do with each other.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Look up the specific scheme's published figure
- Compare a London offer against real rent, not the headline
- Ask about the review point and progression in year two
- Ask what a typical bonus actually paid last year
- Give a researched range if asked for expectations

### Don't

- Quote a national graduate average as your benchmark
- Try to negotiate a fixed cohort rate
- Assume the recruiter can move base pay on a scheme
- Ignore pension contribution when comparing
- Take a London figure at face value

## Scenarios: comparing two offers

### Scenario 1: London scheme against a regional one

Subtract real rent for a specific postcode from each. The regional figure frequently leaves more disposable income despite the smaller headline.

### Scenario 2: A scheme against a smaller direct hire

The scheme's rate is fixed but its progression is published; the smaller employer may pay less now and negotiate individually later.

### Scenario 3: You are asked for expectations before an offer

Give a researched range with a reason, drawn from the published figures for comparable schemes rather than a national average.

> **Tip:** Look up the specific scheme's published salary rather than a national graduate average. Most large UK schemes publish the figure, and the average blends sectors that have nothing to do with each other.

## Common Mistakes

- **Comparing to a national average.** Comparing your offer to a single national average. Sector variation is larger than the headline figure suggests.
- **Comparing London and regional pay.** The real gap is often much smaller than the salary gap alone.
- **Negotiating a fixed cohort rate.** Trying to negotiate a structured scheme's fixed cohort rate. Rarely works and rarely worth the effort.
- **Ignoring the wider package.** Pension, bonus and benefits are a real part of total compensation.
- **Not researching before the conversation.** Not researching before the conversation a vague answer to "what are you expecting" is a weak position.
- **Assuming base pay is negotiable.** Assuming the recruiter can move on base pay when they cannot. Ask about what is genuinely flexible instead.

## Understanding how pay progresses beyond the starting figure

Ask directly during the interview process what realistic salary progression looks like over the first several years, rather than focusing purely on the starting figure, a modest starting salary with genuinely strong, structured progression can outperform a higher starting figure with a flatter subsequent trajectory.

Research typical progression timelines for your specific sector, some sectors have well-understood, fairly standard progression milestones, others are considerably more variable and individually negotiated, understanding which category your target sector falls into helps calibrate realistic expectations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a realistic starting salary for a UK graduate?

It depends heavily on sector and city, the range across the whole graduate market is wide, and a sector-specific survey figure is far more useful than a single national average.

### Should I negotiate my graduate scheme offer?

Base pay on a structured scheme is usually fixed across the cohort. Start date and, occasionally, a signing bonus or location are more realistic things to raise.

### Is London pay worth it?

Frequently less than it appears once housing costs are properly counted. Run the real comparison rather than assuming the higher number wins.

### How do I find out what a role actually pays?

Check the posting, read a sector salary survey, and talk to someone doing the role, a combination of all three gives the most reliable figure.

### Does my degree classification affect my starting salary?

Mostly indirectly, through which employers and schemes you can access, rather than through pay for an identical role once you are in it.

### What should I say when asked my salary expectations?

Give a researched range with a brief reason, rather than declining to answer or naming a single figure with no basis.
