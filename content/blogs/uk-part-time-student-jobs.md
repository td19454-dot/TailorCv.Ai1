## Key Takeaways

- Student visa holders have strict term-time hour limits, and breaching them is an immigration matter rather than an employment one.
- Universities employ students directly, and those roles are the best-paid and most CV-relevant on campus.
- Work that relates to your degree is worth substantially more later than work that simply pays.
- You are entitled to the National Minimum Wage for your age bracket, and to holiday pay, even part-time.
- Keep a record of what you actually did — student jobs become graduate CV content if written properly.

Most students take a part-time job to pay for things, choose it purely on hours and pay, and get to graduation with nothing on their CV but a job title. That is a missed opportunity, because the same hours spent somewhere marginally more relevant compound into a genuinely stronger graduate application.

There is also a compliance dimension international students cannot afford to get wrong.

This guide covers the rules, where to look, and how to make the work count.

## The rules if you are on a Student visa

**Hour limits apply during term time.** Student visa holders studying at degree level are typically permitted a restricted number of hours per week during term, with more permitted during official vacation periods. The exact allowance depends on your course level and is stated on your visa.

**This is not a soft limit.** Breaching it is an immigration breach, not an employment dispute. It can affect your current permission and any future application, including a switch to the Graduate visa — see [the UK Graduate visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-graduate-visa-guide).

**Practical points:**

- **Check your own visa conditions**, since they vary by course level
- **"Term time" is defined by your institution**, not by whether you personally have classes
- **Self-employment and freelancing are generally prohibited** on a Student visa, which catches out students doing paid freelance design or coding work
- **Keep records of your hours** — payslips and rotas — in case you ever need to evidence compliance
- **Tell your employer your limit** in writing, so a manager cannot roster you over it

Your status and how to state it are covered in [UK right to work explained](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-right-to-work-explained).

## Your rights, regardless of visa status

**National Minimum Wage / National Living Wage.** Rates are set by age bracket and updated annually. Part-time and student status does not reduce your entitlement.

**Holiday pay.** You accrue paid holiday pro rata even on a part-time or zero-hours contract. Many students never claim it because nobody tells them it exists.

**Payslips.** You are entitled to one showing pay and deductions.

**Breaks.** Rest break entitlements apply to shifts over a certain length.

**Zero-hours contracts** are legal but you cannot be prevented from working elsewhere.

**Tax.** You pay income tax only above the personal allowance, and many students earn under it. If you are taxed on an emergency code, you can usually reclaim it.

## Where to look, in order of value

**Your own university.** Consistently the best option and consistently overlooked.

- **Student ambassador and open day work** — pays well, flexible around teaching, and genuinely good for communication skills
- **Library, IT support and lab demonstrating** — often directly relevant to a technical degree
- **Research assistant roles** — the single most valuable campus job for anyone considering postgraduate study or research
- **Students' union** — bar, events, and administrative roles

University jobs understand your timetable, sit on campus, and frequently pay above local retail and hospitality rates.

**Department-specific work.** Marking, tutoring, technical support within your own school. Ask your tutors directly — much of this is never advertised.

**Tutoring.** Well paid, flexible, and directly demonstrates subject mastery. Note the self-employment restriction if you are on a Student visa — agency-employed tutoring is the safer route.

**Sector-adjacent part-time roles.** A computer science student doing part-time IT support, a business student in an office administration role. Lower pay than some retail, worth far more on a graduate CV.

**Retail and hospitality.** Widely available and genuinely valuable for demonstrable customer-facing and pressure-handling skills — just write them up properly rather than dismissing them.

**Internships and placements** during vacations — see [the UK internship and placement year guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-internship-placement-year-guide).

## Making it count on your CV

This is the part that separates students who arrive at graduation with a CV from those who arrive with a list.

**Weak:**
> Sales Assistant, [Retailer], 2024-2026. Served customers and worked on the till.

**Strong:**
> Sales Assistant, [Retailer] — 2024-2026 (16 hrs/week alongside full-time study)
> Handled peak-period service on the busiest department, regularly processing 200+ transactions per shift. Trained four new starters on till and stock systems. Selected as key holder after eight months.

The second version demonstrates responsibility, scale, training others, and progression — all things a graduate employer scores. The job was identical; the write-up was not.

**Include the hours per week** alongside full-time study. It evidences time management without you having to claim it.

**Name any progression** — key holder, shift supervisor, trainer.

**Quantify** — transactions, covers, students tutored, events staffed. See [how to quantify achievements on your resume](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-quantify-achievements-on-resume).

**Keep notes as you go.** Reconstructing two years of a part-time job in your final year is much harder than jotting things down at the time.

## Balancing it with study

**Be realistic about capacity.** Research consistently suggests that beyond a certain weekly threshold, paid work begins to affect academic results — and your degree classification matters for graduate schemes, many of which screen at 2:1.

**Protect assessment periods.** Agree reduced hours around exams and deadlines in advance rather than asking at the last minute.

**Front-load vacation work.** Earning more during holidays, when visa limits are also higher, reduces the term-time pressure.

## Common Mistakes

**Breaching Student visa hour limits.** It is an immigration breach rather than an employment one, and it can affect your permission and any later Graduate visa application.

**Freelancing on a Student visa.** Self-employment is generally prohibited, and paid freelance design or coding work catches students out regularly.

**Never claiming holiday pay.** You accrue it pro rata even on zero-hours contracts, and most students simply do not know.

**Choosing purely on pay.** Slightly less money in a sector-adjacent role is worth substantially more on a graduate CV.

**Overlooking university employment.** Campus roles pay well, understand your timetable, and include research assistant work that is genuinely valuable later.

**Writing part-time work as duties.** "Served customers" scores nothing; transaction volumes, training others and progression to key holder all do.

**Not recording achievements as they happen.** Reconstructing two years of work in your final year produces a much weaker write-up.

**Working too many hours during assessment periods.** Your degree classification gates graduate schemes that screen at 2:1, so protecting it has real financial value.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many hours can I work on a Student visa?

Term-time hours are restricted and the limit depends on your course level, with more permitted during official vacations. Check your own visa conditions rather than relying on what other students tell you.

### Can I freelance as an international student?

Generally not — self-employment is usually prohibited on a Student visa. Agency-employed tutoring or similar arrangements are the safer route.

### Do I get holiday pay in a part-time job?

Yes, accrued pro rata, including on zero-hours contracts. Many students never claim it because nobody mentions it.

### Do I pay tax as a student?

Only on earnings above the personal allowance. Many students earn under it, and emergency-code deductions can usually be reclaimed.

### What is the best kind of student job?

University employment — ambassador work, IT support, lab demonstrating and especially research assistant roles. Well paid, timetable-aware, and genuinely useful on a graduate CV.

### Should I put a retail job on my graduate CV?

Yes, written properly. Transaction volumes, training new starters and any progression demonstrate exactly what graduate employers assess.

### How many hours is too many?

Beyond a moderate weekly commitment, academic performance tends to suffer — and since many graduate schemes screen at a 2:1, protecting your classification has direct financial value.

## Make This Practical

Check your own visa conditions first if you are an international student, tell your employer your hour limit in writing, and keep payslips and rotas. Breaching the limit is an immigration matter that can affect your Graduate visa switch later, and it is entirely avoidable.

Then look at your own university before anywhere else. Ambassador work, IT support, lab demonstrating and research assistant roles pay well, work around your timetable, and carry far more weight on a graduate CV than equivalent off-campus hours.

Finally, write it up as you go. Note transaction volumes, people you trained, and any progression at the time rather than reconstructing it in final year — then present each role with hours per week alongside full-time study, which evidences time management without you having to claim it.
