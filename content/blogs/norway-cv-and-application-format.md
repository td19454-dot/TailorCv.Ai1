## Key Takeaways

- A Norwegian CV runs to **two pages**, plain and factual, with **no photo** and no personal details beyond the basics.
- The **søknad** — the application letter — is standard and genuinely read, and a generic one damages the application.
- **References are actually called.** Norwegian employers check them properly, so brief your referees.
- **Understatement is the register.** Overselling reads as untrustworthy in a culture shaped by Janteloven.
- **State your work permission and your Norwegian level honestly** — both are screened on early.

## The structure

Norwegian [employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) want a [document](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist) that is quick to read and easy to verify.

**Personal details** at the top: name, phone, email, city, and [LinkedIn](/blog/linkedin-profile-optimization-guide). No photo, no date of birth, no marital status. Norway takes equal-treatment seriously and these are conventionally omitted.

**A short profile** of three or four lines. Optional but useful when it says something concrete rather than listing adjectives.

**Work [experience](/blog/resume-matching-experienced-professionals)**, [reverse chronological](/blog/chronological-vs-functional-resume), with employer, location, role, dates and what you actually achieved. Include part-time and summer work — Norwegian culture values having worked, and a summer job in a warehouse is not something to hide.

**Education**, with institution, [degree](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume), dates and grades. If your degree has no clear Norwegian equivalent, one clarifying line helps.

**[Skills](/blog/resume-skills-match-job-description)**, including software and technical competencies stated concretely.

**Languages** with levels, using [CEFR](/blog/bilingual-language-skills-on-resume). Your Norwegian level in particular is read carefully, and inflating it is a bad idea in a country where you will be tested socially long before you are tested formally.

**Courses and [certifications](/blog/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume)**, which Norwegian employers read as concrete evidence.

**[References](/blog/resume-references-guide)** — named, with role and [contact details](/blog/resume-contact-section), and with their permission. "Available on request" is weaker here than simply listing them.

## The søknad

The [application](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day) letter is a real part of the assessment, not a formality.

**[One page](/blog/ats-resume-length-guide). Three or four paragraphs.**

**Open with the role and why this specific organisation.** Something concrete about what they do, not general enthusiasm.

**Middle: what you bring, with evidence.** Connect specific experience to specific requirements in the advert. Norwegian readers discount claims that are not checkable.

**Close with your availability and your work permission**, stated plainly.

**Write in Norwegian if the advert is in Norwegian.** If your Norwegian cannot support a professional letter unaided, that is real information about whether the role fits. For English-language adverts, English throughout is correct and expected.

**Do not restate the [CV](/blog/resume-optimization-guide).** The letter connects your background to their need; the CV lists the background.

## Janteloven, and how it changes your writing

This is the cultural fact that most affects how an international CV is received.

**Janteloven** is an unwritten social code discouraging anyone from presenting themselves as better than others. It is discussed with some irony by Norwegians themselves, and it is nonetheless a real influence on professional communication.

**What it means practically:** assertive self-promotion that reads as confident in the US reads as arrogant here. "Exceptional leader with a proven track record of transformational results" is actively counterproductive.

**The way through is specificity, not modesty.** "Led a team of six through a system migration that cut [processing time](/blog/how-long-to-hear-back-after-applying) from four hours to twenty minutes" conveys competence without any adjective doing the work. Let the facts carry it.

**Understate slightly and evidence heavily.** That combination is read as credible in Norway in a way that neither pure modesty nor pure confidence is.

## References, and why they matter more here

Norwegian employers genuinely check references, and this differs from markets where it is a box-ticking exercise at the end.

**List them properly** — name, role, organisation, phone and email — rather than promising them later.

**Ask permission first**, and tell them which role and which organisation.

**Brief them on what matters.** A referee who knows the job description can speak to the right things.

**Former managers are preferred** over colleagues or academic contacts, though a supervisor from a thesis or internship is entirely acceptable for a graduate.

**International referees are a known problem.** Norwegian employers know that calling a referee in another country and another language is harder, so make it easy — note the time zone, confirm the referee speaks English, and if possible include one referee who is reachable and comfortable in English or a Scandinavian language.

## Automated screening and Norwegian employers

Larger Norwegian employers and the energy corporates screen with software before anyone reads your document.

**Use standard section headings** the parser recognises — Work Experience, Education, Skills. A creatively named section may not register as a section at all.

**Single column, no text inside images.** Two-column CVs interleave into nonsense when extracted, and contact details rendered as graphics are invisible.

**Mirror the advert's exact terms.** Matching is literal — if the posting says "HAZOP" or "Aker Solutions standards" or a specific software package, use that exact wording where it is genuinely true of you.

**Export as PDF** with your own name in the filename, and confirm you can select the text in the exported file. If you cannot select it, no system can read it.

**Run it through an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) once** before your first application rather than after a month of silence.

## Adapting an international CV for Norway

Three shifts most international candidates do not make.

**Strip the self-promotion.** Adjective-heavy summaries written for a US or Indian market read badly here. Replace claiming language with specific facts and let them do the work.

**Add the modest jobs back.** Many international candidates delete supermarket, warehouse and hospitality work from their CV. In Norway that work is respected, and its absence in a graduate's history is more conspicuous than its presence.

**Translate your qualification.** If your degree has no obvious Norwegian equivalent, add one line stating the level and duration. NOKUT is the Norwegian agency for recognition of foreign education, and for regulated professions a formal recognition is required rather than optional.

**Keep dates precise and gaps explained.** One honest line about a gap is better than the question it otherwise generates.

**Maintain both a Norwegian and an English version** if you are applying across sectors, and update them together rather than letting one drift.

## The interview that follows

The document gets you the intervju; the conversation has its own register.

**Informal but structured.** First names from the outset, a relaxed tone, and a set of prepared questions underneath it. Do not mistake the informality for a lack of assessment.

**Competency questions with real examples.** Prepare five or six situations with concrete outcomes, each tellable in about two minutes.

**"Why Norway?" is asked of international candidates.** Answer with the industry, the company or the specific work — not the scenery. Employers are testing whether you will stay past the first winter.

**Expect questions about how you handle disagreement.** Norwegian teams work by consensus and interviewers genuinely want to know whether you can argue a point and then accept a decision that went against you.

**Modesty is expected; vagueness is not.** Describe what happened and what came of it, in plain terms, and let the specifics carry the weight.

**Ask about the team and the work.** Curiosity about how things are actually done lands better than questions about progression and title.

**Salary usually comes up late** and refers to the applicable tariff more than to a negotiation.

## Tailoring without rewriting

Norwegian employers expect a document aimed at the role, and the efficient way is one master you cut down.

**Keep a complete master version** containing everything you have done. It is never sent.

**Cut rather than write.** Remove the irrelevant, reorder so the relevant sits highest, adjust the profile to name the role and the organisation. Twenty minutes of cutting beats two hours of writing.

**Use the advert's own nouns.** If the posting says "HMS" or "prosjektledelse" or names a specific standard, use that exact word where it is genuinely true of you.

**Adjust the søknad every time.** It is the document that is read for evidence you understood this role, and a recycled one is obvious.

**Keep a log of what you sent where.** Norwegian processes run over weeks, and being asked about a document you cannot recall is a poor position.

## Digital tools you will meet

Norwegian recruitment runs through a few systems worth recognising.

**Application portals are the norm.** Larger employers use applicant tracking systems and an email to a general address usually goes nowhere. Apply where they ask.

**Some portals want structured entry** rather than an upload — typing your history into fields. Keep a plain-text version of your CV so copy-paste does not carry broken formatting into a form.

**finn.no applications** sometimes route through the employer's own system and sometimes collect directly. Read which before assuming your document arrived.

**BankID appears in some processes** for identity verification, particularly in the public sector, which is another reason to sort it early.

**Keep file names professional and consistent** — "Sharma_Priya_CV.pdf" and "Sharma_Priya_Soknad.pdf" — because a recruiter downloading forty applications remembers the tidy one.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are applying to an energy employer in Stavanger

English throughout is entirely normal. Lead with technical specifics and named systems, keep the register plain, and state your permit position in the first lines.

### Scenario 2: The advert is in Norwegian and your Norwegian is B1

Apply in English only if the organisation is international. For a Norwegian-language employer, a weak Norwegian letter undermines the language level you claimed on the CV — and a role that runs in Norwegian will test it anyway.

### Scenario 3: All your referees are abroad

Say so and make it easy. Note their time zone, confirm they are comfortable in English, and brief them properly. Norwegian employers will call, and an unreachable referee is a silent problem.

> **Tip:** List named referees with contact details rather than writing "available on request". Norwegian employers actually call them, and making that step frictionless quietly separates you from candidates who added a hurdle.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Keep it to two pages
- Include part-time and summer work
- State Norwegian level honestly in CEFR terms
- Name your referees and brief them
- Write a specific søknad
- Understate and evidence

### Don't

- Include a photo or date of birth
- Oversell with strong claiming adjectives
- Write "references available on request"
- Inflate your Norwegian level
- Restate the CV in the letter
- Write in Norwegian beyond your real ability

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding a photo.** Conventionally omitted in Norway, unlike Germany or Switzerland.
- **Overselling.** Janteloven means assertive self-promotion reads badly rather than confidently.
- **Withholding referees.** They are genuinely called, so listing them helps you.
- **Inflating Norwegian.** You will be tested socially long before formally.
- **A generic søknad.** It is read, so a generic one actively damages the application.
- **Hiding modest early jobs.** Having worked is valued here, whatever the work was.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I include a photo on a Norwegian CV?

No. Photos, dates of birth and marital status are conventionally omitted, in line with Norway's approach to equal treatment in hiring.

### How long should a Norwegian CV be?

Two pages. Plain formatting, reverse chronological, and factual rather than designed.

### Is the søknad actually read?

Yes. The Norwegian application letter is a genuine part of the assessment, which is why a generic one is worse than a short specific one.

### Do Norwegian employers really check references?

Yes, properly. List named referees with contact details and brief them on the role rather than writing "available on request".

### What is Janteloven and does it affect my application?

An unwritten code discouraging self-aggrandisement. It means confident American-style self-promotion reads badly; specific evidence stated plainly reads well.

### Should I include summer and part-time jobs?

Yes. Norwegian employers read having worked as a positive signal regardless of what the work was.
### Do Norwegian employers use automated screening?

Larger employers and the energy corporates do. Use standard headings, single-column layout, mirror the advert's exact terms, and confirm the text is selectable in your exported PDF.

### Do I need my foreign degree recognised in Norway?

For regulated professions, yes — NOKUT handles recognition of foreign education. For unregulated roles it is not required, but a line clarifying the level and duration of your degree helps employers evaluate it.
### What are Norwegian interviews like?

Informal in tone and structured underneath — first names, competency questions with real examples, and genuine interest in how you handle disagreement in a consensus-driven team.

### Will I be asked why I chose Norway?

Almost certainly. Answer with the industry, the company or the specific work rather than the scenery — employers are testing whether you will stay.
