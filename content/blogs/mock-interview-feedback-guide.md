## Key Takeaways

- Most employers give no substantive feedback, largely for legal caution rather than indifference.
- Recruitment agencies are the exception — they receive candid client feedback and will frequently share it.
- Asking a specific question gets a real answer far more often than asking for general feedback.
- "We went with someone with more experience" is usually genuine rather than a brush-off.
- Self-assessment immediately after each interview is the feedback you can always get.

Interview feedback is the thing candidates most want and least often receive. Employers are cautious about saying anything that could be construed as discriminatory, so the default response is a polite non-answer.

That is frustrating and it is not the whole picture. There are ways to ask that work better than others, sources that are more forthcoming, and a self-assessment habit that gives you most of the value regardless.

This guide covers all three.

## Why you rarely get it

**Legal caution.** Anything specific could theoretically support a discrimination claim, so many companies have a blanket policy of declining.

**Volume.** A recruiter processing many candidates cannot write considered feedback for each.

**Discomfort.** Telling someone why they were not good enough is unpleasant, and people avoid it.

**Sometimes there is no clean reason.** Two candidates were close and one edged it. That is genuinely hard to articulate as feedback.

**Understanding this helps** because it stops you reading silence as a judgement about you specifically.

## Asking so that you get something

**Ask a specific question, not for general feedback.**

"Do you have any feedback?" invites the standard non-answer. A narrow question is easier to answer honestly and frequently gets a real reply.

**Instead of:** "Could you share any feedback on my interview?"

**Try:**

> Thank you for letting me know. Was there a particular area where you'd have wanted to see more depth — the system design round in particular is one I'm working on.

**Why it works:** you have named the area, given them permission to be specific, and made it easy to answer in one line.

**Other framings that work:**

- "Was there anything in my background that raised a concern I could address better next time?"
- "If a similar role came up in six months, what would you want to see that isn't there yet?"
- "Was the gap technical, or more about fit for this particular team?"

**Ask promptly** — within a day or two of the outcome — and ask once.

## Who actually gives feedback

**Recruitment agencies.** The most forthcoming source by a wide margin. Consultants receive candid feedback from clients that would never be given to a candidate directly, and they share it because they want to place you next time. **Always ask your consultant** — this is one of the genuine advantages of applying through an agency, and most candidates never use it. See [the UK recruitment agencies guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-recruitment-agencies-guide).

**Internal recruiters**, sometimes, particularly if you built rapport during the process.

**The hiring manager**, occasionally, if you interviewed with them directly and ask well.

**Smaller companies** more often than large ones, which tend to have stricter policies.

**Your university placement cell**, which frequently receives round-wise outcome data from campus recruiters even when individual feedback is not shared — see [how to get shortlisted for campus placement](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-get-shortlisted-for-campus-placement).

## Interpreting what you get

**"We went with someone with more experience."** Usually literally true. It is not always code for something else.

**"It was a very close decision."** Frequently genuine, and worth taking as a signal to stay in contact — these candidates get called back when the next role opens.

**"We're looking for someone with more [specific skill]."** The most useful answer you can receive. Take it at face value and address it.

**"You weren't quite the right fit."** The least useful, and worth one gentle follow-up: "Was that about the team's working style, or more about the technical scope?"

**Silence.** Not a judgement. Most rejections are silent, and the volume involved makes it administrative rather than personal.

## Self-assessment, which always works

The feedback you can guarantee, and most people skip it.

**Immediately after every interview**, before the details fade, write down:

- **Every question you were asked**, as precisely as you remember
- **Which answers went well**, and which did not
- **Where you hesitated or lost your thread**
- **What you wish you had said**
- **Any question that caught you unprepared**

**Why this is so valuable:**

**Questions repeat.** Across companies and across years. By your fourth interview you are preparing from real data rather than from a generic list.

**Patterns emerge.** If you have stumbled on "tell me about a conflict" three times, that is a clear signal no employer was going to give you.

**It is immediate.** No waiting for a reply that will not come.

Combine it with recorded practice on the weak areas — see [how to practise mock interviews](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/mock-interview-how-to-practice) and [mock interview common errors](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/mock-interview-common-errors).

## Feedback in mock interviews

Where you can actually demand it.

**Ask your practice partner for specifics**, not encouragement. "That was good" is useless. Ask them to note length, structure, whether you said "I" or "we", and whether the point arrived early.

**Ask them to be uncomfortable.** Politeness is the main failure mode in peer practice.

**Ask what they would have asked next** — that reveals where your answer left an obvious gap.

## Common Mistakes

**Asking for general feedback.** It invites the standard non-answer; a narrow, specific question gets a real reply far more often.

**Not asking your recruitment consultant.** They receive candid client feedback that would never reach you directly, and it is the most reliable source available.

**Reading silence as a verdict.** Most rejections are silent for volume and legal reasons rather than because something was badly wrong.

**Pushing after a non-answer.** One gentle follow-up is fine; repeated requests damage a relationship you may need later.

**Not writing down the questions afterwards.** They repeat across companies and years, and this is the only feedback you can guarantee.

**Dismissing "more experience" as an excuse.** It is frequently literal, and treating it as code leads you to fix the wrong thing.

**Asking a practice partner who will not be critical.** Encouragement teaches nothing, and politeness is the main failure in peer practice.

**Waiting for feedback before continuing to apply.** It usually does not come, and pausing costs you opportunities.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do employers not give interview feedback?

Mainly legal caution — specific comments could theoretically support a discrimination claim — plus volume and simple discomfort.

### How should I ask for feedback?

With a specific question about one area rather than a general request. Narrow questions are easier to answer honestly and get real replies more often.

### Who is most likely to give useful feedback?

Recruitment consultants, by a wide margin. They receive candid client feedback and share it because they want to place you next time.

### Is "we chose someone with more experience" a real reason?

Usually literally true rather than a euphemism. Taking it at face value is generally correct.

### What if I get no response at all?

Normal, and not a judgement. Most rejections are silent. Fall back on self-assessment and keep applying.

### How do I self-assess after an interview?

Write down every question asked, which answers went well, where you hesitated, and what you wish you had said — immediately, before the detail fades.

### How many times should I follow up?

Once. A single gentle follow-up after a non-answer is reasonable; more damages a relationship you may need later.

## Make This Practical

Write down every question you were asked immediately after each interview, along with where you hesitated and what you wish you had said. Questions repeat across companies and years, so by your fourth interview you are preparing from real data rather than a generic list — and this is the only feedback guaranteed to arrive.

Then ask employers properly when you do ask. A narrow question about one specific area gets a real answer far more often than a general request, and asking your recruitment consultant gets you candid client feedback that would never reach you directly.

Finally, take what you receive at face value. "Someone with more experience" is usually literal, "a close decision" is usually genuine and worth staying in contact over, and silence is administrative rather than personal — none of it is a reason to pause applying while you wait.
