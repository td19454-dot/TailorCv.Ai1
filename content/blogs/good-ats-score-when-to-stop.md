## Key Takeaways

- There is no industry-standard passing score - the number is produced by whichever tool you used, not by the employer's system.
- Treat the score as a relevance signal for one specific posting, not as a grade for your resume.
- Most of the available gain comes early; the last stretch of chasing a higher number usually damages readability.
- A moderate score on a well-matched job beats a high score achieved by stuffing.
- If your score is low and you cannot raise it honestly, that is information about fit, not a formatting failure.

The question "what is a good ATS score?" has no fixed answer, and the confident numbers you find online - 75%, 80% - are not standards. They are thresholds chosen by individual tools.

That does not make the number useless. It makes it a different kind of useful than most people assume.

## What the Number Actually Measures

A score is a comparison between two documents: your resume and one job posting. It broadly reflects how much of the posting's language and requirements are evidenced in your resume.

Three consequences follow, and they matter more than the number itself.

**It is posting-specific.** The same resume scored against two jobs will produce two different numbers. A drop is not your resume getting worse; it is a different comparison.

**It is tool-specific.** Different scanners weight sections, synonyms and repetition differently. Comparing a score from one tool to a threshold quoted for another is meaningless.

**It is not the employer's number.** Unless you are scanning inside the employer's own system, you are seeing an approximation. A useful one, but an approximation.

So the honest framing is: the score tells you roughly how legible your relevance is for this posting. It does not tell you whether you will get an interview.

## A Working Range

With that caveat stated plainly, people still need something actionable. Based on how these tools generally behave:

**Below about half.** Something structural is usually wrong - a parsing problem, or a genuine mismatch. Check whether the scanner can read your document at all before you touch the wording.

**Around half to two-thirds.** Typically a real but under-expressed match. This is the range where tailoring pays off most: your evidence exists, it is just phrased differently or buried.

**Two-thirds to roughly eighty percent.** A good working range for a job you genuinely fit. Most resumes that reach here are legible and competitive.

**Above that.** Increasingly hard to reach honestly. Sometimes it reflects an excellent match. Often it reflects a resume that has started to mirror the posting too literally.

The useful target is the middle-upper band, reached honestly - not the highest number the tool will give you.

## Where the Returns Stop

The first round of edits usually produces most of the improvement. You fix a parsing problem, rename two or three things in the posting's vocabulary, move key evidence up the page. That is where the movement happens.

After that, each additional point costs more effort and buys less. Worse, the edits available to you at that stage tend to be the damaging kind: adding marginal terms, repeating keywords, padding the skills list with things you have touched once.

Here is what that deterioration looks like in practice.

> **Honest and well-matched:** Built and maintained ETL pipelines in Python and Airflow, cutting daily reporting lag from 6 hours to 40 minutes.
>
> **Optimized past the point of usefulness:** Built and maintained ETL pipelines (ETL, data pipelines, pipeline development) using Python, Airflow, SQL, data engineering and workflow orchestration tools.

The second scores higher. It is also visibly worse to any human who reads it, and it invites questions in an interview about tools you barely know.

**The stopping rule:** when your next available edit would make the line worse to read, stop. The scanner is not the last reader.

## When a Low Score Is the Right Answer

Sometimes the score is low because the match is genuinely poor. You are missing the central requirement, or you are three years short on a hard threshold, or the role is adjacent to but not actually your field.

No amount of rewording fixes that, and trying to is how people end up with resumes that claim more than they can defend.

A low score on a badly matched posting is the system working. It is telling you to spend your limited time elsewhere - which is more valuable advice than a higher number would have been.

## What to Do With Your Score

**1. If it is very low, check readability first.** Copy-paste your PDF into a plain text editor. Missing sections explain low scores faster than any wording change.

**2. If it is mid-range, tailor once, properly.** Align vocabulary on the two or three things the posting repeats most, and move that evidence into the top third of the page.

**3. Re-scan once.** See whether the change moved the number meaningfully.

**4. Then stop and apply.** A second and third optimization pass on the same posting almost never changes the outcome, and the time is better spent on the next application.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is 70% good enough to apply?**
For a job you genuinely fit, yes. The score is one signal among several, and a well-matched application at 70% is stronger than a stuffed one at 90%.

**Why did my score drop when I added more keywords?**
Some tools penalise unnatural repetition, and adding low-relevance terms dilutes the proportion of your resume that is clearly relevant. More words is not more signal.

**Should I aim for 100%?**
No. A resume that scores 100% against a posting is usually mirroring it rather than describing you, which reads badly to the human and is hard to defend in an interview.

**Do employers see the same score I do?**
No. Your score comes from the tool you used. The employer's system, if they use one, has its own weighting you cannot see.

**My score is high but I get no interviews. What now?**
That points away from the resume's keyword coverage and toward its substance, your targeting, or the volume of applicants - the score has done its job and the problem is elsewhere.
