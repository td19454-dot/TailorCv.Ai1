## Key Takeaways

- Express Entry is a points-based ranking system, and a job offer is one input among several rather than a requirement.
- Arranged employment points were removed from the CRS in 2025, which changed the calculus considerably for many candidates.
- Category-based draws now target specific occupations and French ability, which can matter more than a job offer for some profiles.
- Provincial Nominee Program streams remain a substantial route, and many of them do value or require a job offer.
- Improving language scores is usually the fastest controllable way to raise your CRS.

Express Entry is the main route to Canadian permanent residence for skilled workers, and it is genuinely transparent: the criteria are published, you can calculate your own score, and the cutoffs for each draw are announced.

What confuses people is where a job offer fits. For years, arranged employment carried significant CRS points, and an entire industry grew around helping candidates obtain qualifying offers. That changed in 2025 when those points were removed, which materially altered the best strategy for many applicants.

This guide covers how the system works now, where a job offer still helps, and what else moves your score.

**A note on accuracy:** Canadian immigration policy changes frequently, and 2024-2025 saw substantial revisions. Verify current rules with Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada before making decisions.

## How Express Entry Works

Express Entry manages applications across several federal economic immigration programs, principally the Federal Skilled Worker Program, the Canadian Experience Class, and the Federal Skilled Trades Program.

The process:

1. **You check eligibility** for at least one of the programs.
2. **You create a profile** and receive a Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) score.
3. **You enter the pool**, ranked against everyone else in it.
4. **Draws are held periodically**, issuing Invitations to Apply to candidates above a cutoff score.
5. **If invited**, you submit a full permanent residence application.

**The CRS score** is calculated across core human capital factors - age, education, official language ability, and Canadian work experience - plus spouse factors, skill transferability combinations, and additional points for things like provincial nomination and French proficiency.

## The Job Offer Change

This is the most important recent development and much online guidance has not caught up.

**Arranged employment points were removed from the CRS in 2025.** Previously, a qualifying job offer added meaningful points - and considerably more for senior managerial roles. That is no longer the case under the federal CRS.

**What this means practically:**

**Chasing a job offer purely for CRS points no longer works.** Candidates who were pursuing an LMIA-supported offer specifically to boost their score should reassess.

**A job offer still matters for other reasons** - it is often required or heavily weighted by Provincial Nominee Program streams, it lets you work in Canada while your PR application processes, and Canadian work experience itself carries CRS points.

**Provincial nomination remains the single largest CRS addition available**, and many provincial streams do consider or require employment ties.

Given the pace of change here, confirm the current position directly with IRCC rather than relying on any article, including this one.

## Where a Job Offer Still Helps

**Provincial Nominee Programs.** Many streams require or strongly favour a job offer from an employer in that province. A provincial nomination adds a very large number of CRS points - effectively guaranteeing an invitation. If you can obtain a qualifying offer in a province whose stream matches your occupation, this is frequently the strongest available path.

**Getting a work permit.** A job offer, often with an LMIA, can let you work in Canada now rather than waiting. That matters because Canadian work experience earns CRS points and qualifies you for the Canadian Experience Class - see [the LMIA job search guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/lmia-job-search-guide).

**Category-based draws.** IRCC now holds draws targeting specific categories including certain occupations, French-language proficiency, and trades. If your occupation is in a targeted category, you may be invited at a lower score than a general draw requires. Working in that occupation - which usually means having a job - supports this.

**Practical settlement.** Arriving with employment arranged is materially easier than arriving to job hunt, particularly given the Canadian experience problem described in [the Canadian experience problem](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canadian-experience-problem).

## What Actually Moves Your CRS Score

If your score is short, these are the controllable levers, roughly in order of return.

### Language test results

Usually the fastest and largest improvement available. The jump between score bands is significant, and both official languages count.

**Retake the test.** Many candidates take IELTS or CELPIP once, score adequately, and never retake. Moving up one band across all four abilities can add substantial points, and preparation genuinely improves results.

**Consider French.** Additional points are available for French proficiency, and French-language category draws have had notably lower cutoffs. For candidates with any French background - or the time to reach a functional level - this is frequently the highest-return investment available.

### Provincial nomination

The largest single addition. Research streams that match your occupation and circumstances, since each province runs its own criteria and some target specific fields.

### Education credential assessment

Get your foreign qualifications assessed. If you have multiple credentials, assess them all - two credentials can score higher than one.

### Canadian work experience

Earns points directly and opens the Canadian Experience Class. This is the practical argument for a work permit route.

### Spouse factors

Your spouse's language results and education contribute. If they have not tested, that is unclaimed points sitting on the table.

## Building the Application

**Your resume matters at two stages** - getting the job, and supporting your work history claims in the PR application. Consistency across documents is important, because employment letters, contracts, and your profile are read together.

**Canadian resume conventions differ** from many markets - no photo, no personal details, achievement-focused, and typically two pages. See [the resume for Express Entry guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/resume-for-express-entry-canada) and [ATS in Canada](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ats-in-canada-guide).

**NOC codes matter.** Your occupation is classified under Canada's National Occupational Classification, and eligibility, category draws, and provincial streams all reference it. Identify your correct code early, because it determines which routes are open to you.

**Employment reference letters** need to state your job title, dates, hours, salary, and duties matching the NOC description. Request these from former employers early - it is the most common cause of delay.

## Common Mistakes

**Chasing a job offer purely for CRS points.** Arranged employment points were removed in 2025, so a strategy built around this is now out of date - and the effort is usually better spent on language scores.

**Not retaking the language test.** This is typically the largest controllable improvement available, and many candidates take it once, score adequately, and never revisit it despite a band increase being achievable with preparation.

**Ignoring French entirely.** French-language draws have had substantially lower cutoffs, and additional points apply for proficiency, which makes it one of the highest-return investments for anyone with time or any existing background.

**Overlooking Provincial Nominee Programs.** A provincial nomination is the largest single CRS addition available and effectively guarantees an invitation, yet many candidates focus only on federal draws.

**Not assessing all education credentials.** Multiple assessed credentials can score higher than one, so leaving a second qualification unassessed is unclaimed points.

**Forgetting spouse factors.** Your spouse's language results and education contribute to your score, and an untested spouse is straightforwardly leaving points behind.

**Using the wrong NOC code.** Eligibility, category-based draws, and provincial streams all reference your occupational classification, so an incorrect code can quietly close routes that were open.

**Requesting employment letters late.** They must state title, dates, hours, salary, and duties matching the NOC description, and former employers abroad are frequently slow - this is the most common avoidable delay.

**Relying on outdated guidance.** Canadian immigration policy changed substantially across 2024 and 2025, and much of what circulates online describes rules that no longer apply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a job offer for Express Entry?

No. Express Entry is a points-based ranking system and a job offer is not required. Since arranged employment points were removed from the CRS in 2025, a job offer no longer directly raises your federal score.

### Does a job offer still help at all?

Yes, indirectly and sometimes substantially. Many Provincial Nominee Program streams require or favour one, a work permit lets you accumulate Canadian experience that does earn points, and it makes settlement considerably easier.

### What is the fastest way to raise my CRS score?

Usually language test results - the jump between bands is significant and preparation genuinely improves scores. French proficiency and provincial nomination are the other large levers.

### What is a category-based draw?

IRCC holds draws targeting specific categories such as particular occupations, trades, or French-language ability, with cutoffs that can be lower than general draws. Whether your occupation is targeted can matter more than your raw score.

### Do I need an LMIA?

It depends on the route. Some work permits require one; others are exempt. Since arranged employment CRS points were removed, the reason to pursue an LMIA is generally to obtain a work permit rather than to raise your score - see [the LMIA job search guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/lmia-job-search-guide).

### How important is my NOC code?

Very. It determines program eligibility, whether you match a category-based draw, and which provincial streams are open to you. Identify it correctly before building your strategy around anything else.

### Should I use an immigration consultant?

Not necessary for a straightforward application, since the criteria are published and self-assessable. For complex situations - unusual work history, previous refusals, or provincial stream strategy - regulated professional advice is worth the cost.
