## Key Takeaways

- UK employers are generally not legally obliged to provide a reference at all, outside certain regulated sectors.
- Most large employers give factual-only references confirming job title and dates, as a policy to limit liability.
- A reference must be accurate and fair — an employer can be liable for a negligently misleading one, whether positive or negative.
- Offers are conditional until references clear, so do not resign before written confirmation.
- Ask your referees before naming them, and brief them on the role.

References cause anxiety out of proportion to what they usually contain. Most candidates imagine a former manager writing candid paragraphs about their performance. In practice, the majority of UK references confirm three facts and nothing more.

The parts worth understanding are what an employer may say, what your rights are if a reference is inaccurate, and how to choose referees who will actually help.

This guide covers all three.

## The obligation, or lack of one

**There is generally no legal duty to provide a reference** in the UK, outside specific regulated sectors — financial services and some regulated professions being the notable exceptions, where regulatory rules require certain information to be passed on.

**But if an employer does give one, it must be accurate and fair.** They owe a duty of care both to you and to the recipient. A reference that is misleading — including by omission, or by giving a partial picture — can create liability.

**Which is why factual-only policies exist.** Many large employers now instruct managers to confirm only:

- Job title
- Employment dates
- Sometimes reason for leaving

Nothing about performance, conduct or suitability. It is a liability-management decision, not a comment on you, and candidates who worry that a bland reference reflects badly are usually misreading a blanket policy.

## What a reference typically contains

**Factual reference** — the common case. Title, dates, sometimes salary and reason for leaving.

**Detailed or character reference** — more common from smaller employers, academic supervisors and personal referees. Covers performance, reliability, strengths.

**Regulated-sector references** — in financial services particularly, there are prescribed requirements to disclose certain regulatory matters, and these are more substantive by design.

**What employers should not do:** state opinions presented as fact, include unproven allegations, or reveal information that breaches data protection or is discriminatory.

## Choosing referees

**Ask first, every time.** Naming someone without asking risks a lukewarm or surprised response, and it is discourteous. A referee who has been asked has time to prepare.

**Choose people who actually saw your work.** A direct line manager beats a senior person who barely knew you, even if the title is more impressive.

**Two is standard**, usually your most recent employer plus one other.

**If you cannot use your current employer** — because you are searching confidentially — say so. "Available on request, though I would ask that my current employer is approached only after an offer" is completely normal and universally understood.

**For graduates**, an academic supervisor plus a part-time employer works well — see [UK part-time student jobs](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-part-time-student-jobs).

**Brief your referees.** Send them the job description and a short note on what the role involves. A referee who knows what is being assessed gives a more useful reference than one answering cold.

**Keep them warm.** A referee you last spoke to four years ago is a weaker reference than one you have stayed in touch with — see [networking messages for referrals](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/networking-messages-for-referrals).

## On your CV

**Do not write "References available on request."** It is assumed, it wastes a line, and it dates the document. UK CV conventions are covered in [the UK CV format guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-cv-format-guide-2026).

**Do not list referees' contact details** on the CV itself. Provide them when asked, which also lets you warn the referee that a call is coming.

## Timing, and why it matters

References are usually taken **after a conditional offer**, not before shortlisting. Sometimes they are the last step before the offer becomes unconditional.

**Do not resign until you have written confirmation that all conditions are satisfied.** An offer subject to references is exactly that, and resigning against a conditional offer is how people end up with nothing. The same discipline applies as in [notice period negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/notice-period-negotiation-guide).

**Allow time.** Former employers can be slow, particularly if the request goes to a central HR function or the company has been acquired. Chasing politely is reasonable.

## If you are worried about a bad reference

**It is rarer than people fear**, because factual-only policies mean most employers cannot say anything negative even if they wanted to.

**If you genuinely expect a problem:**

**Choose different referees** where you can. There is no rule requiring your most recent manager specifically, though a large gap in who you can offer invites questions.

**Be upfront with the new employer** rather than letting it surprise them. Framing a difficult departure yourself is far better than the employer hearing it cold.

**Request a copy.** Under data protection law you can generally make a subject access request to see a reference held by the *receiving* employer, though there are restrictions around identifying the author.

**If a reference is inaccurate and has caused you loss**, an employer can be liable for a negligently prepared one. That is a legal matter, and worth advice rather than a confrontation.

**Agreed references** are common where employment ended by settlement — a pre-agreed wording both parties will use.

## Common Mistakes

**Naming referees without asking them.** A surprised referee gives a weaker reference, and it is discourteous besides.

**Choosing seniority over familiarity.** A director who barely knew you produces a thinner reference than the manager who saw your work daily.

**Writing "references available on request" on the CV.** It is assumed, occupies a line, and signals an outdated template.

**Resigning against a conditional offer.** References are frequently the final condition, and resigning before written confirmation is how people end up with nothing.

**Not briefing referees on the role.** Someone who knows what is being assessed gives a far more useful reference than someone answering cold.

**Assuming a factual reference reflects badly on you.** It is usually a blanket corporate policy, not a comment on your performance.

**Letting a difficult departure surprise the employer.** Framing it yourself is always better than them hearing it from a former manager.

**Losing touch with referees entirely.** Someone you last spoke to years ago gives a vaguer reference than someone you have kept warm.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Must a UK employer give a reference?

Generally no, outside certain regulated sectors such as financial services. If they do provide one, it must be accurate and fair.

### What can a former employer say about me?

Whatever is accurate and fair. Most large employers restrict themselves to job title and dates as a matter of policy, to limit liability.

### Can they say I was dismissed?

If it is factually accurate, yes. Many employers avoid detail and confirm only dates and title. Regulated sectors have specific disclosure requirements.

### Can I see my reference?

Often, through a subject access request to the employer who received it, though there are restrictions where it would identify the author without consent.

### How many referees do I need?

Two is standard — usually your most recent employer plus one other. Graduates commonly use an academic supervisor and a part-time employer.

### Can I refuse to give my current employer as a referee?

Yes, and asking that they be approached only after an offer is completely normal when searching confidentially.

### What if my reference is wrong?

An employer can be liable for a negligently prepared reference that causes you loss. Raise it in writing and take advice rather than confronting them informally.

## Make This Practical

Ask your referees before naming them, every time, and choose people who genuinely saw your work over people with impressive titles. Then send each of them the job description and a short note on what the role involves, so they are answering with context rather than cold.

Then protect the transition. Treat any offer as conditional until you have written confirmation that references have cleared, and do not resign before that — references are frequently the final condition, and this is where people lose both jobs.

Finally, if you are worried about a particular former employer, handle it directly. Choose alternative referees where you can, frame a difficult departure yourself rather than letting it surprise the new employer, and remember that most large employers give factual-only references anyway — which usually makes the fear larger than the risk.
