## Key Takeaways

- Foreign workers generally need an **Alien Employment Permit (AEP)** plus a **9(g) pre-arranged employment visa**.
- The AEP requires the employer to show that **no Filipino is available and willing** for the position.
- **Several professions are constitutionally reserved** for Filipino citizens, which is a hard barrier regardless of qualifications.
- **Special visas exist** including SIRV for investors, SRRV for retirees, and arrangements for regional headquarters staff.
- **ACR I-Card registration and annual reporting** are ongoing obligations.

## The standard route

How most foreign employment is authorised.

**Alien Employment Permit** from the Department of Labour and Employment. The employer applies, publishing the position and demonstrating that no Filipino citizen is available, competent and willing to perform the work.

**The AEP is position and employer specific**, valid for the period of employment up to a maximum.

**9(g) visa** from the Bureau of Immigration — the pre-arranged employment visa, applied for on the basis of the AEP and the employment contract.

**ACR I-Card**, the alien certificate of registration identity card, issued to registered foreign nationals.

**Annual report** to the Bureau of Immigration, an ongoing obligation within a defined window each year.

**Special Work Permit** exists for short-term assignments not requiring a full 9(g), with its own limits and conditions.

**And provisional work permits** may bridge the gap while a 9(g) is processed, depending on circumstances.

## Reserved professions

The barrier that catches people.

**The Philippine Constitution reserves the practice of certain professions to Filipino citizens**, and professional regulation reinforces this across a range of licensed fields.

**Practically**, this means qualification and employer willingness are not sufficient — the profession itself must be open.

**Engineering, law, medicine, accountancy, architecture and several others** are subject to citizenship and licensing requirements in their practice.

**Reciprocity provisions** exist in some cases, allowing nationals of countries that extend equivalent rights to Filipinos to practise, but these are narrow and specific.

**Corporate and management roles** are generally available, which is why foreign employment concentrates there.

**Check your specific profession** with the relevant professional regulatory body before assuming anything, because this is a constitutional and statutory barrier rather than an administrative preference.

## Other routes

Beyond employment.

**SIRV** — Special Investor's Resident Visa — for those investing above a defined threshold.

**SRRV** — Special Resident Retiree's Visa — administered by the Philippine Retirement Authority, with deposit requirements varying by age and pension status. It is popular and it does not automatically confer work rights.

**Regional headquarters and regional operating headquarters** arrangements provide for staff of qualifying multinational entities with their own visa treatment.

**Treaty trader and investor visas** for nationals of countries with relevant treaties.

**Spouse of a Filipino citizen** carries provisions including a route to residence.

**Former Filipino citizens** have specific reacquisition and residence provisions worth knowing about if they apply.

**And digital nomad provisions** have been discussed and the current position should be verified rather than assumed.

## Practical process notes

What the sequence involves.

**The employer publishes the position** as part of the AEP process, which is a genuine requirement rather than a formality.

**Documents you will need** include your passport, qualifications, employment contract, and depending on the case authenticated certificates.

**Authentication** through apostille or consular legalisation depending on your country's status under the Apostille Convention.

**Medical examination** where required.

**NBI or police clearance** depending on circumstances.

**Processing** varies and the AEP and 9(g) run in sequence rather than parallel, which extends the timeline.

**Provisional arrangements** may allow you to begin working while the 9(g) is processed in some circumstances — confirm rather than assume.

**And downgrade procedures** apply when leaving employment, which need handling properly rather than simply departing.

## Family and dependants

The arrangements that follow.

**Dependants** — spouse and unmarried minor children — can generally obtain 9(g) dependent visas on the basis of your employment.

**A spouse's right to work is separate** and requires their own AEP and visa through their own employer.

**Schooling** is available through an extensive international and private school sector, with English-language instruction being the norm across Philippine education generally.

**Healthcare** has good private facilities in Manila and Cebu and is more limited elsewhere, making insurance sensible.

**ACR I-Card registration** applies to dependants as well.

**And annual reporting** applies to each registered foreign national including dependants, which families frequently overlook.

## Comparing the Philippines regionally

Where it sits on work authorisation.

**Against Vietnam and Indonesia.** All three are employer-sponsored and expertise-based. The Philippines adds the constitutional reserved profession barrier, which is more restrictive for licensed professionals and comparable otherwise.

**Against Thailand.** Thailand's restricted occupation list plays a similar role, and both require employer conditions to be met.

**Against Malaysia and Singapore.** Both are considerably more developed for skilled foreign employment with clearer criteria and faster processing.

**The English advantage is the Philippine differentiator**, making daily and professional life genuinely accessible in a way that Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia are not for English-only speakers.

**And the retirement route is unusually accessible**, with the SRRV being one of the better-known retirement visa programmes in the region.

**The summary:** comparable process, more restrictive for licensed professions, considerably more accessible linguistically.

## Costs and timing

The practical dimensions.

**Fees apply** at both the AEP and 9(g) stages, and the employer typically covers them though this should be confirmed in writing.

**Agent fees** are common and add to the total.

**Timelines run in weeks to months** rather than days, and the sequential nature of AEP then 9(g) is what extends it.

**Document authentication is the usual bottleneck**, particularly apostille or consular legalisation from your home country, which should begin as early as possible.

**Renewals** need starting well before expiry, and lapses cause disproportionate problems.

**And departure requires proper handling** — the downgrade procedure exists and simply leaving the country creates issues that surface at your next entry.

## Common problems

What goes wrong.

**Starting work before authorisation.** It happens and it creates real exposure for both parties.

**Assuming portability.** A new employer means a new AEP and a new visa.

**Missing the annual report.** Penalties accrue and it is easily forgotten.

**Underestimating the reserved profession barrier.** Qualified people discover it late.

**Late authentication.** The single most common cause of delay.

**And relying on informal advice** rather than the Bureau of Immigration and Department of Labour directly, since requirements shift and secondhand information dates quickly.

## Verifying current requirements

Where to check rather than assume.

**The Department of Labour and Employment** for the Alien Employment Permit, its conditions and current fees.

**The Bureau of Immigration** for the 9(g), the ACR I-Card, annual reporting and downgrade procedures.

**The Professional Regulation Commission** for whether your specific profession is open to foreign nationals and on what terms.

**The Philippine Retirement Authority** for the SRRV and its current deposit requirements.

**The Board of Investments** for SIRV and investment-linked routes.

**And the Philippine embassy or consulate** in your country for document authentication requirements specifically.

**Requirements change** and anything you read secondhand, this article included, should be confirmed against the source before you act on it.

## Route comparison

Setting the options side by side.

| Route | Who it suits | Work rights |
|---|---|---|
| **AEP + 9(g)** | Employed professionals with a Philippine offer | Yes, employer and position specific |
| **Special Work Permit** | Short-term assignments | Limited, time bound |
| **SIRV** | Investors above the threshold | Residence, with conditions |
| **SRRV** | Retirees meeting deposit requirements | Not automatic |
| **RHQ / ROHQ** | Staff of qualifying multinational entities | Yes, within the entity |
| **Spouse of a citizen** | Married to a Filipino national | Route to residence |

**The employment route is the default** for anyone with a job offer, and everything else is a specific circumstance rather than an alternative to it.

**Note that SRRV holders frequently want to work** and discover that residence and work authorisation are separate questions, which is the most common misunderstanding in this area.

## If your profession is closed

What the options actually are.

**Consultancy or advisory arrangements** may be structured differently from licensed practice, though the boundary matters and warrants proper advice.

**Corporate or management roles** within a company operating in your field, rather than licensed professional practice.

**Regional headquarters positions**, where the entity structure changes the analysis.

**Reciprocity**, if your nationality qualifies under a specific provision.

**Teaching or research**, which sits differently from practice in several fields.

**Or a different country.** Several regional neighbours are less restrictive for licensed professionals, and that is a legitimate answer rather than a failure.

## Questions to ask your employer

Before you accept.

**Have you sponsored an AEP before, and recently?** Experience is the single biggest predictor of a smooth process.

**Who handles it internally, and is an agent involved?**

**Who pays the fees, at both stages?**

**What is your realistic timeline based on previous cases?**

**When can I actually start, and is a provisional arrangement contemplated?**

**What happens if the AEP is refused** — is the offer conditional, and what does that mean for a resignation already tendered?

**And what support exists for the ACR I-Card, annual reporting and renewals**, which are ongoing rather than one-off.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have a Philippine job offer in a management role

The standard AEP and 9(g) route applies. Confirm your employer has done it before and start document authentication immediately, since that is the usual delay.

### Scenario 2: You are a licensed professional — engineer, architect, accountant

Check the reserved profession and licensing position first, before anything else. This is a constitutional and statutory barrier and no employer can work around it.

### Scenario 3: You want to retire or invest rather than work

SRRV and SIRV are the relevant routes, with deposit or investment thresholds. Note that SRRV does not automatically confer the right to work.

> **Tip:** If you are a licensed professional, check the reserved profession rules before you do anything else. The Philippine Constitution limits the practice of several professions to citizens, and this is a hard barrier that qualifications and employer enthusiasm cannot overcome.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Check reserved profession rules first
- Confirm the employer's AEP experience
- Understand AEP and 9(g) as separate steps
- Register for the ACR I-Card
- Complete annual reporting
- Check reciprocity if your profession is regulated

### Don't

- Assume any profession is open
- Work before the permits are issued
- Assume the AEP transfers between employers
- Miss the annual report window
- Confuse SRRV with a work permit
- Rely on outdated regulatory information

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring reserved professions.** A constitutional barrier that catches qualified people.
- **Confusing AEP with the visa.** They are separate and both are required.
- **Missing the annual report.** An ongoing obligation with penalties.
- **Assuming SRRV permits work.** It does not automatically.
- **Late document authentication.** The usual cause of delay.
- **Assuming permit portability.** AEP is employer and position specific.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an Alien Employment Permit?

A permit from the Department of Labour allowing a foreigner to work in a specific position for a specific employer, requiring the employer to show no Filipino is available and willing for the role.

### Do I need both an AEP and a visa?

Yes. The AEP authorises the employment and the 9(g) pre-arranged employment visa authorises your stay. They are separate steps and both are required.

### Which professions are closed to foreigners?

The Constitution reserves the practice of several professions to Filipino citizens, with professional regulation reinforcing it. Check your specific profession with the relevant regulatory body.

### What is the SRRV?

The Special Resident Retiree's Visa, with deposit requirements varying by age and pension status. It grants residence and does not automatically confer the right to work.

### Do I have to report to immigration?

Yes. Registered foreign nationals must file an annual report with the Bureau of Immigration within a defined window each year.

### Can I change employers on my AEP?

Not by transfer. The AEP is employer and position specific, and a new one is required for a different role or employer.
### Can my family join me in the Philippines?

Dependants can generally obtain 9(g) dependent visas through your employment. A spouse's right to work is separate and requires their own AEP and visa through their own employer.

### Does annual reporting apply to my family too?

Yes. Each registered foreign national including dependants must file the annual report with the Bureau of Immigration, which families frequently overlook.
### How does Philippine work authorisation compare regionally?

Comparable to Vietnam, Indonesia and Thailand in being employer-sponsored and expertise-based, more restrictive for licensed professions due to constitutional limits, and considerably more accessible linguistically.
