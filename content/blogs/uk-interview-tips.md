## Key Takeaways

- Competency-based questions dominate UK interviews - "tell me about a time when" is the default format, and STAR is the expected answer structure.
- British interview tone rewards understatement, so the American register of confident self-promotion can read as arrogant.
- Assessment centres are common for graduate schemes and large employers, combining group exercises, presentations, and tests in one day.
- Interviewers expect you to have researched the organisation specifically, not the industry generally.
- Asking no questions at the end is read as a lack of interest, and it is noticed.

The technical content of a British interview is not unusual. What surprises international candidates is the format and the register - a run of "tell me about a time when" questions, answered in a tone that would sound underconfident in New York and reads as measured in London.

Get those two things right and most of the rest follows.

This guide covers the question format, the tone, assessment centres, and the practical etiquette.

## Competency Questions Are the Default

UK employers, particularly large ones and the public sector, structure interviews around competencies. Rather than asking what you would do, they ask what you did.

Typical framings:

- "Tell me about a time when you had to deal with a difficult stakeholder."
- "Give me an example of when you had to work to a tight deadline."
- "Describe a situation where you disagreed with your manager."
- "Tell me about a time a project did not go to plan."

The expected answer structure is **STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, Result.

- **Situation** - one or two sentences of context. Not five.
- **Task** - what you specifically needed to achieve.
- **Action** - what you did. This is the bulk of the answer, and it should be "I", not "we".
- **Result** - what happened, quantified where possible.

The most common failure is spending ninety seconds on the situation and ten on the action. Interviewers are scoring the action.

The second most common is answering in the plural. "We redesigned the process" tells the interviewer nothing about you. Credit the team once, then describe your own contribution.

Preparation approach and worked examples are in [behavioral interview questions and answers](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/behavioral-interview-questions-and-answers) and [competency-based interview questions](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/competency-based-interview-questions-guide).

### Prepare six stories, not twenty answers

Most competency questions map onto a handful of underlying themes - conflict, pressure, failure, leadership, persuasion, change. Prepare six solid examples with quantified results and you can adapt them to almost any question, rather than trying to anticipate the exact wording.

## Tone: Confident, Not Promotional

This is where American candidates most often misjudge the room, and it is a genuine cultural difference rather than a stylistic preference.

British professional culture rewards understatement. Claims are expected to be proportionate, and overselling triggers scepticism rather than admiration.

**Instead of:** "I completely transformed the department and became the go-to expert on everything data."

**Try:** "I rebuilt the reporting process over about six months. It took the monthly close from nine days to four, and the team has kept it running that way since."

The second version makes a stronger claim, because it is specific and verifiable. That is the pattern - let the evidence carry the assertion, exactly as with [how to write resume bullet points](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-write-resume-bullet-points).

Two related conventions:

**Acknowledge what did not work.** The "tell me about a failure" question is asked genuinely. A candidate who cannot name one reads as lacking self-awareness. Give a real example, explain what you changed, and do not pick a disguised strength.

**Do not dismiss your previous employer.** Criticism of a former manager lands badly.

Understatement is not the same as underselling. Be specific about what you achieved - just skip the adjectives.

## Assessment Centres

Common for graduate schemes, large corporates, the civil service, and consulting. A half or full day combining several exercises, and they are scored independently, so a weak group exercise does not necessarily sink you.

Typical components:

**Group exercise.** A team task with observers watching. The scoring is about how you contribute - do you bring others in, build on ideas, keep the group to time. Dominating the discussion scores badly, and so does silence. Contributing twice with substance beats talking constantly.

**Presentation.** Often prepared on the day from a brief. Structure and timekeeping matter as much as content.

**Written exercise or case study.** Analysis under time pressure. Answer the question asked.

**Psychometric and aptitude tests.** Numerical, verbal, and situational judgement. These are practisable, and practice genuinely improves scores.

**Interview.** Usually competency-based, as above.

## Research Expectations

UK interviewers expect specific knowledge of the organisation, and "I've always admired your company" is a recognisable non-answer.

Before the interview, know:

- What the organisation actually does, and how it makes money
- Something recent - a result, an acquisition, a product launch, a public issue
- Who your interviewers are, if you have their names
- How the role fits the team, and why it is open

This feeds directly into your questions at the end, which are part of the assessment.

## Practical Etiquette

**Punctuality.** Arrive five to ten minutes early. Much earlier is awkward; late is serious.

**Dress.** Business formal for finance, law, and the public sector. Smart casual is increasingly normal in tech and creative industries. If unsure, ask the recruiter - it is a reasonable question and nobody minds.

**Small talk.** Expect a couple of minutes of it at the start. It is not filler; it is part of the impression.

**Handshake and names.** First names are normal in most UK workplaces from the outset.

**Video interviews.** Very common at first stage. Neutral background, tested connection, camera at eye level. More in [remote job interview preparation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/remote-job-interview-preparation-2026).

**Salary.** Do not raise it in a first interview. Wait for them, or for the offer stage - see [UK salary negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-salary-negotiation-guide).

## Questions to Ask

Not asking is read as disinterest. Have four prepared, and ask two or three.

Good ones:

- "What does success look like in this role after twelve months?"
- "What are the team's biggest challenges right now?"
- "Why is the role open?"
- "How would you describe the way the team works together?"

If you need visa sponsorship, asking directly about their process is legitimate and shows you understand it - the mechanics are in the [UK Skilled Worker visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-skilled-worker-visa-guide).

Avoid opening with holiday allowance and remote working policy. Both are fair questions at offer stage.

## Common Mistakes

**Answering competency questions with generalities.** "I'm good under pressure" is not an example. They want the specific occasion.

**Saying "we" throughout.** The interviewer is scoring you.

**Overselling.** Reads as arrogance rather than confidence.

**Front-loading the situation.** Keep context short and spend the time on your actions.

**No genuine failure example.** A disguised strength is transparent.

**Generic research.** Industry knowledge is not company knowledge.

**Asking nothing at the end.** Consistently noted.

**Raising salary too early.** Wait for the offer stage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a competency-based interview?

An interview structured around evidence of past behaviour rather than hypotheticals. Questions begin "tell me about a time when", and answers are expected in STAR format - Situation, Task, Action, Result.

### How formal are UK interviews?

Moderately. Business formal for finance, law, and public sector; smart casual is common in tech and creative sectors. The tone is generally polite and conversational rather than adversarial.

### How many interview rounds should I expect?

Typically two or three - a screening call, a competency interview, and often a final stage with a senior manager. Graduate schemes and large employers may add an assessment centre.

### Should I send a thank-you email?

It is less expected than in the US and never harmful. A short, specific note within a day is fine. Following up excessively is not.

### Can I ask about visa sponsorship in the interview?

Yes, and you should if you need it. It is a practical question and asking it shows you understand the process. Ideally it has already been established with the recruiter before this stage.

### How do I handle the "greatest weakness" question?

Give a real one, then say what you have done about it. Disguised strengths like "I work too hard" are recognised instantly and score badly.
