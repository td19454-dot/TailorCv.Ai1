## Key Takeaways

- Japanese university career centres are deeply embedded in the recruiting system and hold employer relationships that do not exist publicly.
- They run the calendar — sessions, deadlines, on-campus events — which is the structure the whole [shūkatsu process](/blog/japan-job-hunting-shukatsu-timeline) depends on.
- Many institutions run dedicated support for international students, and it is frequently separate from the main service.
- Going in your penultimate year rather than your final one is the difference between using the system and chasing it.
- Document review, mock interviews and aptitude test practice are free and heavily used by Japanese students, which is the standard you are competing against.

## What they actually hold

**Employer relationships.** Japanese companies maintain long-standing relationships with specific universities, and the career centre is where those relationships live. Some recruiting happens through channels that only exist because of an institutional relationship, and they are not advertised publicly.

**Exclusive listings.** Roles posted to the university rather than to public job boards, seen by a fraction of the number who see a general posting.

**The recruiting calendar.** Session dates, application deadlines, on-campus company visits. Missing the calendar is the most common failure mode in Japanese job hunting, and the career centre is where the calendar is published.

**Alumni data.** Which companies hired from your programme, in what numbers, in which years. This turns a vague target list into a specific one and it is data they already have.

**Document review.** Checking your [rirekisho](/blog/japan-resume-rirekisho-guide) and [work history document](/blog/japan-shokumu-keirekisho-guide) against convention. For a foreign student writing formal Japanese, this alone justifies the visit — set phrases and register are conventional and errors in them are conspicuous.

**Mock interviews.** Practising [the standard question set](/blog/japan-mensetsu-interview-questions-guide) with someone who knows what interviewers are listening for.

**Aptitude test practice materials.** Japanese students prepare extensively for [the standardised tests](/blog/japan-group-interview-and-spi-tests), and the career centre is where the practice materials are.

## Support specifically for international students

**Many institutions run this separately** from the main career service, and students frequently do not know it exists.

**What it typically covers.** Guidance on the [work visa process](/blog/japan-work-visa-for-graduates) and the status change from student to employee. Lists of employers who have hired international graduates. Japanese-language document support. Sometimes dedicated advisers who understand both the recruiting system and the immigration side.

**The question worth asking first:** which employers have hired international graduates from this university recently. The answer is a target list of companies with a demonstrated willingness, which is worth more than any general advice.

**Ask about the job-hunting extension.** Graduates of Japanese universities who have not secured employment may be able to extend their stay to continue searching, subject to institutional support. This provision is genuinely useful and many students never hear of it — the [visa guide](/blog/japan-work-visa-for-graduates) covers the mechanics, and your institution is the authority on your own eligibility.

**Ask about language support.** Some institutions run business Japanese classes aimed specifically at job hunting, which addresses exactly the gap between conversational and business ability that the [language requirements guide](/blog/japan-japanese-language-requirements-jobs) describes.

## When to go

**Penultimate year, spring.** This is the moment. The [conventional cycle](/blog/japan-job-hunting-shukatsu-timeline) begins with preparation in the spring of your penultimate year, summer internships that same year, and the main selection period the following spring. A student arriving at the career centre in their final autumn has missed most of the sequence.

**First and second year, once.** Just to find out what exists and what the calendar looks like. Twenty minutes, and it prevents the far more expensive discovery two years later.

**Final year.** Still worth using heavily for document review, mock interviews and the roles that continue to appear, and the structural advantages of early engagement are gone.

**The pattern is the same everywhere.** The [Canadian career services point](/blog/canada-university-career-services-guide) applies identically — these offices are designed to be used before the search is urgent, and most students arrive when it already is.

## Using it well

**Book a proper appointment** rather than dropping in, and bring specific questions.

**Ask for the alumni employment data** by programme, not the institution-wide figures.

**Ask which employers have hired international graduates**, if that applies to you.

**Get your documents reviewed early**, before you need them for a deadline.

**Book mock interviews before your first real one**, not after.

**Ask which on-campus sessions are worth attending.** Advisers know which companies are genuinely recruiting from your programme and which are running a branding exercise.

**Ask about the second new graduate route** if you are already past the standard cycle, since some employers recruit recent graduates outside it.

**And ask what you have not thought to ask.** An adviser who has run hundreds of students through this system knows the failure modes, and a direct question about what typically goes wrong for students like you produces unusually useful answers.

## What they cannot do

**They cannot get you hired.** They hold access, structure and advice. The applications, the self-analysis, the language ability and the interviews are yours.

**They cannot substitute for language ability.** No amount of career support closes the gap if your Japanese is below what a target employer requires — that is a study problem with a study solution.

**They may not know immigration in depth.** Career support and international student services are frequently separate functions, and anything touching status, eligibility or the extension should be confirmed with whoever actually handles it.

**Quality varies by institution.** Some are exceptionally well-resourced; others are stretched. If your first appointment is unhelpful, that is information about that adviser rather than the whole service.

**And they cannot replace your own network.** Alumni introductions are a starting point; what converts them is [what you do afterwards](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students).

## Common Mistakes

- **Arriving in the final year.** The system is built around engagement from the penultimate spring.
- **Not asking about international student support specifically.** It frequently exists as a separate service.
- **Skipping document review.** Formal Japanese convention is exactly what an adviser can fix quickly.
- **Not asking for alumni employment data.** It converts a vague search into a target list.
- **Assuming the main service covers immigration.** Usually a separate function.
- **Using it as the only channel.** It is one of several, alongside direct applications and outreach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I first visit?

Once early, just to learn the calendar and what exists, then seriously from the spring of your penultimate year.

### Do they help international students specifically?

Many institutions run dedicated support, frequently separate from the main service. Ask explicitly rather than assuming the general service covers it.

### Can they help with my visa?

They can point you to the process and to the international student office, which is usually the function that actually handles status questions.

### Will they review documents written in Japanese?

Generally yes, and it is one of the most valuable things they do for foreign students, since formal convention and set phrases are where errors are most conspicuous.

### What if my Japanese is not strong enough for most employers?

Ask them which employers hire in English and which have hired international graduates, and look at the [English-conducted market](/blog/japan-english-speaking-jobs-guide) and [technology route](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates) in parallel with continuing to study.

### Is it worth using if I want to work outside Japan?

Less directly, and they may still know which employers offer overseas placement, particularly [trading companies](/blog/japan-trading-companies-sogo-shosha-hiring) where international assignment is standard.
