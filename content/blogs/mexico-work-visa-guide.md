## Key Takeaways

- The standard route is a **temporary resident visa with permission to work**, initiated by the employer through the **INM**.
- **The canje** — exchanging your visa for a residence card after arrival — is mandatory and time-limited.
- **A financial solvency route exists** for temporary residence without an employer, though it does not automatically confer work rights.
- **Permanent residence** becomes available after four years of temporary residence, or immediately in certain circumstances.
- **The process is comparatively straightforward** by regional standards, and the deadlines are unforgiving.

## The employment route

How the standard process works.

**Your [employer](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) applies to the INM** — the Instituto Nacional de Migracion — for authorisation to hire you, which requires the employer to be registered as a certified employer with the institute.

**INM issues an authorisation** referencing a case number, which is what the consulate works from.

**You attend a Mexican consulate** in your country of residence within the validity period, present [documents](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist) and are interviewed.

**The consulate places a visa in your passport**, valid for a single entry within a defined window.

**You enter Mexico** and receive an entry form marked for the canje.

**You complete the canje** at an INM office within thirty days of entry, exchanging the visa for a temporary resident card with permission to work.

**And you register** for CURP, RFC and IMSS, which follow from the residence card.

**The thirty-day canje [deadline](/blog/campus-placement-preparation-timeline) is the part people miss**, and missing it creates disproportionate problems relative to how simple the step is.

## Routes without an employer

Alternatives worth knowing.

**Financial solvency** — temporary residence based on demonstrating sufficient income or savings over a defined period. It grants residence and does not automatically include permission to work, which must be applied for separately.

**Family unity** for spouses, partners and relatives of Mexican citizens or residents, which generally does include work rights.

**Mexican children** carries strong provisions for parents.

**Investment** in a Mexican company above a threshold.

**Student visas**, which permit limited work in defined circumstances.

**Permanent residence directly**, available in specific cases including retirement with sufficient income, family circumstances and points-based criteria that exist on paper.

**And the humanitarian and refugee framework**, which is separate.

**Note the distinction that catches people:** residence and work permission are separate questions on several of these routes, and having one does not automatically give you the other.

## Documents and preparation

What the process actually requires.

**Passport** with adequate validity and blank pages.

**INM authorisation** with its case number, which your employer obtains and provides.

**Photographs** to consular specification, which is exacting and worth checking.

**Completed forms**, which vary slightly by consulate.

**Proof of the employment relationship** in the form your consulate specifies.

**Apostilled documents** where required, including qualifications and civil documents.

**Translation into Spanish** where the consulate requires it.

**Fees**, payable at the consular stage and again at the canje.

**And an appointment**, which at busy consulates can be the longest single wait in the process and should be booked the moment the authorisation issues.

## After the canje

The registrations that follow.

**CURP** is generated as part of the residence process and functions as your national identity code.

**RFC** from the tax authority, required for employment, invoicing and most financial activity.

**IMSS** enrolment, handled by your employer, giving access to public [healthcare](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist) and building social security entitlements.

**INFONAVIT** [contributions](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer), the [housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide) fund, which accrue alongside.

**Bank account**, requiring your residence card and RFC.

**Address registration** and notification of changes to INM, which is an obligation people forget.

**And renewal**, which must be started before expiry — temporary residence is granted for a period and renewed up to four years total before permanent residence becomes available.

## Professional licensing

A separate question from immigration.

**Regulated professions require a cedula profesional** to practise, issued by the Direccion General de Profesiones.

**Foreign qualifications require revalidation** through the education authorities before a cedula can be issued.

**Medicine, law, engineering, architecture, accountancy and several other fields** are affected.

**Some professions carry nationality requirements** or reciprocity conditions, which need checking specifically.

**Technology and most business roles are unregulated**, which is why foreign hiring concentrates there.

**And having work authorisation does not mean you may practise** — these are genuinely separate systems and conflating them causes real problems for professionals who arrive assuming otherwise.

## Family members

The arrangements for dependants.

**Spouses, concubinage partners and dependent children** can obtain residence on the basis of family unity with your temporary residence.

**They apply through the same consular and canje sequence**, with their own documents and their own deadlines.

**Work permission for a spouse is a separate [application](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day)** rather than automatic, and it can generally be obtained.

**Schooling** is available through a substantial private and international school sector in the major cities, with public education free and variable.

**Healthcare** through IMSS for registered family members, with private insurance being a standard employer benefit and effectively expected at professional level.

**Apostilled marriage and birth certificates** with Spanish translation are required, and this is where family applications usually stall.

**And children born in Mexico are Mexican citizens**, which has significant and permanent implications for the family that are worth understanding in advance rather than discovering later.

## Permanent residence and citizenship

Where the route leads.

**Four years of temporary residence** generally qualifies you to apply for permanent residence.

**Family circumstances can shorten this considerably**, particularly marriage to a Mexican citizen or having a Mexican child.

**Retirement with sufficient income** allows permanent residence directly in some cases.

**Permanent residence carries unrestricted work rights** and no renewal obligation, which is a genuine simplification.

**Naturalisation** becomes available after a qualifying residence period, shorter for spouses of Mexican citizens and for nationals of Latin American and Iberian countries.

**Spanish language and Mexican history examinations** form part of naturalisation.

**Mexico permits dual citizenship**, and Mexican citizenship carries the additional value of TN visa access to the United States under USMCA for listed professions.

## Where to verify

Requirements change and this article should be checked against the sources.

**INM** for residence categories, the canje, renewals and change notifications.

**Your nearest Mexican consulate** for visa application specifics, which vary meaningfully by post.

**SAT** for RFC and tax registration.

**IMSS** for social security enrolment and entitlements.

**The Direccion General de Profesiones** for cedula profesional and regulated professions.

**And SEP** for revalidation of foreign qualifications, which is a separate process from anything immigration-related.

## The TN visa, in reverse

Worth mentioning because it shapes [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide) decisions in this region.

**Mexican and Canadian nationals** have access to the TN visa for professional work in the United States under USMCA, covering a defined list of occupations.

**It is considerably simpler than the H-1B** — no lottery, no annual cap, and renewable indefinitely in principle.

**The occupation list is specific** and your role must genuinely map to it, with the [degree](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume) requirement being strict.

**Which matters for anyone weighing Mexico** as a destination, since acquiring Mexican citizenship eventually opens this route in a way that most other nationalities cannot access.

**And it works in the other direction too**, since American and Canadian professionals moving to Mexico frequently do so within companies that operate across the whole USMCA area.

## Route comparison

Setting the options side by side.

| Route | Who it suits | Work rights |
|---|---|---|
| **Temporary resident with work permission** | Employed professionals with an offer | Yes, tied to registered employment |
| **Financial solvency** | Those with income or savings, no employer | Not automatic, applied for separately |
| **Family unity** | Spouses and relatives of citizens or residents | Generally yes, separate application for spouses |
| **Investor** | Those investing above a threshold | Yes, within the business |
| **Permanent residence** | After four years, or specific circumstances | Unrestricted |
| **Student** | Enrolled students | Limited, defined circumstances |

**The employment route is the default** for anyone with an offer, and the financial solvency route is what makes Mexico unusually accessible to people without one — provided they understand that residence and work permission are separate.

## Common problems

What actually goes wrong.

**Missing the canje.** Thirty days, and it is the single most common failure.

**Assuming solvency residence permits work.** It does not automatically.

**Letting the consular visa window lapse** between issue and entry.

**Working on a tourist entry** while waiting, which is more common than it should be and carries real exposure.

**Not notifying INM of a change** of employer or address.

**Late renewal**, which turns a routine step into a problem.

**And confusing immigration status with professional licensing**, which catches regulated professionals who arrive with valid residence and discover they cannot practise.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have a Mexican job offer

The employer-driven route applies. Confirm your employer is INM-certified, book the consular appointment as soon as the authorisation issues, and diarise the canje deadline before you fly.

### Scenario 2: You want to live in Mexico without a job offer

The financial solvency route grants temporary residence on demonstrated income or savings. Note carefully that it does not automatically include permission to work.

### Scenario 3: You are married to a Mexican citizen

The family unity route is considerably simpler than the employment one and generally includes work rights, and it is worth pursuing in preference to employer sponsorship.

> **Tip:** Diarise the canje deadline before you get on the plane. You have thirty days from entry to exchange your visa for a residence card, and missing that window causes problems out of all proportion to how simple the step actually is.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Confirm your employer is INM-certified
- Book the consular appointment promptly
- Complete the canje within thirty days
- Get CURP and RFC early
- Check whether your route includes work permission
- Start renewals well before expiry

### Don't

- Assume residence includes work rights
- Miss the canje window
- Let the consular visa window lapse
- Work on a tourist entry
- Assume the permit transfers between employers without notification
- Rely on outdated requirement summaries

## Common Mistakes

- **Missing the canje.** Thirty days from entry, and it catches people constantly.
- **Confusing residence with work rights.** They are separate on several routes.
- **Working on a tourist entry.** It happens and the exposure is real.
- **Assuming employer portability.** Changes must be notified to INM.
- **Late [apostille](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide).** The usual cause of consular delay.
- **Overlooking family routes.** Frequently simpler than employment sponsorship.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What visa do I need to work in Mexico?

A temporary resident visa with permission to work, initiated by your employer through the INM and issued at a Mexican consulate in your country of residence.

### What is the canje?

The mandatory exchange of your consular visa for a temporary resident card, completed at an INM office within thirty days of entering Mexico.

### Can I get residence without a job offer?

Yes, through the financial solvency route based on demonstrated income or savings. It grants residence and does not automatically include permission to work.

### How long until permanent residence?

Generally after four years of temporary residence, with certain circumstances including family and retirement allowing it sooner or directly.

### Can I change employers?

Changes must be notified to INM. The permit is tied to your registered employment and a change requires the proper procedure rather than being automatic.

### Is the process difficult?

Comparatively straightforward by regional standards. The deadlines, particularly the thirty-day canje, are the part that causes most problems.
### What documents does a Mexican work visa need?

Passport, the INM authorisation with its case number, consular-specification photographs, proof of the employment relationship, apostilled qualifications and civil documents, and Spanish translations where required.

### Do I need a cedula profesional?

Only for regulated professions, and it requires revalidation of foreign qualifications through the education authorities. It is a separate system from immigration and having work authorisation does not confer it.
### Can my family join me in Mexico?

Yes, through family unity residence, following the same consular and canje sequence with their own documents. A spouse's work permission is a separate application rather than automatic.

### How long until Mexican permanent residence?

Generally four years of temporary residence, considerably shorter with family circumstances such as marriage to a Mexican citizen or having a Mexican child.
### What is the TN visa?

A United States professional work visa available to Mexican and Canadian nationals under USMCA, covering a defined occupation list. It has no lottery or annual cap, making it considerably simpler than the H-1B.

### What goes wrong most often?

Missing the thirty-day canje deadline, assuming financial solvency residence includes work rights, letting the consular visa window lapse, and confusing immigration status with professional licensing.
