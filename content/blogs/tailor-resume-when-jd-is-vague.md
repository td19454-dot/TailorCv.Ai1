## Key Takeaways

- **A vague posting is still full of signal**, just not in the requirements section.
- **The company, the team and the title** tell you more than a thin description does.
- **Vagueness is itself information** about how well defined the role is internally.
- **Tailor to the inferred role**, not to the words on the page, and state your interpretation clearly.
- **Ask.** A short message to the recruiter frequently produces the detail the posting omitted.

## Where the signal actually is

What to read when the requirements are generic.

**The job title carries real weight**, because titles map to salary bands, seniority and function inside the organisation even when the description is boilerplate.

**The team or department named** in the posting narrows the work substantially. A data analyst in finance operations does something quite different from one in growth marketing.

**The reporting line**, when stated, tells you the seniority and the function you would sit under.

**The company itself** is often the largest source of signal. Its size, sector, stage and public product tell you what the role must involve, regardless of what the posting says.

**Other postings from the same company** fill in the gaps. If they are hiring three of these, the pattern across all three reveals the shape of the team.

**The application questions** frequently ask for exactly what the description omitted, and they are written by someone closer to the actual work.

**And the salary band, where present**, is the single most reliable seniority indicator available, because it is the one number that cannot be vague without consequences.

## Reading vagueness as a signal

What a thin posting tells you about the employer.

| The posting looks like | It usually means |
|---|---|
| **Copied boilerplate, no specifics** | HR wrote it without the hiring manager |
| **Very broad, many responsibilities** | The role is undefined or genuinely wide |
| **Buzzwords with no concrete tasks** | The team may not know what it needs yet |
| **Short but precise** | Deliberately concise, usually a good sign |
| **Vague on duties, precise on tools** | The tools are the real requirement |

**Distinguish vague from short.** A four line posting that names the exact stack and the exact problem is not vague; it is efficient, and it is frequently written by the hiring manager directly.

**Genuine vagueness usually means one of two things** — either the description was produced by someone distant from the work, or the role genuinely has not been defined. The first is a minor inconvenience and the second is worth knowing about before you accept.

**Neither is a reason not to apply.** Undefined roles frequently offer more scope to shape the work than tightly specified ones, which suits some people well.

## Researching the company for signal

Where to look when the posting itself gives you nothing.

**The company's own website tells you what they sell**, which constrains what any role there can actually involve. A team building payment infrastructure needs different work from one running a content platform, regardless of how generically the posting is written.

**The careers page as a whole** is more informative than any single listing. The mix of roles open tells you which functions are growing and where this position sits.

**Recent news and funding announcements** explain why they are hiring now. A team that just raised money is building; a team that just restructured is consolidating, and the same job title means different things in each case.

**Employee profiles in the same function** show you what people in this role actually did before they were hired, which is the most direct evidence available about what the employer values.

**The product itself**, if you can use it, tells you more in twenty minutes than most postings do.

**And the interview process described publicly** by former candidates frequently reveals the real requirements, because what they test for is what they actually need.

**Twenty minutes of this research beats an hour of staring at a thin posting.** The information exists; it is simply not in the document they gave you.

## Writing the summary when you are inferring

How to state your interpretation without overcommitting.

**Name the role as you understand it** in the first line, using the posting's title so the match is unambiguous.

**State the relevant experience in terms of the problem** rather than the tooling, because the problem is what you inferred and the tooling is what you might have guessed wrong.

**Keep one line broad.** A sentence covering the adjacent capability protects you if your interpretation was slightly off.

**Avoid narrow claims you cannot support** if the role turns out to be different from your guess.

**Do not apologise for the ambiguity** or reference it. The summary should read as confident fit, not as a hedged interpretation of an unclear document.

**And put the specific question in the cover letter instead**, if a letter is expected, where asking about the priorities of the role reads as engagement rather than confusion.

## Mini checklist

- [ ] Title interpreted against standard seniority levels
- [ ] Team or department identified and researched
- [ ] Company size, sector and stage assessed
- [ ] Other postings from the same employer reviewed
- [ ] Application questions read for hidden requirements
- [ ] Salary band used as a seniority indicator
- [ ] Interpretation of the role stated plainly in your summary
- [ ] Recruiter contacted for clarification where possible
- [ ] Resume kept broad enough to survive a wrong guess

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The posting is three generic paragraphs

Research the company and the team, infer the role from what the business actually does, and tailor to that inference. State your interpretation in your summary so the reader can see the fit you are claiming.

### Scenario 2: The posting lists twenty responsibilities across three functions

This is either a genuinely broad role at a small company or an undefined one at a larger company. Lead with the responsibilities you are strongest on and ask which are the priority.

### Scenario 3: The title is unfamiliar or invented

Ignore the title and read the responsibilities and the team. Invented titles are common and usually map to a standard role underneath.

> **Tip:** When a posting is vague, send the recruiter one short question — which part of this role takes the most time in the first six months. The answer is specific, it is easy for them to give, and it tells you precisely what to tailor toward.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Read the company as carefully as the posting
- Use the title and salary band as seniority signals
- Check other postings from the same employer
- State your interpretation of the role explicitly
- Ask the recruiter a specific, easy question
- Keep some breadth in case you guessed wrong

### Don't

- Send a generic resume because the posting was generic
- Assume vagueness means the role is unimportant
- Ignore the application questions
- Over-narrow to a guess you cannot support
- Treat an invented title as meaningless
- Skip applying because the detail is thin

## Common Mistakes

- **Matching vagueness with vagueness.** A generic resume is not the answer to a generic posting.
- **Ignoring the company.** It is the largest source of signal available.
- **Missing the salary band.** It is the most honest seniority indicator in the document.
- **Over-committing to a guess.** Keep enough breadth to survive being wrong.
- **Not asking.** One question frequently resolves the whole problem.
- **Skipping the other postings.** The pattern across a team is revealing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I tailor to a vague job description?

Read the company, the team, the title and the salary band rather than the requirements. Infer what the role must involve from what the business does, then tailor to that inference and state it clearly.

### Does a vague posting mean a bad employer?

Not necessarily. It usually means HR wrote it without the hiring manager. Genuine vagueness sometimes indicates an undefined role, which suits people who want scope to shape the work.

### Should I still apply?

Yes, in most cases. Vague postings attract fewer well-tailored applications, which means a considered one stands out more than it would against a precise posting.

### What is the best question to ask the recruiter?

Which part of the role takes the most time in the first six months. It is specific, easy to answer and tells you exactly what to emphasise.

### What if the job title is invented?

Ignore it and read the responsibilities, the team and the salary band. Invented titles usually map to a standard role underneath.

### How narrow should I go on a guess?

Lead with your interpretation but keep enough breadth that a wrong guess does not disqualify you. Commit in the summary, hedge in the experience section.
### How do I research a company when the posting is vague?

Read what they sell, the full careers page, recent funding or restructuring news, employee profiles in the same function, and the product itself. Twenty minutes there beats an hour re-reading a thin posting.

### How should my summary handle an inferred role?

Name the role using their exact title, describe your experience in terms of the problem rather than specific tooling, and keep one line broad enough to survive a slightly wrong guess.
