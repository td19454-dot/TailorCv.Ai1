## Key Takeaways

- Indonesia is **Southeast Asia's largest economy** with a very large young population and a rapidly digitising consumer market.
- **Jakarta dominates** professional employment, with Bali serving a separate remote-work and tourism economy.
- **Foreign workers require sponsorship** and a KITAS, with certain positions reserved for Indonesians.
- **Knowledge transfer to Indonesian counterparts** is frequently a formal condition of employing a foreigner.
- **Bahasa Indonesia is relatively accessible** as languages go, and it materially improves your position.

## The economy

Scale and structure.

**The population is very large and young**, which drives a consumer economy of genuine scale.

**Natural resources** — coal, palm oil, nickel and minerals — remain foundational to exports, and nickel in particular has become strategically important for battery supply chains.

**Manufacturing** is substantial and growing, with government policy actively pushing downstream processing of raw materials rather than raw export.

**Consumer technology** is the visible growth story — GoTo (Gojek and Tokopedia), Traveloka, Bukalapak and a substantial startup ecosystem serving a large domestic market.

**Financial services**, including a fast-growing fintech sector reaching a large unbanked and underbanked population.

**Palm oil, agriculture and food processing**, a very large employer.

**Tourism**, concentrated in Bali and increasingly promoted elsewhere.

**And infrastructure**, with sustained government investment including the new capital project.

**For a foreign professional**, employment concentrates in international companies, resource and energy firms, senior technical roles and specialist positions where local expertise is genuinely scarce.

## The restricted positions question

The first thing to establish.

**Indonesia restricts certain positions to Indonesian nationals**, with human resources roles being the most commonly cited example, and the list has been revised over time.

**Employers must generally justify** employing a foreigner, including demonstrating that the expertise is not locally available.

**Knowledge transfer obligations** are a distinctive feature — a foreign worker is frequently formally required to have an Indonesian counterpart being trained toward taking over the role.

**This shapes the job.** Part of your remit is genuinely expected to be developing local capability, and it is worth understanding that as a real expectation rather than a formality.

**Time limits apply** to some positions, reinforcing the transitional intent.

**Check the current regulations**, which have been amended, rather than relying on older accounts.

## Where the work is

| Location | What concentrates there |
|---|---|
| **Jakarta** | Corporate headquarters, finance, technology, government-adjacent, professional services |
| **Surabaya** | Manufacturing, trade, the second commercial city |
| **Bali** | Tourism, hospitality, and a very large remote-worker community |
| **Batam** | Manufacturing and electronics, close to Singapore |
| **Kalimantan and Sulawesi** | Mining, resources, nickel processing |
| **Bandung** | Universities, technology, textiles |

**Jakarta is where professional employment is**, by a wide margin, and it is where almost all corporate foreign roles sit.

**Bali is a different economy** — tourism, hospitality and a remote-working population that is largely not employed locally.

**Resource locations** are frequently remote, with rotational arrangements and site-based living.

## Money and cost of living

The financial picture.

**Expatriate packages at international companies** are the attractive category, frequently including housing, schooling and flights for senior roles.

**Local-contract salaries** are modest by international standards, and a foreigner on one will earn accordingly.

**Resource sector roles** frequently pay well with rotational arrangements and site allowances.

**Personal income tax is progressive**, with residents taxed on worldwide income and residency determined by presence and intent.

**Jakarta costs vary enormously** by lifestyle — local living is inexpensive, expatriate-standard housing and international schooling are not.

**Traffic is the defining daily cost in Jakarta**, and it affects where you should live substantially.

**Healthcare** is mixed, with good private and international facilities in Jakarta and limited provision elsewhere, which makes insurance essential.

**And international schooling** is the largest family expense, as across the region.

## Working culture

Worth calibrating.

**Hierarchy is respected** and seniority carries weight.

**Indirect communication is the norm**, and direct disagreement, particularly with a senior person in public, causes real damage.

**Relationships underpin business** and time invested is genuinely part of the work.

**Consensus and harmony are valued**, which can make it hard to read whether a decision has actually been made.

**Religious observance shapes the working day and year**, including prayer times and the Ramadan period, and respecting this is straightforward and appreciated.

**Bureaucracy is substantial** in dealings with government and administration, and patience is a practical requirement.

**And Indonesian colleagues are notably warm and welcoming**, with a social culture that includes foreigners readily.

## Where roles are advertised

Indonesia's channels.

**LinkedIn is the strongest** for professional and international roles, and Jakarta's business community is well represented.

**Jobstreet Indonesia** carries substantial domestic volume.

**Glints and Kalibrr** cover the startup and technology market.

**Company career pages** for the multinationals, resource companies and the large domestic groups.

**Executive search firms** dominate senior appointments, particularly in resources and financial services.

**The bilateral chambers of commerce** run networking and job listings and are genuinely useful for foreign professionals.

**And referrals**, which matter in a business culture built on relationships.

**State your work authorisation position clearly**, since employers screen on whether sponsorship is required and whether the position permits it.

## Building a career from Indonesia

Where it leads.

**Regional roles** are a natural progression, since Indonesia's scale makes it a significant market within any Southeast Asian portfolio.

**Resource sector experience** transfers globally and Indonesia is a major producer in several commodities.

**Consumer technology experience** at scale is genuinely valuable, given that Indonesian platforms operate at volumes few markets match.

**Emerging market experience** is a real credential for people moving into similar roles elsewhere.

**And the localisation dimension is worth naming.** Because foreign positions are frequently transitional by design, planning your next step from the beginning is more necessary here than in markets where you could simply stay.

## Living in Jakarta

The practical realities.

**Traffic defines the day.** Jakarta's congestion is severe and it shapes where you should live more than almost any other factor. The MRT and commuter rail have improved things on their corridors.

**Housing ranges enormously**, from inexpensive local accommodation to expatriate-standard apartments and houses in South Jakarta.

**South Jakarta** — Kemang, Senopati, Pondok Indah — is where much of the expatriate community lives, near international schools and with the amenities that follow.

**International schooling is excellent and expensive**, and it frequently determines where a family lives.

**Healthcare** has good private options in Jakarta and is limited elsewhere, making insurance and evacuation cover genuinely important.

**Air quality is a real issue** in Jakarta and worth planning around.

**Flooding** affects parts of the city seasonally.

**And the city is genuinely vibrant** — the food, the social life and the energy are real, and people who engage with it rather than staying inside the expatriate bubble tend to enjoy it far more.

## Common misreadings

Four beliefs that mislead.

**"Indonesia means Bali."** Bali is a tourism economy with a remote-worker population. Indonesia's professional market is Jakarta.

**"The talent pool is weak."** It is large, young and improving quickly, which sets a real bar for foreign hiring.

**"A foreign role is permanent."** Foreign positions are frequently transitional by design, with counterpart training and time limits.

**"It is like Malaysia or Thailand."** The scale is entirely different, the regulatory approach is more restrictive on foreign employment, and the market dynamics follow from the size of the domestic population.

## The resource sector

Indonesia's largest source of foreign technical employment and frequently overlooked in general job guides.

**Coal** remains a very large export industry with substantial operations in Kalimantan and Sumatra.

**Nickel** has become strategically significant, with Indonesia holding major reserves and pursuing a policy of domestic processing rather than raw export — which created substantial smelting and processing investment, much of it Chinese-backed, in Sulawesi.

**Copper and gold**, including some of the world's largest deposits.

**Oil and gas**, both onshore and offshore, with the associated services sector.

**Palm oil**, an enormous industry with plantation, processing and sustainability functions.

**The roles** span mining engineering, geology, metallurgy, processing, maintenance, HSE, and increasingly ESG and community relations as scrutiny of the sector intensifies.

**Conditions vary enormously.** Site locations are frequently remote, rotational arrangements are standard, and the environment ranges from well-run international operations to considerably less so.

**Due diligence matters here** more than in most sectors — the employer's safety record, environmental practices and reputation are worth researching seriously before accepting anything.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have senior technical or resource sector experience

Indonesia has genuine demand, particularly in mining, energy and processing. The knowledge transfer expectation is real and worth preparing for as part of the role.

### Scenario 2: You want to work in consumer technology

Jakarta's technology sector is substantial and growing, though the local talent pool is strong and a foreign hire needs a clear differentiator — regional experience, a specialism or leadership.

### Scenario 3: You want to live in Bali

Understand that Bali is largely not a local employment market for foreigners. It is a tourism economy plus a remote-working population, and the visa position for the latter needs proper attention.

> **Tip:** Expect knowledge transfer to be a formal part of your role. Indonesia frequently requires a foreign worker to have an Indonesian counterpart being trained toward the position, and treating that as a genuine responsibility rather than paperwork makes the job work considerably better.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Check the restricted position list
- Confirm the employer can sponsor
- Take the knowledge transfer obligation seriously
- Learn Bahasa Indonesia — it is accessible
- Consider resource and manufacturing sectors
- Understand Bali as a separate proposition

### Don't

- Assume any role is open to foreigners
- Ignore the counterpart training expectation
- Assume Bali means local employment
- Work without proper documentation
- Underestimate Jakarta traffic and costs
- Rely on outdated regulatory information

## Common Mistakes

- **Not checking restricted positions.** Some roles are simply unavailable to foreigners.
- **Treating knowledge transfer as a formality.** It is a genuine and formal expectation.
- **Confusing Bali with the job market.** It is tourism plus remote workers, not local professional employment.
- **Underestimating the local talent pool.** Indonesian professionals are capable and numerous.
- **Ignoring Bahasa Indonesia.** It is relatively accessible and materially improves your position.
- **Using stale regulations.** Indonesian employment rules have been amended repeatedly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can foreigners work in Indonesia?

Yes, with employer sponsorship and a KITAS, in positions not reserved for Indonesian nationals and where the employer can justify that the expertise is not locally available.

### What is the knowledge transfer requirement?

Foreign workers are frequently formally required to have an Indonesian counterpart being trained toward taking over the role. It is a genuine expectation rather than a paperwork formality.

### Which positions are restricted?

Certain roles are reserved for Indonesian nationals, human resources being the most commonly cited. The list has been revised, so check the current position.

### Is Bali a job market?

Not for local professional employment in the way Jakarta is. It is a tourism and hospitality economy plus a large remote-working population employed elsewhere.

### Do I need Bahasa Indonesia?

Not for most international-company roles, but it is relatively accessible as languages go and it materially improves both your work and your daily life.

### Where should I look besides Jakarta?

Surabaya for manufacturing and trade, Batam for electronics near Singapore, and Kalimantan and Sulawesi for mining and resource processing.

Expatriate packages at international companies frequently include housing, schooling and flights. Local contracts pay modest local rates, and resource sector roles often pay well with rotational arrangements.

### Where should I look for jobs in Indonesia?
