## Key Takeaways

- Swiss employers expect a **complete dossier**: [cover letter](/blog/cover-letter-generator-by-country), CV, work certificates, diplomas and [references](/blog/resume-references-guide) — not a CV alone.
- The **Arbeitszeugnis** (work certificate) is a formal reference document from each previous employer, and Swiss recruiters genuinely expect to see them.
- A **photo is still conventional** on Swiss CVs, unlike the UK, Ireland or the Netherlands.
- **Write in the language of the advert.** German, French, Italian or English — matching it is not [opt](/blog/opt-cpt-resume-guide)ional.
- Presentation is formal and precise. Swiss hiring rewards **completeness and accuracy** over personality.

## The dossier, not the CV

This is the structural difference that catches every international applicant.

In the UK or the US you send a CV and possibly a cover letter. In Switzerland you send a **Bewerbungsdossier** — a single, complete application package. A missing component is read as carelessness, and carelessness is expensive in a market this formal.

**The standard dossier contains:**

1. **Motivationsschreiben / [lettre de motivation](/blog/france-cv-lettre-de-motivation-guide)** — a one-page cover letter.
2. **Lebenslauf / CV** — [two pages](/blog/ats-resume-length-guide), with a photo.
3. **Arbeitszeugnisse** — work certificates from previous employers.
4. **Diplomas and certificates** — degree certificates, transcripts, professional qualifications.
5. **Language certificates** where relevant.
6. **References** — named contacts, with permission.

**Combine it into one clearly-named PDF** unless the portal asks for separate uploads, and order it as above. A recruiter opening a single well-ordered file thinks better of you than one opening seven attachments.

## The Arbeitszeugnis, explained

This is the document nobody outside the German-speaking world has heard of, and it matters here.

**What it is.** A formal written certificate issued by an employer when you leave, describing your role, your duties, your period of employment and an assessment of your performance and conduct. In Switzerland, Germany and Austria you are legally entitled to one.

**Why it matters.** Swiss recruiters expect to see them for every relevant previous role, and their absence raises a question.

**The coded language.** Arbeitszeugnisse are written in a conventionalised register where appa[rent](/blog/canada-major-city-job-markets)ly bland phrases carry specific meaning, because the document must be benevolent in tone but truthful in substance. Experienced Swiss recruiters read them fluently. If you receive one, it is worth having a native speaker read it before you file it.

**If you have never worked in a German-speaking country**, you will not have any, and that is understood. Provide reference letters from previous employers instead, and include named referees. What is not acceptable is providing nothing and offering no explanation.

## The CV itself

Two pages, factual, [reverse chronological](/blog/chronological-vs-functional-resume).

**Personal details** at the top: name, address, phone, email, date of birth, nationality, and — importantly for Switzerland — your **[work permit](/blog/canada-work-permit-types-guide) status**. Swiss employers screen on this early, and stating "EU citizen, no permit required" or your existing permit type is genuinely helpful.

**A photo is conventional.** A plain, professional headshot in the top corner. This differs from the UK, Ireland and increasingly the Netherlands, and following the local convention here is the right call.

**Work experience** with employer, location, title, dates and concrete responsibilities and results.

**Education** with institution, qualification, dates and grades. Swiss employers care about formal qualifications more than in some markets, so state them precisely and completely.

**Languages** with levels, using the CEFR scale. In a four-language country this is not a footnote — it is frequently the deciding criterion.

**Skills and IT competencies**, listed concretely.

**Do not use a [creative layout](/blog/resume-design-color-guide).** Swiss professional documents favour clarity and precision. A designed CV signals a mismatch with the culture rather than initiative.

## The cover letter

One page, formal, and specific.

**Address a named person** where you can find one. "Sehr geehrte Frau [Name]" or "Madame, Monsieur" if you genuinely cannot.

**Open with the role and where you saw it.** Swiss letters are direct about their purpose from the [first sentence](/blog/cover-letter-opening-lines-that-work).

**Middle paragraphs:** [why this company](/blog/how-to-answer-why-do-you-want-this-job), what you bring, and evidence for both. Connect specific experience to specific requirements from the advert.

**State your availability and permit position** explicitly — a start date and your right to work.

**Close formally.** "Mit freundlichen Grüssen" or "Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées."

**Write it in the language of the advert.** An English letter in response to a German advert signals that you did not read the requirements. If your German is not strong enough for a professional letter, that is information about whether the role is right for you.

## Getting foreign qualifications recognised

Switzerland places heavy weight on formal qualifications, and a foreign degree sometimes needs translating into Swiss terms before an employer can evaluate it.

**Swiss ENIC** is the national information centre for the recognition of foreign qualifications. It can issue a statement of comparability describing how your qualification relates to the Swiss system, which is genuinely useful when your degree title has no obvious local equivalent.

**Regulated professions are different.** Medicine, nursing, law, teaching, architecture and several engineering disciplines have formal recognition requirements administered by the relevant federal body. If your profession is regulated, recognition is not optional and it takes real time — start it early.

**For unregulated professions**, recognition is not legally required and an employer simply forms a judgement. A comparability statement helps them do that accurately, and one clarifying line on your CV describing the level and length of your degree helps almost as much.

**Include the transcript, not just the certificate.** Swiss employers read grades, and a transcript that shows what you actually studied answers questions a certificate alone does not.

## Applying, and following up

The submission mechanics are ordinary; the etiquette differs.

**Apply through the portal when there is one.** Swiss employers of any size use [applicant tracking system](/blog/ats-score-guide)s, and an email to a general address usually goes nowhere. Standard headings and a clean single PDF matter here as much as anywhere — an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) once removes the [parsing](/blog/ats-resume-parser-friendly-format) risk.

**Confirm receipt if you hear nothing after a week or two.** A single polite enquiry is fine.

**Then wait.** Swiss processes are slower than in the UK or US, and multiple rounds spread over several weeks is normal rather than a bad sign. Repeated chasing reads as impatience and it costs you.

**Prepare for a formal register.** Address people by title and surname unless invited otherwise, arrive early rather than on time, and expect structured, prepared questions rather than a conversation.

**[References are actually called](/blog/reference-check-process-guide).** Brief your referees, tell them which role and which company, and make sure the [contact details](/blog/resume-contact-section) are current. In markets where reference checks are a formality this feels excessive; in Switzerland it is not.

## Tailoring, and the details that get noticed

Swiss reviewers read carefully, which cuts both ways.

**Precision is the whole aesthetic.** Exact dates, exact titles, exact qualification names. An approximate date or a vague job title stands out in a document where everything else is specific.

**Explain gaps.** An unexplained gap in a Swiss CV attracts a question you will be asked anyway. One honest line is better than the question.

**Tailor the letter, not just the CV.** The motivation letter is where Swiss employers look for evidence that you understood the role, and a generic one undermines an otherwise strong dossier.

**Mirror the advert's own terminology.** If the posting names a specific standard, tool or [certification](/blog/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume), use that exact term where it is genuinely true of you.

**Check the file names.** "Muster_Anna_Lebenslauf.pdf" is professional; "cv_neu_final2.pdf" is not, and in a market that reads precision as competence this is not a small thing.

**[Proofread](/blog/resume-proofreading-checklist) in the target language.** A German or French letter with errors does more damage than an English one, because it invites doubt about the language level you claimed.

## The interview, and what the dossier has to survive

The dossier gets you into the room, and the room tests whether it was accurate.

**Expect your CV to be walked through line by line.** Swiss interviewers frequently go chronologically through the document asking about each entry. Anything vague, exaggerated or approximate is found here.

**Know your own dates.** It sounds trivial until an interviewer notices a two-month discrepancy between your CV and your certificate.

**Be ready to discuss your Arbeitszeugnisse.** If you have them, an interviewer may well have read the coded assessment and formed a view.

**Language will be tested, not just claimed.** If you wrote C1 German, part of the conversation will probably happen in German. This is the strongest argument for claiming the level you actually have.

**Prepare questions about the role and the company**, and make them substantive. Swiss interviewers read the absence of questions as an absence of interest.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have never worked in a German-speaking country

Include reference letters from previous employers and named referees instead, and add a line explaining that work certificates are not issued in your previous jurisdictions. Silence on the point is the only real mistake.

### Scenario 2: The advert is in German and your German is B1

Apply in German only if you can write a professional letter unaided. Otherwise focus on the English-language employers — a poorly written German letter does more damage than an English one to an English-speaking employer.

### Scenario 3: You are an EU citizen

Put it on the CV and in the cover letter. It is the single most reassuring thing a Swiss employer can read about an international applicant, and leaving it out costs you.

> **Tip:** Combine the whole dossier into one properly named PDF — "Kowalski_Anna_Bewerbung_DataAnalyst.pdf". Recruiters handling many applications remember the one that arrived as a single ordered document rather than seven loose attachments.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Send a complete dossier
- Include a professional photo
- State your permit status prominently
- Write in the language of the advert
- List languages with CEFR levels
- Combine everything into one named PDF

### Don't

- Send a CV alone
- Ignore the Arbeitszeugnis question in silence
- Use a creative or designed layout
- Write in English to a German-language advert
- Leave your work permit status unstated
- Omit grades and formal qualification details

## Common Mistakes

- **Sending only a CV.** Switzerland expects a full dossier and an incomplete one reads as careless.
- **Ignoring work certificates.** If you have none, explain why and substitute reference letters.
- **Omitting the photo.** It remains conventional here, unlike in the UK or Ireland.
- **Wrong language.** Answering a German advert in English signals you did not read it.
- **Creative formatting.** Swiss documents favour precision over personality.
- **Burying permit status.** It is the first thing screened, so put it at the top.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a Bewerbungsdossier?

The complete Swiss application package — cover letter, CV, work certificates, diplomas and references — submitted together rather than as a CV alone.

### What is an Arbeitszeugnis?

A formal work certificate issued by an employer describing your role, duties and performance. Standard in German-speaking countries, and Swiss recruiters expect to see them.

### What if I do not have work certificates?

Provide reference letters and named referees instead, with a brief line explaining that certificates are not issued in your previous jurisdictions. Explaining is essential; omitting silently is not.

### Should I include a photo?

Yes. A plain professional headshot remains conventional on Swiss CVs, unlike in the UK, Ireland and increasingly the Netherlands.

### Which language should I apply in?

The language of the advert. German, French, Italian or English — matching it is a requirement, not a preference.

### How long should a Swiss CV be?

Two pages. Precision and completeness matter more than brevity, but a third page is rarely justified for a graduate.
### Do I need my foreign degree recognised?

For unregulated professions, no — but a Swiss ENIC comparability statement helps employers evaluate it accurately. For regulated professions like medicine, nursing, law and teaching, recognition is required and takes time.

### Should I follow up on an application?

One polite enquiry after a week or two is fine. Beyond that, wait — Swiss processes are deliberately slow and repeated chasing reads as impatience.

### Do Swiss employers really check references?

Yes, genuinely. Brief your referees on the specific role and company, and make sure contact details are current.
