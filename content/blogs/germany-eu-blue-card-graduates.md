## Key Takeaways

- The **EU Blue Card (Blaue Karte EU)** is the main [residence permit](/blog/canada-work-permit-types-guide) for [university](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume)-qualified workers in Germany, and the whole thing turns on your salary meeting a published [threshold](/blog/resume-for-h1b-visa-sponsorship).
- **[Shortage occupations](/blog/in-demand-jobs-canada-2026)** — IT, engineering, mathematics, natural sciences, and human medicine among them — qualify at a **lower salary threshold** than other fields.
- Your degree must be **recognised or comparable**, which is what the anabin database determines.
- The Blue Card leads to **[permanent residence](/blog/canada-provincial-nominee-guide) faster than the standard route**, and faster still with strong German.
- Thresholds are revised annually — check the cur[rent](/blog/canada-major-city-job-markets) year's figures on the official source before planning around any number.

## What the Blue Card is

The Blue Card is an EU-wide residence title for highly qualified workers, implemented by each member state. In Germany it is the standard route for someone with a university degree and a graduate-level [job offer](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email).

Three conditions have to be met together:

1. **A recognised university degree**, or one assessed as comparable to a German qualification.
2. **A binding job offer or employment contract** in a role that matches your qualification.
3. **A gross annual salary meeting the threshold** for your category.

That third condition is where applications actually succeed or fail, and it is the one worth understanding in detail before you accept any offer.

## The two salary thresholds

Germany operates a general threshold and a lower one for shortage occupations.

| Category | Threshold | Which fields |
|---|---|---|
| **General** | The higher figure | Most professions |
| **Shortage occupations (Engpassberufe)** | A meaningfully lower figure | IT and ICT specialists, engineers, mathematicians, natural scientists, human medicine, and several others |
| **Recent graduates** | Reduced threshold available in the first years after graduating | Applies across categories, subject to current rules |

Both figures are set by regulation and **revised annually**, which is why no article should be your source for the actual number. The Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF) and the Make it in Germany portal publish the current figures, and they are the ones to check.

**What matters practically is the gap between the two.** A [software engineer](/blog/resume-matching-software-engineer) or a mechanical engineer clears the bar at a salary that would not be enough in a non-shortage field. If your degree is in one of those areas, a graduate-level offer will frequently qualify you where the same salary in another field would not.

## Why anabin comes first

Your degree has to be recognised, and **anabin** is the official database that records how foreign qualifications and institutions are classified.

Look up two things: your **institution** (is it recognised, and at what status) and your **degree** (is it assessed as comparable to a German qualification). The outcome determines whether the Blue Card route is open to you at all.

If your qualification is not listed or the classification is unclear, a formal individual assessment through the **Zentralstelle für ausländisches Bildungswesen (ZAB)** is the next step. It takes time, so start it early rather than after you have an offer waiting.

For **[regulated professions](/blog/canada-regulated-professions-guide)** — medicine, law, teaching, certain [engineering roles](/blog/how-to-get-first-tech-job) — recognition is a separate legal requirement on top of this, and it is a longer process.

## From Blue Card to permanent residence

This is the genuine advantage of the route, and the reason it is worth targeting rather than settling for a standard work permit.

| Route | Permanent residence after |
|---|---|
| Blue Card, with sufficient German (B1) | The shortest qualifying period |
| Blue Card, with basic German (A1) | A somewhat longer period |
| Standard work permit | Considerably longer |

The pattern is consistent even as the specific durations are adjusted by legislation: **the Blue Card shortens the path substantially, and German [language ability](/blog/bilingual-language-skills-on-resume) shortens it further.** Two people who arrive on the same day can reach permanent residence years apart based on which permit they hold and what German they have.

The practical read: if you intend to stay, learn German deliberately from the start rather than treating it as [opt](/blog/opt-cpt-resume-guide)ional. It affects your permit, your permanent residence timeline, and the size of your accessible job market simultaneously.

## Changing jobs on a Blue Card

Early in the permit, changing employer generally requires notifying or obtaining approval from the immigration authority — the Blue Card is tied to qualified employment, not to one specific company indefinitely. After an initial period this becomes more flexible.

Do not treat this as a reason to stay in a bad first job, but do check the current rules before resigning, because the sequencing matters.

## How the application actually runs

The sequence depends on whether you are already in Germany or applying from abroad, and the difference is significant.

**If you are already in Germany** — for example on a student residence permit after graduating — you generally apply to change status at your local **Ausländerbehörde** (immigration office). You do not have to leave and re-enter. Book the appointment early, because waiting times at busy city offices run into weeks or months.

**If you are applying from abroad**, you generally apply for a national visa (D visa) at the German mission in your country, then convert it to the Blue Card after arriving. The mission appointment is frequently the longest single delay in the whole process.

Either way the documents are broadly the same: passport, degree certificate with recognition evidence, the signed employment contract, a completed application, biometric photos, proof of [health insurance](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist), and the fee.

**Health insurance is not optional.** Germany requires everyone to hold it, and you will need proof at the application. Statutory (gesetzlich) cover through your employer is the normal route for an employee.

## Bringing family with you

This is one of the Blue Card's genuine strengths and it is frequently overlooked.

Spouses of Blue Card holders can generally join, and — unlike several other German residence routes — a spouse joining a Blue Card holder is typically **not required to demonstrate German language ability before arriving**. A joining spouse also generally receives unrestricted access to the labour market, meaning they can work without a separate job offer.

Children can join as dependents. For a couple weighing two offers in different countries, this provision is often worth more in practice than a difference in salary.

## If you lose your job

Worth knowing before it matters rather than during it.

Holding a Blue Card, losing employment does not end your right to stay immediately. There is a period in which you can look for new qualified employment, and you are generally required to **notify the Ausländerbehörde** that your employment has ended.

The important points: notify rather than stay silent, understand how long your specific permit allows, and remember that your next role also has to meet the Blue Card conditions — salary threshold included — for the permit to continue on the same basis. Speak to the immigration office or an adviser early rather than at the end of the window.

## Costs and timeline, realistically

Two things people underestimate: the fee is small, and the waiting is not.

The Blue Card application fee itself is modest — a two-figure sum rather than a serious expense. What costs is everything around it: a ZAB individual assessment if your degree needs one, [certified translations](/blog/canada-credential-assessment-guide) of your documents, and in some cases apostille or legalisation of your certificates in your home country.

The real cost is time. A German mission appointment abroad can take weeks to secure. A busy city Auslanderbehoerde appointment can take longer still. A ZAB assessment runs on its own schedule. None of these run in parallel with each other by default, so sequence them deliberately: start the degree recognition first, because everything else depends on its outcome.

Build the whole thing backwards from your intended start date and add margin. Employers who hire internationally are used to this and will generally accommodate a realistic start date, but only if you tell them early rather than a week before you were meant to begin.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have a computer science degree and an offer in Berlin

Check whether the role counts as a shortage occupation — IT specialists generally do — then compare the offer against the lower threshold. A graduate-level tech salary in Germany will frequently clear it.

### Scenario 2: Your offer is just below the threshold

This is a legitimate [negotiation](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) point, and employers who hire internationally understand it immediately. Raising a base salary by a modest amount to clear a visa threshold is a normal conversation, not an imposition.

### Scenario 3: Your degree is not clearly listed on anabin

Start a ZAB individual assessment before you go further. It takes real time, and discovering the problem after you have an offer with a start date is the expensive version of this.

> **Tip:** Check the shortage-occupation list before you check the salary. If your field is on it, you are comparing your offer against a materially lower threshold — and a great many graduates assume they fall short when they are actually being measured against the wrong number.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Check anabin before anything else
- Confirm whether your field is a shortage occupation
- Compare your offer against the current year's threshold
- Negotiate salary explicitly where you are close to the bar
- Start learning German for the permanent residence timeline
- Verify the current rules before changing employer

### Don't

- Rely on a threshold figure from an article or a forum post
- Assume your degree is automatically recognised
- Accept an offer below the threshold without raising it
- Treat German as optional if you plan to stay
- Change jobs without checking the notification requirement
- Leave a ZAB assessment until after you have an offer

## Common Mistakes

- **Using an out-of-date salary figure.** The thresholds are revised annually; check BAMF or Make it in Germany, not a blog post.
- **Not checking the shortage-occupation list.** It carries a lower threshold, and candidates routinely measure themselves against the wrong number.
- **Assuming recognition is automatic.** anabin decides how your degree is classified, and an unclear result needs a ZAB assessment that takes time.
- **Accepting an offer just under the bar.** Employers who hire internationally expect this conversation and can usually accommodate it.
- **Ignoring German.** It directly shortens the path to permanent residence, on top of widening your job market.
- **Switching employer without checking.** Early-stage Blue Card holders generally need approval, and sequencing a [resignation](/blog/how-to-write-two-weeks-notice) wrongly creates a real problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What salary do I need for a Blue Card?

There are two thresholds — a general one and a lower one for shortage occupations — and both are revised annually. Check the current figures on the BAMF or Make it in Germany site rather than relying on any article.

### Which jobs count as shortage occupations?

IT and ICT specialists, engineers, mathematicians, natural scientists and human medicine are the commonly cited categories. Confirm your specific occupation against the current official list.

### How do I know if my degree is recognised?

Search the anabin database for both your institution and your degree. If the classification is unclear, apply for an individual assessment through the ZAB.

### How fast does the Blue Card lead to permanent residence?

Faster than a standard work permit, and faster again if you hold B1 German rather than only A1. The exact periods are set by legislation and worth confirming currently.

### Can I change employer on a Blue Card?

Generally yes, but early in the permit it usually requires notifying or getting approval from the immigration authority. Check the current requirement before resigning.

### Is the Blue Card the only route?

No — the Opportunity Card is a job-seeker route for arriving before you have an offer, and standard work permits exist for roles that do not meet Blue Card conditions.
### Do I apply from Germany or from my home country?

If you are already in Germany on a student permit you can usually change status at your local Ausländerbehörde without leaving. From abroad, you apply for a national D visa at the German mission first.

### Can my spouse work if they join me?

Generally yes — spouses joining a Blue Card holder typically receive unrestricted labour market access, and usually are not required to prove German ability before arriving.

### What happens if I lose my job on a Blue Card?

You do not lose your right to stay immediately, but you must notify the Ausländerbehörde and find new qualified employment that also meets the Blue Card conditions within the permitted period.
