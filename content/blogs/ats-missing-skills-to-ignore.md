## Key Takeaways

- Scanners tokenise the entire posting - culture text, benefits, legal boilerplate and the company's own name - so a large share of any flagged list was never a requirement.
- Seven recognisable categories account for most false flags, and each takes a second to spot once you know them.
- Clustered alternatives like "Tableau, Power BI or Looker" almost always mean one of these, not all three.
- Acting on false flags is exactly how resumes become padded, and padding is visible to recruiters at a glance.
- After sorting, the genuinely actionable list is usually two or three items.

A scan does not know which parts of a job posting are requirements. It reads the whole page — the role description, the culture paragraph, the benefits, the equal-opportunity statement — and reports words that do not appear on your resume.

That is why "collaborative", "PTO" and the company's own name turn up as missing keywords.

Learning to discard these quickly is most of what makes a report usable. It takes about ninety seconds once you know the categories.

## Why Scanners Flag Things That Were Never Required

The tool has no model of what a job is. It extracts terms from one document, extracts terms from another, and reports the difference.

It cannot tell that "fast-paced environment" describes an office and "Kubernetes" describes a skill. Both are just tokens that appear in the posting and not in your resume.

This is worth understanding rather than just accepting, because it tells you what the report *is*: a diff, not a verdict. The [four-bucket method for reading a report](/blog/how-to-read-your-ats-report) builds on exactly this point.

## The Seven Categories

### 1. Culture and personality language

Fast-paced, dynamic, self-starter, team player, passionate, detail-oriented, go-getter, hungry, scrappy.

These describe a desired temperament, not a checkable skill. Adding "self-starter" to your resume satisfies nothing and costs you a line.

If you want to convey the trait, evidence it instead — a bullet showing you started something nobody asked you to start does more than the adjective ever will.

### 2. Company nouns

The employer's name, their product names, internal team names, office locations.

You cannot have prior experience with a company you have not worked at, and nobody expects you to. Ignore entirely.

### 3. Benefits and administrative text

Health insurance, 401(k), PTO, hybrid, equity, visa sponsorship, equal opportunity employer, pension, parental leave.

This is contract and compliance language that happened to sit on the same page as the requirements. It is never something to put on a resume.

### 4. Clustered alternatives

The posting lists Tableau, Power BI and Looker. Or AWS, Azure and GCP. Or Jira, Asana and Monday.

Several similar tools listed together almost always means **one of these is fine**. If you have one, that cluster is satisfied.

Listing all three because the scan flagged them is transparent padding, and it is the kind that gets probed in an interview — see [keyword stuffing versus coverage](/blog/keyword-stuffing-vs-coverage) for where that line sits.

### 5. Generic verbs and business nouns

Manage, collaborate, deliver, support, drive, execute, synergy, ownership, impact, stakeholder.

These appear in every posting and most resumes. If your resume describes real work, these concepts are already present in substance. Inserting the literal words adds nothing a reader would notice.

### 6. Seniority and framing words

Senior, junior, lead, principal, experienced, proven track record, results-driven.

These describe the level of the role, not a skill to list. Your seniority is communicated by your titles, scope and dates — which is why [how you present your job title](/blog/use-posting-job-title) matters more than any adjective.

### 7. Degree and certification names you do not hold

Flagged because the posting names them; not actionable because you either have the credential or you do not.

If you hold it, it should already be on the resume. If you do not, adding the name is a false claim about a verifiable fact — the easiest kind to get caught on, since [background checks confirm credentials routinely](/blog/tailoring-without-lying).

## The One-Second Test

For any flagged term, ask: **could a specific person's resume genuinely evidence this, and could they be asked about it in an interview?**

| Flagged term | Could it be evidenced? | Could you be asked about it? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kubernetes | Yes | Yes | Act on it if you have it |
| Fast-paced environment | No | No | Ignore |
| Stakeholder management | Yes | Yes | Act on it if you have it |
| 401(k) | No | No | Ignore |
| Acme Corp | No | No | Ignore |
| dbt | Yes | Yes | Act on it if you have it |
| Detail-oriented | No | No | Ignore |
| PMP certification | Yes | Yes | Only if you hold it |

The question filters almost everything correctly, at about a second per term.

## A Full Worked Sort

A posting for a mid-level data analyst produced nineteen flagged terms:

> SQL, Python, Tableau, Power BI, Looker, dbt, Snowflake, stakeholder management, A/B testing, fast-paced, self-starter, cross-functional, Acme Analytics, hybrid working, equity, detail-oriented, communication, Agile, bachelor's degree

Applying the categories:

| Terms | Category | Action |
|---|---|---|
| fast-paced, self-starter, detail-oriented | 1 — culture | Ignore |
| communication, cross-functional | 5 — generic | Ignore |
| Acme Analytics | 2 — company noun | Ignore |
| hybrid working, equity | 3 — benefits | Ignore |
| Tableau / Power BI / Looker | 4 — cluster | One suffices |
| bachelor's degree | 7 — credential | Have it or not |
| Agile | 5 — generic process | Usually ignore |
| **SQL, Python, dbt, Snowflake, stakeholder management, A/B testing** | **Real** | **Act on these** |

Nineteen became six. Of those six, the analyst already had four — described in different words. The actual editing job was three sentences.

## What Chasing the Full List Looks Like

The clearest way to see the cost is side by side. Same candidate, same posting, two responses to the same nineteen flagged terms.

> **Chasing every flag — skills section:**
>
> SQL, Python, R, Tableau, Power BI, Looker, dbt, Snowflake, Redshift, BigQuery, Airflow, Agile, Jira, A/B testing, experimentation, stakeholder management, cross-functional collaboration, communication, presentation skills, detail-oriented, self-starter, fast-paced environments

Twenty-two items. Roughly six are genuine strengths. A recruiter cannot tell which, so the whole list is discounted — and an interviewer picking a topic has a two-in-three chance of landing on something the candidate cannot discuss.

> **Sorted first — skills section:**
>
> **Data:** SQL (advanced), Python (pandas, scikit-learn), dbt, Snowflake
> **Analysis:** A/B testing, cohort analysis, experiment design
> **BI:** Tableau

Seven items, grouped, every one defensible under questioning. The cluster is satisfied by Tableau alone. The culture words are gone because they were never requirements.

The second version scores a few points lower and is unambiguously the stronger document — the same trade covered in [what counts as a good ATS score](/blog/good-ats-score-when-to-stop).

## What Acting on False Flags Costs You

Three things go wrong when you try to clear the whole list.

**Your skills section fills with terms you cannot discuss.** Interviewers select probe topics from that section precisely because it is your own claim about yourself. A list of twenty tools where you know eight well is an invitation to be asked about the other twelve.

**Your bullets acquire keyword clusters.** Parenthetical strings of synonyms read as obviously machine-fitted, and [recruiters spot generic resumes](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) in seconds.

**Your genuine strengths get diluted.** The two things you are excellent at now sit among fifteen things you have heard of. The reader remembers nothing from a list of thirty and something from a list of ten — which is why [where you place keywords](/blog/where-to-place-keywords-resume) matters more than how many you list.

The resume scores better and performs worse.

## How the Sort Changes by Career Stage

The seven categories are constant, but which flagged items matter shifts with experience. Applying the same rules blindly at every level is a common mistake.

### Freshers and first-job applicants

Your flagged list will be long, because postings describe an ideal candidate and you have less to match against. That is normal and not a signal to pad.

What genuinely matters at this stage is **named tools and named coursework**, because they are the only checkable evidence you have. What to ignore hardest: seniority words, "proven track record", and anything implying years of experience.

A fresher who lists eight tools they actually used in projects beats one who lists twenty-five they have heard of — the [ATS score for freshers](/blog/ats-score-for-freshers) guide covers what a realistic match looks like.

### Mid-career applicants

Here the clusters matter most. You have probably used one tool from each family, and the temptation to list all three is strongest because you have touched them all at some point.

The honest test is the two-minute one. "Touched it in a project two years ago" is not the same as "can discuss it under questioning."

### Senior and executive applicants

Flagged tool names matter least at this level and scope language matters most. A posting for a Head of Data that flags "Tableau" is not asking whether you build dashboards — it is describing the environment.

What to act on instead: budget, headcount, and P&L terms. What to ignore: individual tools, unless the posting names one in the title. [ATS mistakes experienced professionals make](/blog/ats-mistakes-experienced-professionals) covers this inversion in detail.

### Career changers

The hardest case, because genuine gaps and vocabulary gaps look identical on a flagged list.

Sort twice. First pass: which flagged terms describe work you have done under another name? Second pass: which describe the domain knowledge you genuinely lack? The first group is [adjacent-skill translation](/blog/adjacent-skills-resume-credit); the second is real, and it tells you how big the jump is.

## When a "False Flag" Is Actually Real

Three exceptions worth knowing, because the categories above are heuristics rather than rules.

**A soft skill named repeatedly and specifically.** If a posting says "stakeholder management" four times across the title, summary and bullets, it is a real requirement even though it sounds generic. Repetition beats category.

**A tool in the job title.** "Salesforce Administrator" means Salesforce is not a clusterable preference. Anything in the title is central.

**A named framework in a regulated field.** GDPR, HIPAA, SOC 2, IFRS. These are literal requirements, not categories, and knowing one does not substitute for another.

The general rule: **position and repetition override category.** A term in the title, repeated across sections, is real regardless of how generic it sounds.

## Three Situations That Look Like Noise and Are Not

Some flagged items sit in a grey zone where the category rules give the wrong answer. These three come up constantly.

### The soft skill that is actually the job

"Stakeholder management" reads like category 5 filler. But for a Business Partner or Programme Manager role it is the core function, not a descriptor.

The tell is placement: if it appears in the title or the first paragraph, it is the job. Category rules lose to position every time.

### The tool you used once, properly

You spent three weeks building something real in a tool you have not touched since. That is not padding — it is genuine, limited experience.

State it accurately rather than dropping it or inflating it: "built a reporting prototype in Looker (one project)" survives questioning in a way that a bare skills-list entry does not.

### The certification you are halfway through

Flagged as missing because you do not hold it. But "CKA, exam scheduled March" is honest, checkable, and tells the employer something useful about your direction.

The rule: in-progress is fine when stated as in-progress. It becomes a problem only when written to imply completion.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating the flagged list as a to-do list.** It is a diff between two documents. Diffs contain plenty of irrelevant differences.

**Adding culture words to the skills section.** "Team player" in a skills list is the clearest signal to a recruiter that the resume was written for a machine.

**Listing every tool in a cluster.** If the posting offers three options, having one is the requirement met.

**Ignoring the report entirely.** The opposite failure. The genuine items in it are valuable and take five minutes to address.

**Assuming a longer list means a worse match.** Long flagged lists usually mean a long posting, not a poor fit — and [job descriptions are often written aspirationally](/blog/read-a-jd-like-a-recruiter) by several contributors.

After ignoring the noise, pick three edits that matter - [turning a scan result into three edits](/blog/scan-result-into-three-edits) covers which three.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### If a term is flagged, doesn't that mean the employer wants it?

Not necessarily. The scan flags words present in the posting and absent from your resume, regardless of whether that word was a requirement, a benefit, or a description of office culture.

### How do I know whether clustered tools mean "one of these"?

Position and phrasing. Several similar tools listed adjacently without separate requirements attached almost always signals flexibility. If the posting says "must have Tableau" specifically, that is different.

### Should I add soft-skill keywords anywhere on my resume?

Better to evidence them than to list them. A bullet showing you resolved a conflict conveys more than the word "collaborative" in a skills line.

### What if ignoring these leaves my score lower?

It usually does, slightly. A moderately scoring honest resume outperforms a high-scoring padded one at the stage that decides your outcome, which is the human read. If your score falls after an edit, [there are six normal reasons](/blog/ats-score-dropped-after-edit) and most need no action.

### Is there any case for adding a term I barely know?

Only if you can genuinely discuss it for two minutes. If not, you are trading a marginal scoring gain for a visible interview risk. When a posting names something you genuinely lack, [there are four honest options](/blog/missing-required-skill-what-to-do) and padding is not among them.

### Why does the scan flag the company's own name?

Because it tokenises every word in the posting, including proper nouns. There is no way for you to have prior experience with the employer, so it is pure noise.

### Do all ATS checkers flag the same noise?

Broadly yes, though some filter culture language better than others. The genuine missing items tend to be consistent across tools even when the scores differ — a point covered in [why two checkers disagree](/blog/why-ats-checkers-disagree).

### How long should sorting a report take?

About ninety seconds once the categories are familiar. If you are spending twenty minutes on a flagged list, you are almost certainly acting on items you should be ignoring.

### Should I remove skills I have but the posting didn't mention?

For a specific application, trimming irrelevant items increases relevance density. Keep them in your master version and cut per application — that is standard [tailoring](/blog/what-to-change-when-tailoring), not deception.

## What to Do Next

Run the seven categories over your flagged list and delete the noise first. That alone usually halves it.

Then apply the one-second test to what remains, and sort into things you can honestly claim and things you cannot. The first group is your worklist — typically two or three items, each fixed by rewriting an existing sentence rather than adding anything new.

The second group is information about fit. If it is one item, apply anyway. If it is the central requirement repeated throughout the posting, spend your time on a better-matched role instead.

[Scan a resume against a posting](/ats-analysis) and try the sort on what comes back. The point is to arrive at a short, genuine list — not to empty the report.
