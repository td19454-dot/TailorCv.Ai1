One of the first questions foreigners face when applying for jobs in Japan is: *do I need a Japanese rirekisho, or will my regular resume do?* The two documents come from very different hiring philosophies. The **rirekisho** is a standardised, formal Japanese personal-history form built around completeness and conformity; the **Western resume** is a flexible marketing document built around standing out with achievements. Choosing the right one — and knowing when each is expected — can make or break your application in Japan.

This guide compares the rirekisho and the Western resume in 2026: their key differences, when to use each, and how foreigners should decide.

## The Two Philosophies

The difference isn't just formatting — it's philosophy:

- **The rirekisho** reflects a hiring culture that values conformity, completeness, diligence, and fit. It's a standardised form where everyone provides the same information in the same way; you demonstrate suitability by filling it out correctly, neatly, and completely.
- **The Western resume** reflects a hiring culture that values differentiation. It's a flexible marketing document where you choose what to highlight to stand out — leading with achievements and impact.

Understanding this explains every specific difference below.

## Side-by-Side: Key Differences

### Format and Structure

- **Rirekisho:** a standardised, fixed-layout form (template-based). Everyone's looks structurally similar.
- **Western resume:** flexible layout you design to present yourself best (within professional norms).

### Photo and Personal Details

- **Rirekisho:** includes a formal photo, date of birth, and other personal details in designated fields.
- **Western resume:** no photo, no age, no personal details — the focus is on skills and experience (and in many countries these are omitted to avoid bias).

### Content Emphasis

- **Rirekisho:** completeness — personal history, education, work history in prescribed format, qualifications, and a motivation section.
- **Western resume:** achievements — quantified accomplishments, impact, and skills tailored to the role.

### Work History Detail

- **Rirekisho:** a concise [chronological](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/chronological-vs-functional-resume) listing; detailed accomplishments go in the separate ***shokumu-keirekisho*** (work-history document).
- **Western resume:** the resume itself carries detailed achievement bullets.

### Tone and Differentiation

- **Rirekisho:** conformity and correctness; you fit the mould.
- **Western resume:** differentiation; you stand out.

### Screening

- **Rirekisho:** reviewed by people in a traditional process.
- **Western resume:** at global firms, often first screened by an **ATS** — see [ATS and English resumes for Japan](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ats-english-resume-japan).

## When to Use the Rirekisho

Use a rirekisho (with a *shokumu-keirekisho*) when applying to:

- **Traditional Japanese companies.**
- **Roles conducted in Japanese**, where Japanese-language documents are expected.
- **Any employer that specifically requests** these documents.

Get the details right — the photo, date format, neatness, and motivation section all matter. See the full [Japanese resume (rirekisho) guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japanese-resume-rirekisho-guide).

## When to Use a Western Resume

Use a Western-style resume when applying to:

- **Global companies, multinationals, and their regional offices** in Japan.
- **Tech firms, startups, and international teams** that hire in English.
- **English-language roles** generally.

These employers value a clear, achievement-focused, ATS-friendly resume. This is the route many foreigners take — see [working in Japan without Japanese](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/working-in-japan-without-japanese) and [how to find a job in Tokyo](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-find-a-job-in-tokyo).

## How Foreigners Should Decide

1. **Identify the employer type.** Traditional Japanese firm → rirekisho. Global/English-language role → Western resume. When unsure, check the posting or ask.
2. **Check the application instructions.** Many postings specify the format and language.
3. **Ask your recruiter.** Agencies that place foreigners will tell you exactly what each employer wants — and some help prepare both.
4. **Consider language.** Japanese-language roles expect Japanese documents; English roles accept English resumes.




Many foreigners primarily use a strong Western resume (for global/English roles) and prepare a rirekisho only when a traditional employer requires it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Sending a Western resume to a traditional firm** that expects a rirekisho (or vice versa).
- **Adding a photo to a Western resume** — standard on a rirekisho, but not on a Western/ATS resume.
- **A sloppy rirekisho** — neatness, correct dates, and a formal photo matter.
- **A generic Western resume** — tailor achievements and keywords to each role.
- **Not confirming the expected format** before applying.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between a rirekisho and a Western resume?**
The rirekisho is a standardised Japanese form emphasising completeness, conformity, and a photo; the Western resume is a flexible, achievement-focused marketing document with no photo. They reflect different hiring philosophies.

**Which one do I need for jobs in Japan?**
It depends on the employer. Traditional Japanese companies expect a rirekisho (plus a shokumu-keirekisho); global companies and English-language roles usually accept a Western resume.

**Can I use my Western resume for jobs in Japan?**
Yes — at global companies, tech firms, startups, and international roles. For traditional Japanese firms, prepare a rirekisho instead.

**Does a Western resume for Japan need a photo?**
No — unlike the rirekisho, a Western/ATS resume should not include a photo, age, or personal details.
