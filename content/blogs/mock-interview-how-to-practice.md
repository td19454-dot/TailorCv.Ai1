## Key Takeaways

- Speaking answers aloud is a separate skill from knowing them, and only the first one is tested.
- Practice only works if it is uncomfortable — same-length, unpaused, with someone who will push back.
- Recording yourself is the highest-return practice available and the one people avoid.
- Six prepared stories cover most behavioural questions, so practise adapting rather than memorising.
- The review afterwards matters more than the session itself.

Most people prepare for interviews by reading their answers, thinking through their examples, and feeling reasonably ready. Then the actual interview happens and the answers come out disorganised, too long, and missing the point they had clearly in mind.

That gap is entirely predictable. Knowing an answer and delivering it under observation are different skills, and only the second one is being assessed.

This guide covers how to practise so the gap closes.

## Why reading answers does not work

**You skip the hard part silently.** Reading through an example in your head, you glide over the section you have not actually worked out. Speaking it aloud, you stop.

**Timing is invisible on paper.** An answer that feels concise in your head frequently runs four minutes out loud.

**Structure collapses under pressure.** The organised version exists when you are calm; the interview version is what emerges when you are not.

**There is no follow-up.** Real interviews probe. Your own review does not ask "what would you do differently?"

**The fix is not more preparation.** It is different preparation — out loud, timed, observed.

## Recording yourself

The highest-return practice available, and the one people avoid because watching it back is unpleasant.

**How:** pick a question, record two to three minutes on your phone, then watch it.

**What you will notice immediately** — and could not have noticed otherwise:

- Filler words you had no idea you used
- Sentences that trail off unfinished
- The point arriving ninety seconds late
- Where you lost your own thread
- Whether you look at the camera or away

**The discomfort is the mechanism.** You cannot fix what you have never heard yourself do.

**Do two a day for a week** and the improvement is substantial. This works whether or not you can find a practice partner, which makes it the foundation.

## Practising with someone

**A peer.** Alternate roles — being the interviewer is unexpectedly instructive, because you hear what a weak answer sounds like from the other side.

**Someone senior in your field.** A former colleague or a senior who was hired where you are applying. They ask better follow-ups and their feedback is calibrated.

**Someone outside your field.** Useful specifically for checking whether you can explain your work to a non-expert, which is exactly what a non-technical interviewer or HR round requires.

**Ask them to push back.** The most common failure in peer practice is politeness — a partner who accepts every answer is not simulating anything. Ask explicitly for follow-up questions: *"what would you do differently", "how did they react", "how did you know it worked"*.

## Running a session properly

**Simulate, do not discuss.**

- **Same length** as the real thing
- **No pausing** to restart an answer
- **Video on** if the real interview will be
- **Dressed appropriately** if it is a video round — it changes how you carry yourself
- **Phone away**
- **Answers spoken in full**, not summarised as "and then I'd talk about the migration"

**Do not stop to discuss mid-session.** Take notes and review at the end, because interruption removes exactly the pressure you are trying to practise under.

## What to practise

**Six behavioural stories** covering conflict, failure, influence without authority, ambiguity, pressure, and technical depth. Most questions map onto these, so practise **adapting** them to different phrasings rather than memorising answers to specific questions — see [the US behavioral interview STAR guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/us-behavioral-interview-star-guide).

**Your introduction.** "Tell me about yourself" opens most interviews and sets the direction of everything after it. Practise it until it runs sixty to ninety seconds naturally.

**Your project explanation**, in a two-minute and a ten-minute version. Different interviewers want different depth — see [campus placement technical interview questions](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/campus-placement-technical-interview-questions).

**Technical problems out loud.** Narrating while solving is a genuinely separate skill from solving — see [the US tech interview process guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/us-tech-interview-process-guide).

**Your questions for them**, which are part of the assessment.

## The review

Where the improvement actually happens.

**For each answer, check:**

- **Length** — was it under three minutes?
- **Structure** — was there a clear beginning, middle and result?
- **"I" or "we"** — did you describe your own contribution?
- **A number** — was there a quantified outcome?
- **The point** — did it arrive in the first thirty seconds?

**Fix one thing at a time.** Trying to correct five habits simultaneously fixes none. Pick the worst, work on it until it improves, then move to the next.

**Keep the stories that work.** Once an answer runs cleanly, stop rewriting it and practise delivery instead.

## Common Mistakes

**Reading answers instead of speaking them.** Only the spoken version is tested, and it is markedly harder than the version in your head.

**Never recording yourself.** It is the only way to hear the filler words, trailing sentences and late-arriving points that you cannot detect from the inside.

**Practising with someone too polite to push back.** A partner who accepts every answer is not simulating an interview.

**Stopping mid-answer to restart.** Real interviews do not allow it, and practising with restarts trains the wrong habit.

**Memorising answers to specific questions.** You cannot anticipate exact wording — practise adapting six stories instead.

**Discussing during the session rather than after.** Interruption removes the pressure you are practising under.

**Trying to fix everything at once.** Correcting one habit at a time works; correcting five works on none.

**Practising only technical questions.** Behavioural rounds eliminate technically strong candidates routinely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many mock interviews should I do?

Enough that speaking your core answers feels routine — commonly one or two full sessions a week during an active search, plus daily short recorded practice.

### Is recording myself really necessary?

It is the single highest-return practice available. Filler words, trailing sentences and late-arriving points are invisible from the inside and obvious on playback.

### Who should I practise with?

A peer for volume, someone senior in your field for calibrated feedback, and someone outside your field to check you can explain your work to a non-expert.

### What if I have nobody to practise with?

Recording yourself covers most of the value. It requires no partner and addresses the delivery problems that matter most.

### Should I memorise my answers?

No. Prepare six stories and practise adapting them, because memorised answers sound recited and collapse when the question is phrased unexpectedly.

### How long should answers be?

Two to three minutes for behavioural questions, sixty to ninety seconds for your introduction. Anything over four minutes is losing the room.

### What should I review afterwards?

Length, structure, whether you said "I" rather than "we", whether there was a number in the result, and whether the point arrived in the first thirty seconds.

## Make This Practical

Record yourself answering one question a day for a week, and watch each one back. It is uncomfortable, it requires no partner, and it is the fastest way to discover the filler words, unfinished sentences and late-arriving points that you cannot hear from inside your own head.

Then run full sessions properly rather than comfortably. Same length, no pausing, video on if the real round is, answers spoken in full, and no discussion until the end — and ask your partner explicitly to push back with follow-up questions rather than accepting what you say.

Finally, review against five specific checks — length, structure, "I" versus "we", a number in the result, and whether the point arrived in the first thirty seconds — then fix one habit at a time rather than all of them at once.
