## Key Takeaways

- UK employees have a statutory right to *request* flexible working from day one, though the employer can refuse on specified business grounds.
- Most UK roles advertised as "remote" are actually hybrid, and the advert frequently does not say how many office days.
- Remote postings attract far more applications than office roles, so referrals matter more.
- Working abroad while employed by a UK company creates tax and legal problems and is usually prohibited.
- Get the arrangement written into your contract, because informal agreements evaporate when managers change.

Remote working in the UK settled into a pattern that adverts describe badly. A posting saying "remote" may mean fully distributed, or two days in an office you have to live near, or "remote for now" pending a policy change nobody has announced yet.

Reading those adverts accurately, and knowing what you can actually request, is most of the game.

## The right to request flexible working

UK employees have a **statutory right to request flexible working**, and following reforms this became a **day-one right** rather than requiring six months' service.

**What the right actually is:** the right to *ask* and to have the request considered properly. Not a right to be granted it.

**How it works:**

- You make a written request
- The employer must deal with it in a reasonable manner and respond within a statutory timeframe
- They must consult with you before refusing
- Refusal must be on one of the specified business grounds — cost, effect on quality or performance, inability to reorganise work or recruit, detrimental effect on meeting customer demand, insufficient work during proposed periods, or planned structural change

**Flexible working means more than location.** It includes hours, days worked, and patterns — compressed hours, term-time working, job shares.

**Practical advice:** frame a request around how the work gets done rather than around your preference. Addressing the business grounds pre-emptively — how you will maintain availability, collaboration and output — makes refusal harder to justify.

## Reading UK remote adverts

**"Remote"** — sometimes fully remote, frequently hybrid. Ask.

**"Hybrid"** — office attendance expected. The advert often omits the number of days, which is the single most important detail. Ask in the first call.

**"Remote-first"** — the company is genuinely built distributed. Meetings assume video, decisions are written down. This is the version that works long term.

**"Flexible"** — usually means flexible hours rather than location.

**"Remote (UK)"** — you must be UK-based and usually UK tax resident.

**The question to ask the recruiter:** *"How many days in the office, at which site, and is that a formal policy or team discretion?"* A vague answer is itself informative — it usually means the policy is under review.

## Pay and location

**Some UK employers adjust pay by location**, though it is less systematic than in the US. London-weighted roles sometimes reduce the weighting for remote workers living elsewhere.

**Ask before relocating.** Moving from London to a cheaper area while working remotely can trigger a pay review at some employers. Get the policy in writing first.

**The disposable income calculation** matters more than the headline. ## Working abroad

Usually not permitted, and the reasons are not arbitrary.

**Tax residency.** Working from another country can create tax liability there for you and potentially for your employer.

**Permanent establishment risk.** Your presence abroad can create a taxable corporate presence for your employer in that country — a serious exposure most employers will not accept.

**Employment law.** You may acquire rights under the local jurisdiction.

**Right to work.** You need permission to work in the country you are physically in, regardless of who employs you.

**Some employers permit short periods abroad** under a formal policy with defined limits. Ask rather than assume, and never do it quietly — it is the kind of thing that ends employment when discovered.

## Before you accept

**Get it in the contract.** A verbal agreement with a manager who later leaves is worth nothing. The written contract is what survives a reorganisation.

**Confirm the office-day expectation** and whether it can change.

**Check the location requirement** — some roles require you within commuting distance of a site even if mostly remote.

**Ask about equipment and expenses.** Who provides the laptop and monitor, and is there a home working allowance.

**Ask how many people on the team are fully remote.** One remote person on an office-based team has a different experience from a distributed team — and it affects progression, as [the first 90 days in a new job](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/first-90-days-new-job) touches on.

## Common Mistakes

**Assuming "remote" means fully remote.** Many UK adverts use it loosely for hybrid, and the number of office days is frequently omitted entirely.

**Not asking how many office days.** It is the most important practical detail and the one most adverts leave out.

**Relying on a verbal arrangement.** Managers change and policies shift; only the written contract survives.

**Believing the right to request is a right to have.** The employer can refuse on specified business grounds — the right is to a proper process.

**Framing a flexible working request around preference.** Addressing the business grounds directly makes refusal harder to justify.

**Working abroad without permission.** It creates tax and permanent establishment exposure for your employer and is usually a dismissal matter.

**Relocating without checking the pay policy.** Some employers reduce London weighting for remote workers who move, and asking afterwards is too late.

**Relying on cold applications.** Remote postings draw a national field, so referrals matter more here than in local hiring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have a right to work from home in the UK?

You have a statutory right to *request* flexible working from day one, and to have the request handled properly. The employer can refuse on specified business grounds.

### What counts as flexible working?

More than location — hours, days, compressed weeks, term-time working and job shares all count.

### Does "remote" mean fully remote in UK adverts?

Often not. Many roles advertised as remote are hybrid, and the advert frequently omits the office-day expectation. Ask in the first call.

### Can I work abroad for a UK employer?

Usually not. It creates tax residency, permanent establishment and employment law exposure. Some employers permit short defined periods under a formal policy.

### Will my pay drop if I move somewhere cheaper?

Possibly. Some employers adjust London weighting for remote workers who relocate. Confirm the policy in writing before moving.

### How do I get remote working written into my contract?

Ask for it explicitly at offer stage, before accepting. Verbal agreements do not survive management or policy changes.

### Are remote jobs harder to get in the UK?

More competitive, since the candidate pool is national rather than local. Referrals and specific targeting matter correspondingly more.

## Make This Practical

Ask one question in the first recruiter call: how many days in the office, at which site, and is that a formal policy or team discretion. The advert usually omits it, it is the detail that defines the job, and a vague answer tells you the policy is under review.

Then get the arrangement written into the contract at offer stage rather than agreeing it verbally with a manager. Contracts survive reorganisations and manager changes; informal understandings do not.

Finally, use the right to request properly if you are already employed. Frame it around how the work gets done — availability, collaboration, output — and address the statutory business grounds directly, because a request that pre-empts the grounds for refusal is considerably harder to refuse.
