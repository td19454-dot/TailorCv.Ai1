## The unfamiliarity problem

This is the specific disadvantage international candidates carry, and it is rarely named directly.

**Your [university](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume) is unknown.** A [recruiter](/blog/how-recruiters-read-resumes) in [Dublin](/blog/ireland-tech-jobs-dublin) or Berlin may have no calibration for an institution that is genuinely excellent in its own country. They cannot tell whether your degree is competitive or ordinary.

**Your [employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) are unknown.** "Senior Analyst at a 4,000-person firm" means something to people at home and nothing abroad.

**Your [references](/blog/resume-references-guide) are unverifiable.** A referee in another country, another time zone, another language is a call most recruiters will not make.

**Your grading system is unfamiliar.** A first, a 3.8, a 1.3, an 8.5 out of 10 — these do not translate cleanly, and a reader who has to work it out often does not.

**The consequence:** everything on your [CV](/blog/resume-optimization-guide) requires the reader to extend trust they have no basis for. A candidate from a local university with local employers requires none of that.

**A [portfolio](/blog/portfolio-from-resume-minutes) inverts it.** Work someone can look at needs no calibration. A working [application](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day), a written analysis, a designed interface, a documented project — the reader evaluates the thing directly, and their unfamiliarity with your background stops mattering.

## What belongs on it

Not everything. Three or four genuinely good pieces beat twelve mediocre ones.

**Projects with the problem stated.** Not "built a web app" but what problem, what constraint, what you chose and why. The reasoning is what demonstrates capability; the artefact alone does not.

**[Something that runs](/blog/portfolio-website-for-job-applications).** A live demo, a deployed page, an interactive notebook. The single biggest advantage of a portfolio over a [repository](/blog/github-profile-for-job-search-2026) is that nobody has to clone anything.

**Your thesis or major project, described as work.** This is usually the most substantial thing a [graduate](/blog/first-resume-for-gen-z-2026) has done and it is usually described in one line on a CV.

**Writing.** Even one clear piece of writing about a technical decision demonstrates communication in a way no [bullet point](/blog/how-to-write-resume-bullet-points) does.

**A short about section** that says what you are looking for and what your work authorisation situation is. Recruiters land here from a CV, and answering their next question saves them asking it.

**Your CV as a downloadable file**, so the page is a complete package rather than a detour.

## Choosing a theme that fits your field

The look is not decoration — it is a signal about what kind of work you do.

**Software and infrastructure.** The terminal, console and code-editor themes read as native to the field and communicate competence before anyone reads a word.

**Data and research.** Clean, structured, document-oriented themes let charts and written analysis carry the page.

**Design and creative.** Magazine and brutalist styles give the work room and demonstrate visual judgement by existing.

**Product, business and general graduate roles.** Conventional and clean. A portfolio for a business role should be readable by someone in a hurry, not a demonstration of front-end skill.

**Engineering outside software.** Structured and technical, with room for diagrams and project documentation.

**The general rule:** pick something that a person in your target field would find unremarkable in a good way. A wildly styled portfolio for a corporate [finance](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs) role signals a mismatch, and a plain one for a creative role signals a lack of judgement.

**Then leave it alone.** Iterating on the theme is a very effective way to avoid writing the [project descriptions](/blog/portfolio-case-study-writing), which is the part that actually matters.

## Keeping it alive

A stale portfolio is worse than none, because it dates you precisely.

**Add one project per term or per quarter.** Not everything — one thing you are actually proud of.

**Remove old work.** The project from three years ago that you would now do differently is not evidence of growth to a stranger; it is just your weakest piece.

**Update the about section** when your situation changes, especially your work authorisation and what you are looking for.

**Check the links.** Dead demo links are the most common portfolio failure, and they are worse than not linking at all.

**Check it on a phone.** A surprising share of first views are on mobile, often by a recruiter between meetings.

**And put the URL in your email signature.** It is the highest-click placement available and almost nobody uses it.

## Writing project descriptions that work

This is the part people skip and it is the part that does the work.

**Start with the problem, not the technology.** "Course timetables were published as PDFs that nobody could search" is a better opening than "a Flask app with a Postgres backend".

**State the constraint.** No budget, two weeks, no access to real data, a legacy system that could not be changed. Constraints are what make a decision interesting.

**Explain one decision and its alternative.** "I used a scheduled batch job rather than a queue because the data only changed daily and the operational cost of a queue was not justified." This single sentence demonstrates more engineering judgement than a list of technologies.

**Say what happened.** Users, accuracy, time saved, what broke. Including what did not work is more credible, not less.

**Then list the stack**, briefly, at the end. It matters for keyword matching and it is the least interesting part for a human.

**Four paragraphs is plenty.** A reader deciding whether to interview you will give each project about ninety seconds.

## Common portfolio failures

Six that recur, all avoidable.

**A wall of repositories.** Links without context ask the reader to do your work. Nobody will.

**Demos that do not load.** A free-tier server that sleeps, an expired certificate, a dead API key. Check quarterly.

**No indication of what you want.** A portfolio with no statement of the roles you are seeking leaves the reader to guess.

**Design over substance.** A beautifully animated page with three sentences of content signals that the presentation was the project.

**Group projects with no attribution.** State clearly what you personally did. Ambiguity here is noticed and it is read badly.

**Never linking to it.** By far the most common failure. A portfolio that is not on your CV, your LinkedIn and your email signature might as well not exist.

## Using it in an application

A portfolio only helps if it is doing work in your process.

**Put the URL in the CV header**, next to your email. Not buried in a projects section on page two.

**Reference one specific page in your cover letter.** "The scheduling project on my site is the closest thing I have done to what this role describes" gives the reader a reason to click and tells them what they will find.

**Link it in the application form** wherever there is a website or portfolio field. A surprising number of forms have one and it goes unfilled.

**Mention it in the interview.** "I can show you" is a strong sentence, and having something to actually show changes the shape of a conversation.

**Track whether it is being visited.** If nobody clicks, the placement is wrong. If people click and nothing follows, the content is.

**And keep the top of the page useful.** Reviewers arrive with a specific question — can this person do the job — and the first screen should start answering it rather than showing a full-height hero image.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Your university is unknown abroad

This is exactly the case a portfolio solves. A reviewer who cannot calibrate your institution can absolutely evaluate a working project, and that evaluation does not depend on knowing anything about your country.

### Scenario 2: You have no work experience yet

Then your projects are your evidence, and describing them properly is the highest-return work available to you. A well-documented [university project](/blog/projects-in-resume) outranks a bare list of coursework by a wide margin.

### Scenario 3: You need something today

Generate one from your resume, pick a theme, publish, and put the URL on your CV this afternoon. A good page that exists beats a perfect one you will build next month.

> **Tip:** Put the URL in your email signature, not just on your CV. Every message to a recruiter then carries a link to your actual work, and people click it far more often from an email than from a PDF.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Show three or four strong projects, not twelve
- Explain the problem and your decisions
- Include something that actually runs
- State your work authorisation
- Put the URL everywhere
- Check it on a phone

### Don't

- List every project you have ever touched
- Link repositories with no explanation
- Bury your thesis in one line
- Leave visa status unstated
- Build it for a weekend instead of publishing today
- Assume recruiters will clone anything

## Common Mistakes

- **Too many projects.** Three excellent ones beat twelve that need excuses.
- **Artefact without reasoning.** The decisions are what demonstrate capability.
- **Nothing that runs.** Reviewers will not clone a repository.
- **No work authorisation line.** It is the reader's next question.
- **Never sharing the URL.** A portfolio nobody visits does nothing.
- **Waiting for perfect.** A published good page beats an unbuilt great one.
