## Key Takeaways

- One-way recorded interviews sit between the tests and the assessment centre, and they are a real cut, not a formality.
- You usually get limited preparation time per question and a small number of takes, sometimes only one.
- Reviewers watch many of these in sequence, so the first fifteen seconds of each answer matter disproportionately.
- Technical setup — light, sound, framing, connection — is the most avoidable category of failure.
- Practise aloud with a timer. Answers that read well silently run long when spoken.

## What they are

You receive a link with a deadline. Questions appear one at a time. For each, you get a short window to prepare — often 30 seconds — and a fixed time to answer, usually one to three minutes. Some platforms allow a retake or two; many allow none.

Nobody is on the other end. You are recording into a system, and a reviewer watches later, sometimes at speed.

They sit after [psychometric tests](/blog/uk-psychometric-tests-guide) and before the [assessment centre](/blog/uk-assessment-centre-guide) in most graduate scheme processes.

## How they are actually reviewed

Understanding the reviewer's experience tells you how to answer.

They are watching many recordings in a session, often with the ability to skip forward. They are scoring against the same competency framework used later in the process. They are frequently looking for reasons to stop watching.

Three consequences:

**Front-load your answer.** Start with the point, then support it. A thirty-second preamble before you say anything substantive risks the reviewer forming a judgement before you get there.

**Keep to the time.** Being cut off mid-sentence looks worse than finishing slightly early. Aim to land comfortably inside the limit.

**Be clear rather than clever.** Complex, winding answers do not survive being watched at pace.

## The structure that works with limited prep time

Thirty seconds is not long enough to plan properly, so have a default shape ready before you start.

For competency questions — "tell us about a time when" — use a compressed STAR:

- **One sentence** of situation
- **One sentence** of what you specifically were responsible for
- **The bulk** on what you did
- **One clear sentence** on the result

For motivational questions — "why this firm", "why this scheme" — use: what specifically attracted you, evidence you have engaged with it, what you would bring.

Having the shape pre-loaded means the 30 seconds is spent choosing which story to use, not deciding how to structure it.

## Preparing the content

The questions are rarely surprising. Prepare six to eight stories in advance, as you would for any [competency interview](/blog/uk-interview-tips), and expect:

- Why this firm and this scheme
- A time you worked in a team
- A time you overcame a difficulty
- A time you led something
- A time you managed competing deadlines
- Something about the sector or a recent development in it

**Research the firm's competencies.** Many publish their graduate values. Preparing one story that clearly demonstrates each is the highest-return preparation, because the reviewer is scoring against exactly that list.

**Practise aloud, timed.** This is not optional. An answer that reads well on paper routinely runs to three minutes when spoken. Recording yourself once — uncomfortable as it is — reveals filler words, rambling and missing results faster than anything else.

## The setup

The most avoidable failures are technical.

**Light on your face, not behind you.** A window behind you turns you into a silhouette. Face the light source.

**Camera at eye level.** A laptop on a desk films up your nose. Raise it on books.

**Look at the lens, not the screen.** Looking at your own image reads as avoiding eye contact. Some people put a small arrow by the camera as a reminder.

**Sound matters more than picture.** Quiet room, door closed, notifications off, phone silent. Wired headphones with a mic usually beat laptop audio.

**Plain background.** Tidy and neutral. A blurred background is fine if the platform supports it.

**Test the platform first.** Most provide a practice question. Use it — the first time you see the interface should not be the scored attempt.

**Check your connection**, and have a backup, such as a phone hotspot.

**Dress as you would for an in-person interview.** Business formal for finance and law, business casual for most else. It also changes how you speak.

## Practicalities

- **Do it early in the window.** Technical problems are recoverable if you have days left, not if you have an hour.
- **Check how many takes you get.** Some platforms allow one. Knowing before you begin changes how you approach the first question.
- **Have water nearby.** Sessions run longer than expected.
- **Have notes off-camera if permitted** — a few bullet prompts, not a script. Reading is obvious on video.
- **Do not do it on a phone** unless there is no alternative. Framing and stability are worse.

## Why candidates underperform here

Two reasons, both fixable.

**No audience.** Speaking to a lens with no reaction is unnatural, and people either flatten out or speed up. Practising aloud is the only fix.

**Treating it as a formality.** Because there is no human present, candidates prepare less than for a real interview. But it is a genuine cut — often removing a large share of remaining applicants — and it is scored against the same framework.

Everything before this stage — application form, CV, tests — got you here. [UK CV format](/blog/uk-cv-format-guide-2026) and passing the [ATS screen](/ats-analysis) are what earn the invitation; this is where a lot of otherwise-strong candidates stop.

## Common Mistakes

- **Backlighting.** The most common setup error and it makes you unwatchable.
- **Reading from a script.** Obvious on camera and it kills delivery.
- **Long preambles.** Reviewers watching at pace form judgements early.
- **Running out of time mid-sentence.** Looks worse than finishing early.
- **Leaving it to the deadline.** Removes any margin for technical failure.
- **Not practising aloud.** The gap between reading and speaking an answer is large.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I redo an answer?

Depends on the platform — some allow one or two retakes, many allow none. Check before you begin, because it changes how you approach the first question.

### Is anyone watching live?

No. It is recorded and reviewed later, sometimes by more than one person and sometimes at speed.

### How long are the answers?

Usually one to three minutes, with around 30 seconds to prepare. The platform states the limits before each question.

### Can I use notes?

Usually permitted, but keep them to brief prompts placed near the camera. Reading is visible and undermines you.

### What if my connection fails partway?

Contact the employer's recruitment team promptly. Genuine technical failures are usually accommodated — which is another reason not to leave it until the deadline.

### Do these actually matter?

Yes. They are a substantial cut in most graduate scheme processes and are scored against the same competencies as later rounds. Preparing for them as you would a live interview is the right calibration.
