## Key Takeaways

- Language requirement varies more by employer type than by industry, and it determines which companies are genuinely open to you.
- Traditional Japanese companies hiring generalists usually expect business-level Japanese, because the entire process and the job run in Japanese.
- Foreign-affiliated firms, some technology companies and specific specialist roles operate substantially in English.
- Business Japanese is a different skill from conversational Japanese, involving formal register and workplace conventions.
- Honest self-assessment matters more than an optimistic one, because a mismatch surfaces immediately in the first interview.

## Why this determines everything else

For a foreign graduate, language ability is the constraint that shapes the whole search. It is not one factor among many — it decides which of the routes described across these guides are actually available to you.

**The [shūkatsu system](/blog/japan-new-grad-shinsotsu-hiring-explained) runs in Japanese.** Entry sheets, aptitude tests, group discussions and multiple [interview](/blog/japan-mensetsu-interview-questions-guide) rounds. A candidate without business-level Japanese cannot realistically compete in the conventional generalist track at a traditional Japanese company, regardless of ability.

**Which does not close Japan to you.** It redirects you toward employer types where the working language differs, and there are more of those than students assume.

**The honest framing:** rather than asking whether you can work in Japan without Japanese, ask which employers work in which language, and apply where your actual level fits.

## What the levels mean in practice

**Conversational.** You can manage daily life, hold social conversations and handle routine situations. Genuinely useful and generally not sufficient for a Japanese-language workplace.

**Business level.** Reading and writing formal documents and emails, participating in meetings, handling the layered politeness register that Japanese business communication requires, and managing nuance under pressure. This is a substantially higher bar than conversational fluency and it is what "business Japanese" means when an employer states it.

**Native or near-native.** Expected for some client-facing, legal, and communications-heavy roles.

**The gap that catches people out.** Someone comfortable in daily conversation frequently discovers that a business meeting, a formal email chain and a keigo-heavy client interaction are a different skill entirely. This gap is real and it is the reason a self-assessment based on social fluency misleads.

## How the requirement varies by employer

**Traditional Japanese companies, generalist track.** Business-level Japanese generally expected, because the recruiting process itself and the work are conducted in Japanese.

**Traditional Japanese companies, technical roles.** Sometimes more flexible, particularly in engineering and research where the technical work dominates, though internal communication remains Japanese.

**Japanese [technology companies](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates).** Varies widely. Some operate substantially in English, particularly those with international engineering teams. Others do not. Ask directly rather than assuming — the [technology hiring guide](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates) covers the spread.

**Foreign-affiliated companies.** Frequently operate in English or bilingually, and this is the largest single category of English-conducted professional work in Japan. Covered in the [English-speaking jobs guide](/blog/japan-english-speaking-jobs-guide).

**Startups.** Extremely variable, and some are deliberately English-operating.

**Teaching and education.** English-language teaching roles obviously require English rather than Japanese, with Japanese useful for daily life and for some school communication — see the [teaching guide](/blog/japan-jet-programme-and-teaching-jobs).

**Trading companies and international business roles.** Frequently want both, since the value of the hire is bilingual capability.

## Qualifications and what they signal

**The [JLPT](/blog/japan-jlpt-n2-n1-for-employment) is the standard reference point.** Employers state levels in job postings and it is the shorthand the market uses. The [level guide](/blog/japan-jlpt-n2-n1-for-employment) covers what each level means for employment specifically.

**A certificate is a signal, not the skill itself.** The test does not assess speaking, which means a certificate and workplace communication ability can diverge in either direction. Employers know this, which is why interviews test the reality.

**Business Japanese qualifications** exist separately and are more directly relevant to workplace communication for some employers.

**List your level prominently.** For a foreign applicant it is frequently the single most consequential line on your [rirekisho](/blog/japan-resume-rirekisho-guide) and your [work history document](/blog/japan-shokumu-keirekisho-guide).

**Do not overstate it.** The first interview reveals your actual level immediately, and a mismatch between your stated qualification and your demonstrated ability damages credibility more than a lower honest level would.

## Improving it deliberately

**Target business register specifically.** General study builds conversational ability; workplace communication needs formal register, email conventions and the politeness levels business interaction requires.

**Practise the job-hunting vocabulary.** The [shūkatsu process](/blog/japan-job-hunting-shukatsu-timeline) has its own conventional language, and being unfamiliar with it is conspicuous.

**Do mock interviews in Japanese.** The single highest-return practice, because the gap between reading ability and speaking under pressure is where candidates actually fail.

**Get your written documents checked by a native speaker.** Set phrases and formal register are conventional, and errors in them are visible.

**Use the time before graduation.** Language ability improves on a timescale of years rather than months, which is why students who decide in their final semester that they want to work in Japan face a harder problem than those who decided earlier.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming conversational fluency is enough.** Business Japanese is a distinct and higher bar.
- **Overstating your level on applications.** It surfaces in the first interview.
- **Assuming no Japanese means no Japan.** A substantial English-conducted market exists.
- **Not asking employers directly what language the role operates in.** The answer varies enormously and is available for the asking.
- **Studying generally rather than for business register.** Different skill, different practice.
- **Leaving language study until the final year.** It improves over years, not weeks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I work in Japan without speaking Japanese?

Yes, in specific segments — foreign-affiliated companies, some technology firms, English teaching, and certain specialist roles. It closes the conventional generalist track at traditional Japanese companies.

### What level do most employers ask for?

Business-level Japanese is the common stated requirement for Japanese-language roles, and postings usually state a JLPT level explicitly.

### Is a certificate enough on its own?

It gets you past a filter and the interview tests the reality. The test does not assess speaking, so demonstrated ability matters more than the certificate.

### How long does it take to reach business level?

Years rather than months for most learners, depending on starting point and intensity. This is the main argument for deciding early if Japan is a serious goal.

### Do technical roles have lower requirements?

Sometimes, particularly in engineering and research, and internal communication is generally still Japanese at traditional employers. Technology companies vary widely.

### Should I put my JLPT level on my resume?

Yes, prominently. For a foreign applicant it is frequently the most consequential single line in the document.
