## Key Takeaways

- Ten minutes is enough if you work in a fixed order and resist rewriting.
- Two minutes reading, three on the summary, one reordering bullets, one on skills, three on vocabulary.
- Set a timer - the work expands to fill whatever time you give it, with diminishing returns after the first ten minutes.
- The routine only works on top of a strong base resume; fix the base once rather than per application.
- Applying to ten jobs with ten-minute tailoring beats applying to three with an hour each.

The reason most people stop tailoring is that they have no routine, so each application becomes an open-ended editing session. Give it a fixed order and a clock and it becomes something you can do ten times in an afternoon.

## Before You Start: The Base Resume

This routine assumes a base resume that is already good — parses cleanly, bullets that state outcomes, no formatting traps.

If your base is weak, tailoring it repeatedly is wasted effort. Spend two hours fixing the base once. Then every application afterwards costs ten minutes.

### What "already good" actually means

Four things, and they are all binary rather than matters of taste:

**It parses.** Paste the file into a plain text editor. Every job, every date, every bullet should arrive in a sensible order. If a role vanishes, no amount of tailoring helps, because [parsing errors delete a job](/blog/parsing-errors-delete-a-job) before any of your wording is read.

**Bullets state outcomes.** "Responsible for reporting" is a duty. "Cut month-end reporting from nine days to four" is an outcome. If your bullets are duties, you are tailoring the wrong layer — fix that first with [a proper bullet rewrite](/blog/resume-bullet-points-rewrite-guide).

**Dates are unambiguous.** Month and year, adjacent to the role. [Dates that parse wrong](/blog/employment-dates-parsed-wrong) cost you experience you actually have.

**Nothing is in a graphic.** Text in an image is invisible to every parser.

Fix those once. They are the difference between a routine that compounds and one that decorates a broken document.

### The two-hour investment, and why it pays back immediately

If you apply to thirty roles over a job search — a modest number — a strong base saves you the ten minutes you would otherwise spend patching the same weaknesses in every single copy. That is five hours recovered from the two you spent.

The less obvious return is that a strong base makes the ten-minute routine *possible*. When the underlying bullets already state outcomes, tailoring is genuinely a matter of emphasis and vocabulary. When they do not, every application quietly turns into a rewrite, which is exactly the open-ended editing session the clock is meant to prevent.

## Minute 0–2: Read for Three Things

Read the posting once. You are looking for exactly three things, not absorbing the whole document.

**The real requirements.** Which two or three items appear in the title, the opening paragraph, and again in the bullets? Repetition marks what matters. Ignore anything mentioned once at the bottom.

**The vocabulary.** What does this employer call things? "Stakeholder management" or "client relations"? "Analytics engineering" or "reporting"? Note their words.

**The level.** Is this a doing role or an owning role? That determines whether your bullets should foreground hands-on specifics or scope and decisions.

Two minutes. Do not take notes beyond three terms.

## Minute 2–5: Rewrite the Summary

Your summary is three lines and it is the highest-leverage thing you can change. Structure:

> [Your role] with [X years] in [the two capabilities this posting repeats]. [One specific proof point relevant to this job.]

Applied:

> Operations coordinator with four years in process improvement and stakeholder management. Delivered a 120-person relocation across four months on a £180k budget, 4% under.

The first sentence matches the posting's language. The second gives one concrete reason to keep reading. That is the whole job.

## Minute 5–6: Reorder Your Top Bullets

Under your most recent role, move the most relevant bullet to first position.

No rewriting. Just reordering. A recruiter reads the first two bullets under your current job, and this ensures the relevant one is among them.

If your most relevant achievement sits under an older role, either move that bullet up within its role, or name the capability in your summary so it appears early regardless.

## Minute 6–7: Adjust the Skills Line

Reorder so the posting's central terms come first. Drop items that are irrelevant to this application.

> **Base:** Excel, PowerPoint, SQL, Tableau, dbt, Snowflake, Jira, Confluence
>
> **For a data engineering posting:** SQL, dbt, Snowflake, Python, Airflow, Git

Shorter and led by what they asked for. You are not adding anything you lack; you are surfacing what fits.

## Minute 7–10: Rename Two or Three Things

Find the two or three places where you did the work but used different words, and switch to theirs.

> **Before:** Looked after relationships with our agency partners and kept projects on schedule.
>
> **After:** Owned vendor management across three agency partners, holding delivery to contracted timelines.

Three of these is plenty. This is the step where people overrun, because the missing-keyword list is long and tempting. Two or three genuine renames capture most of the value.

### What counts as a rename, and what does not

The line is simple: a rename changes the label on work you did. Anything that changes the work itself is a fabrication.

| Their term | Your term | Rename? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor management | Managed agency relationships | Yes | Same work, their label |
| Stakeholder management | Kept department heads aligned | Yes | Same work, their label |
| A/B testing | Ran split tests on the signup page | Yes | Same work, their label |
| Data pipeline | Built the nightly import job | Yes | Same work, their label |
| Kubernetes | Deployed with Docker | **No** | Different technology |
| Team leadership | Mentored one junior | **No** | Different scope |
| P&L ownership | Tracked the budget | **No** | Different responsibility |

The first four are the reason tailoring works at all. The last three are the reason it gets people caught in interviews. If you cannot answer a follow-up question about it for ninety seconds, it is not a rename — this is the whole subject of [tailoring without lying](/blog/tailoring-without-lying).

### A second worked example: a career-changer

The routine above assumes a same-field application. Here it is under harder conditions — a teacher moving into corporate learning and development, where almost none of the vocabulary transfers automatically.

> **Posting repeats:** learning design, stakeholder management, measurable outcomes
>
> **Before:** Taught GCSE history to five classes, including exam preparation and differentiated materials for mixed-ability groups.
>
> **After:** Designed differentiated learning materials for 150 learners across five cohorts, lifting pass rates from 71% to 84% over two years.

Nothing was invented. The classes became cohorts, the pupils became learners, the teaching became learning design, and the result — which was always true — moved to the front where it does work.

This took four minutes rather than three, which is the honest cost of a career change. See [which bullets to rewrite](/blog/which-bullets-to-rewrite) when the gap is wider than vocabulary.

## What This Looks Like at Different Levels

The five steps stay the same. Where the ten minutes go does not.

| Level | Most of the time goes to | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fresher / graduate | Skills line and projects order | Little work history to reorder; relevance is carried by projects and tools |
| Early career (1–4 yrs) | Bullet order and renames | Enough material to prioritise, not enough to cut much |
| Mid-level (5–10 yrs) | Summary and bullet selection | Too much material — the job is choosing what to foreground |
| Senior / lead | Summary almost entirely | Readers assess level and scope first; the summary settles both |
| Career changer | Renames and framing | The vocabulary gap is the whole problem |

A fresher reordering an empty experience section is wasting four of their ten minutes. A senior candidate fiddling with the skills line is doing the same. Spend the time where your reader is actually looking.

## Common Mistakes

### Rewriting instead of re-emphasising

The most common overrun. You open a bullet to change one word, decide the whole sentence is weak, and forty minutes later you have rewritten the role. If a bullet genuinely is weak, that is a base-resume problem — note it, finish the application, fix it once afterwards for every future application.

### Chasing the full missing-keyword list

A scan returns twelve missing terms. Two or three are real; the rest are noise, boilerplate, or things you genuinely do not have. Working down the whole list produces a resume written for a parser rather than a person, and [some missing skills are meant to be ignored](/blog/ats-missing-skills-to-ignore).

### Tailoring the wrong document

If the posting asks for a cover letter, that is where role-specific reasoning belongs — not crammed into a resume summary. Budget for it separately.

### Starting from an already-tailored copy

Always start from the base. Tailoring a copy that was tailored for a different role compounds the drift, and after three or four applications the document reads as though it were written for nobody in particular.

### Not saving the version

Save each tailored file as `name-company-role.pdf`. When the call comes three weeks later, you need to know which version they read.

## Then Stop

At ten minutes, send it.

The instinct to do one more pass is strong and almost never pays. A second pass on the same posting typically produces cosmetic changes, and the time is worth more spent on the next application.

If you have an hour, apply to six jobs at ten minutes each rather than one job at sixty.

## When to Spend Longer

Three cases justify more:

**A role you genuinely want**, where a deeper rework of bullets and section order is worth an hour.

**A career change**, where the whole framing needs to shift and ten minutes cannot do it.

**A stretch role**, where your relevance is not obvious and needs deliberate surfacing.

Everything else gets ten minutes.

### Is ten-minute tailoring actually enough?

For a well-matched role with a strong base resume, yes. The summary, bullet order, skills emphasis and two or three renames cover most of what tailoring achieves. The remaining gains are real but small, and they cost far more than ten minutes to capture.

### Should I tailor my cover letter too?

If you are writing one, it needs more adaptation than the resume — it is the document where role-specific reasoning belongs. Budget separately for it rather than stealing minutes from this routine.

### What if I am applying to 20 jobs a week?

Group by role type. Build two or three base versions — one per role family you are targeting — then the per-application work drops to the summary and bullet order, about four minutes. This is the single biggest efficiency gain available at volume.

### Does this work for senior roles?

Yes, with more weight on the summary and scope framing. Senior readers are assessing level fit before anything else, and the summary establishes that fastest. Expect to spend six of the ten minutes there.

### How do I stop myself over-editing?

Set an actual timer. It sounds trivial and it is the single most effective part of the routine. The work expands to fill whatever time you allow it.

### What if the posting is vague and repeats nothing?

Then there is nothing to match, and you should spend two minutes rather than ten. A posting with no repeated terms is either badly written or deliberately generic; treat it as a low-information application and move on. [Decoding a vague job description](/blog/decode-a-vague-job-description) covers the cases worth persisting with.

### Do I re-tailor if I apply to the same company twice?

Yes, per posting. Two roles at one company can have entirely different requirements, and the second application is often read by a different team.

### Is it worth tailoring for a referral?

Less so. A referral changes who reads the resume and how charitably, so the marginal value of vocabulary matching drops. Spend five minutes on the summary and send it.

### Should the file name change per application?

Yes — `firstname-lastname-company-role.pdf`. It helps you track versions, and it reads as deliberate to whoever opens it.

### What if ten minutes never feels like enough?

That is usually a signal that the base resume is doing too little work, not that the routine is too short. If every application needs substantial rewriting, the problem is upstream: the bullets are duties rather than outcomes, or the summary is generic. Fix the base, and the ten minutes starts to feel generous.
