## Key Takeaways

- Keyword-matching systems credit the exact string; semantic systems credit the meaning. Both are in use in 2026.
- You cannot tell which one is reading your application, so write for the stricter case.
- Use the posting's exact term for the two or three central requirements, and natural language everywhere else.
- Acronyms and full forms should both appear once, together, the first time.
- Never write two synonyms side by side to hedge - that is the pattern recruiters read as stuffing.

If a posting asks for "stakeholder management" and your resume says "client relationship management," does that count?

It depends entirely on which system is reading it, and you have no way of knowing. That uncertainty has a straightforward resolution.

## The Two Kinds of Matching

**String matching.** The system looks for the characters in the posting. "Stakeholder management" and "client relationship management" are simply different text, and the second earns nothing against a requirement for the first. Older and simpler systems work this way, and plenty are still in service.

**Semantic matching.** The system has a model of meaning. It can recognise that "client relationship management" occupies similar conceptual space to "stakeholder management," and give partial or full credit. Newer AI-assisted screening works this way.

Two consequences follow. Semantic systems are more forgiving, so writing for them is easy. String systems are unforgiving, so writing for them is the binding constraint. **Write for the string matcher and the semantic matcher will also be satisfied.**

## The Rule

**For the two or three central requirements, use the posting's exact words.** These are the terms in the title, repeated across sections, marked required. Precision matters most where the stakes are highest.

**Everywhere else, write naturally.** Peripheral terms are not worth distorting a sentence for, and a resume written entirely in the posting's phrasing reads like the posting.

That is the whole approach. What follows is how to apply it without making the writing worse.

## Using the Exact Term Without Damage

The fear is that matching the posting's vocabulary produces stilted prose. It does, if you paste the term in. It does not, if you rewrite the sentence around it.

> **Your original:** Looked after our biggest clients, keeping them happy and fixing problems when they came up.
>
> **Term pasted in:** Looked after our biggest clients (stakeholder management), keeping them happy and fixing problems.
>
> **Sentence rewritten:** Owned stakeholder management for the eight largest enterprise accounts, resolving escalations and running quarterly reviews.

The middle version is what people produce when they treat this as keyword insertion. The third is what happens when you treat it as translation - and it is better writing than the original as well as better matched.

## Acronyms: Use Both, Once

Postings are inconsistent. One says "SEO," another "search engine optimisation." A string matcher credits only what it finds.

The fix is to write both, together, on first use:

> Led search engine optimisation (SEO) across three product sites, growing organic sessions 40% in eight months.

One mention covers both forms. After that, use whichever reads better.

This matters most for terms with common short forms: SEO, CRM, ETL, KPI, API, CI/CD, P&L, QBR, SLA. It matters less for terms everybody writes the same way.

## What Not to Do

The tempting move is to hedge by listing both:

> Managed stakeholder relationships / client relationships / customer accounts and stakeholder management across regions.

This is the single most recognisable stuffing pattern. It scores marginally better against a string matcher and tells any human reader that the resume was assembled for a machine.

Pick the posting's term. Use it once. Move on.

## When the Posting's Word Is Wrong for You

Sometimes the exact term would be a false claim. "Stakeholder management" for two emails to a colleague is an overstatement, and the interview will expose it.

In that case, do not use the term. Write what you actually did, accurately, and accept that this requirement is partially covered. A smaller honest claim survives questioning; a larger false one does not.

The test: if an interviewer said "tell me about your stakeholder management experience," would you have a real answer? If yes, use the term. If you would be reaching, do not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do modern systems really understand synonyms?**
The newer ones handle them reasonably well. The problem is that you cannot tell which system a given employer uses, and simpler string-matching tools remain common.

**Should I use British or American spellings?**
Match the posting. If it writes "organisation," write "organisation." Where you are unsure, the employer's country is the better guide.

**What if the posting uses a term incorrectly?**
Use their version anyway if it is what they will search for, provided it is not an outright false claim about your work. You are writing to be found by their reader, not correcting their vocabulary.

**Is it worth including both a job title variant and the posting's title?**
Yes, where accurate - "Business Partner II (HR Business Partner)" covers an internal title and the market term without claiming a role you did not hold.

**Does plural versus singular matter?**
Rarely. Most systems normalise simple variations. Do not write both forms to cover it.
