## Key Takeaways

- Spain has **persistently high youth unemployment** and **low entry-level pay**, and any honest guide has to start there.
- **Barcelona and Madrid are genuine international hubs**, with large English-working technology, shared services and startup sectors.
- Spain's **temporary contract culture** was reformed to push employers toward permanent contracts, which changed the market meaningfully.
- **Spanish is required for most of the market**; the international sector in the two big cities is the exception.
- Spain is unusually strong for **remote and hybrid international work**, with a dedicated digital nomad route.

## The honest starting point

Spain is a lovely place to live and a difficult place to start a [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide), and both are true.

**Youth unemployment has been persistently high** for well over a decade, among the highest in the EU. It has improved but remains a structural feature rather than a temporary condition.

**Entry-level [salaries](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) are low** by Northern European standards, and lower relative to [housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide) costs in Barcelona and Madrid than the headline numbers suggest.

**Temporary contracts were historically dominant** for young workers, creating a two-tier market. A significant labour reform pushed [employers](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes) toward permanent contracts and reduced the use of short temporary ones, which has genuinely changed things — but the underlying pay levels did not transform.

**And yet the international sector is real and growing.** Barcelona and Madrid have attracted a substantial number of international companies, shared service centres, technology firms and startups, and those employers pay and behave differently from the domestic market.

**The practical resolution:** target the international sector or a specific Spanish strength. A generalist search in the domestic Spanish market is one of the harder propositions in Western Europe.

## Where the work is

| City | What concentrates there |
|---|---|
| **Madrid** | Corporate head offices, banking, insurance, energy, consulting, telecoms |
| **Barcelona** | Technology, startups, pharmaceuticals, automotive, tourism, design |
| **Valencia** | Logistics, automotive, an emerging technology and startup scene |
| **Bilbao / Basque Country** | Industry, engineering, machine tools, energy |
| **Seville / Málaga** | Aerospace in Seville; Málaga has built a genuine technology park |
| **Zaragoza** | Logistics and automotive manufacturing |

**Málaga deserves attention.** The Andalusian technology park and a deliberate strategy of attracting technology companies have produced a real and growing sector, with dramatically lower living costs than Barcelona or Madrid.

**Bilbao and the Basque Country** hold Spain's most sophisticated industrial base — machine tools, energy engineering, aerospace components — and it is under-applied by internationals who fixate on the two big cities.

**Barcelona's technology scene** is the largest in Spain and genuinely international, with a substantial share of teams operating in English.

## Language and the international sector

**Spanish is required across most of the domestic market**, and in Catalonia, Catalan matters too for public-sector and some local roles.

**Where English works:** Barcelona and Madrid's technology companies, international shared service centres, startups with international teams, tourism management, and multinational offices.

**Shared service centres are a specific opportunity.** Many multinationals run their European [finance](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs), HR, customer service and IT support operations from Spain, hiring for language [skills](/blog/resume-skills-match-job-description). If you speak German, Dutch, Nordic languages or French alongside English, these centres actively want you, and they are a genuine entry route.

**Spanish is learnable quickly** for speakers of other European languages, and the return is high — it opens the domestic market, Latin America, and a substantial part of the world.

**Do not underestimate Catalan** if you intend to build a long career in Barcelona. It is not required for most private-sector work and it matters socially and for public-sector roles.

## Contracts, pay and what leaves your account

The financial mechanics, which differ from [Northern Europe](/blog/southern-europe-vs-northern-europe-jobs) in specific ways.

**The labour reform** significantly restricted the use of temporary contracts and pushed employers toward the contrato indefinido — the permanent contract. Fixed-discontinuous contracts became the mechanism for genuinely seasonal work. This changed the market meaningfully and older advice about Spain's temporary contract culture is now partly out of date.

**Salaries are quoted annually**, frequently over fourteen payments — twelve monthly plus two extra payments (pagas extras), usually in summer and December. An annual figure needs clarifying against this.

**Social security contributions** are substantial on the employer side and meaningful on yours, funding [healthcare](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist), pensions and unemployment.

**Convenio colectivo** — the sector collective agreement — sets minimum pay and conditions by role and sector. Find out which one covers you; it tells you the floor.

**Income tax is progressive** with a regional component, so effective rates differ between autonomous communities. Madrid has historically been lower-tax than Catalonia, which is a genuine factor in a close comparison.

**Healthcare is public and good** once you are contributing, with a private sector on top frequently offered as an employer benefit.

**And the honest note on housing:** Barcelona and Madrid rents have risen substantially and consume a large share of a [graduate](/blog/first-resume-for-gen-z-2026) salary. This is the single biggest constraint on Spanish graduate life.

## Getting set up

The administrative sequence, which has hard dependencies.

**NIE — Número de Identidad de Extranjero.** Your foreigner identity number, needed for essentially everything. EU citizens get one alongside their registration; non-EU nationals obtain it as part of the residence process.

**Empadronamiento** — registering at your local town hall, which is required for healthcare and many other services and needs proof of address.

**Social security number**, obtained once you have a [job offer](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email) or are registering as self-employed.

**Bank account**, which generally needs the NIE.

**Healthcare card** from your regional health service, following social security registration.

**Expect bureaucracy and appointment scarcity.** Spanish administrative appointments — cita previa — can be genuinely hard to obtain in the big cities, and this is a real practical obstacle rather than a minor annoyance. Persistence and early booking both help.

## Where roles are advertised

Spain's channels are distinctive and a few carry most of the market.

**InfoJobs is the dominant national board** and has no exact international equivalent in how central it is to the Spanish market.

**[LinkedIn](/blog/linkedin-profile-optimization-guide) is strong in Barcelona and Madrid** and for the international sector generally.

**Indeed España and Tecnoempleo** carry substantial volume, the latter specifically for technology roles.

**SEPE**, the public employment service, and the regional employment services run official listings.

**Company career pages** for the large employers — the banks, the energy companies, the telecoms operators.

**Startup platforms** and the Barcelona ecosystem's own channels for the technology scene.

**ETTs — temporary work agencies** — are significant in Spain and a genuine route into permanent roles, particularly in shared services and industry.

**And networks matter more than in Northern Europe.** [Referrals](/blog/networking-messages-for-referrals), university connections and professional contacts carry real weight, so activating any connection you have is worth more than an extra [fifty applications](/blog/chrome-extension-50-applications-workflow).

## Working culture

Warmer, later, and more relationship-led than Northern Europe.

**The working day runs later.** Start times are often later, lunch is a genuine break, and finishing at eight in the evening is normal in parts of the domestic market. The jornada intensiva — a compressed day finishing early — is common in summer.

**Relationships precede business.** Time spent building rapport is part of the work, not a preamble to it.

**[Hierarchy](/blog/hr-round-interview-questions-answers) is present but not rigid**, and the register is warm rather than formal after the first meeting.

**August largely closes.** Expecting responses in August is unrealistic.

**International employers operate differently.** Technology companies, startups and multinationals in Barcelona and Madrid run on international norms with earlier finishes and flatter structures, which is a genuine cultural difference from the domestic market and worth knowing which one you are joining.

**[Statutory holiday](/blog/questions-to-ask-in-an-interview) is generous** and public holidays are numerous, varying by region and even by city.

## Living costs and the quality-of-life trade

The reason people accept Spanish salaries, and where the arithmetic actually lands.

**Barcelona and Madrid housing is the constraint.** Rents have risen substantially and consume a large share of a graduate salary. Shared flats well into your late twenties are normal.

**Everything else is inexpensive.** Food, eating out, transport, coffee and daily life cost noticeably less than in Northern Europe, which offsets a meaningful part of the salary gap.

**Málaga, Valencia, Seville and Bilbao** are dramatically cheaper than the two big cities while having real employment.

**Healthcare is public, good and free at the point of use** once you are contributing.

**Public transport in the cities is cheap and good.**

**And the intangibles are the actual argument.** Climate, daylight, food, social life and a culture that protects time outside work. People weigh these heavily, and given that a Northern European salary buys grey winters and expensive rent, it is not an irrational trade.

**The honest calculation:** you will save less in Spain than in Germany or the Netherlands, and you will likely enjoy the year more. Which matters depends entirely on what stage you are at and what you want next.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You speak English plus another European language

Shared service centres in Madrid, Barcelona and increasingly Málaga actively recruit for exactly this. It is a genuine entry into the Spanish market and one that most people overlook.

### Scenario 2: You want technology work

Barcelona first, Madrid second, Málaga as the growing and much cheaper option. Barcelona's scene is the largest and most international in Spain.

### Scenario 3: You already work remotely for a foreign employer

Look at Spain's digital nomad route seriously. It is one of the more accessible in Europe and it lets you have Spain without needing the Spanish [job market](/blog/choosing-country-to-study-abroad-for-jobs) at all.

> **Tip:** If you speak English plus German, Dutch, French or a Nordic language, target the multinational shared service centres in Madrid, Barcelona and Málaga. They recruit specifically for language combinations, they pay above the domestic market, and most job seekers never think of them.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Target the international sector deliberately
- Use a second European language as your advantage
- Consider Málaga, Bilbao and Valencia
- Understand the contract type in any offer
- Learn Spanish — the return is high
- Compare salary against real rent

### Don't

- Run a generalist search in the domestic market
- Expect Northern European entry-level pay
- Ignore shared service centres
- Assume English works outside the two big cities
- Underestimate Barcelona and Madrid housing costs
- Dismiss Catalan entirely if Barcelona is long-term

## Common Mistakes

- **A generalist domestic search.** Spain's graduate market is genuinely difficult without a specific angle.
- **Ignoring shared service centres.** They hire for language combinations and are widely overlooked.
- **Only Barcelona and Madrid.** Málaga, Bilbao and Valencia have real work and far lower costs.
- **Expecting Northern European salaries.** Spanish entry-level pay is substantially lower.
- **Not reading the contract.** The reform changed the landscape and contract type still matters.
- **Skipping Spanish.** It opens the domestic market and much of the world beyond it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Spain a good place for a graduate job?

For the international sector in Barcelona, Madrid or Málaga, or for a specific Spanish industrial strength, yes. For a generalist domestic search it is one of Western Europe's harder markets.

### Do I need to speak Spanish?

For most of the domestic market, yes. Technology companies, startups, shared service centres and multinationals in the big cities operate substantially in English.

### What are shared service centres?

Multinational operations running European finance, HR, IT or customer functions from Spain, recruiting specifically for language combinations. They are a genuine and widely overlooked entry route.

### Why is Spanish youth unemployment so high?

A long-standing structural feature involving slow growth, a historically two-tier contract market and a mismatch between graduate output and demand. Reform has improved it without transforming pay.

### Which city has the best technology jobs?

Barcelona has the largest and most international scene, Madrid the larger corporate market, and Málaga a fast-growing technology park with much lower living costs.

### Do I need Catalan for Barcelona?

Not for most private-sector work. It matters for public-sector roles and for long-term social and professional integration in Catalonia.
### How are Spanish salaries structured?

Usually quoted annually over fourteen payments — twelve monthly plus two extra payments in summer and December. The sector collective agreement sets the minimum for your role.

### What is an NIE and how do I get one?

The foreigner identity number, needed for almost every transaction in Spain. EU citizens obtain it alongside registration; non-EU nationals get it as part of the residence permit process.
### Where should I look for jobs in Spain?

InfoJobs dominates the national market, with LinkedIn strong in Barcelona and Madrid, Tecnoempleo for technology roles, and temporary work agencies acting as a genuine route into permanent positions.

### What is the Spanish working day like?

Later than Northern Europe in the domestic market, with a genuine lunch break and evening finishes. International employers in Barcelona and Madrid run on international hours, which is a real difference.
