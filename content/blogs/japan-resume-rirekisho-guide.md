## Key Takeaways

- The rirekisho is a standardised form covering personal history, education and employment — not a freely designed document.
- Its structure is fixed, which means the differentiation Western resumes rely on is largely unavailable here.
- Conventions around photograph, handwriting, format and phrasing are specific and taken seriously.
- It is usually paired with a second document describing your work in detail, which is where substance goes.
- Foreign applicants who submit a Western-style resume instead are visibly working from the wrong playbook.

## What it is and why it differs

**A standardised personal history form.** Rather than designing a document to present yourself as advantageously as possible, you complete a form with predetermined fields. Templates are widely available and broadly consistent.

**Standardisation is the point.** The form allows employers to compare candidates on identical fields in an identical order. It is a fundamentally different philosophy from a market where a resume is a marketing document and its design is part of the argument.

**Which means differentiation happens elsewhere.** You cannot lead with your strongest evidence, restructure sections, or emphasise what suits you — the form determines the order. Substance goes into the [accompanying work history document](/blog/japan-shokumu-keirekisho-guide) and into the interviews.

**Comparison with other markets.** A [US resume](/blog/usa-resume-format-for-students) removes personal details entirely and rewards [tailoring](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) and design. The rirekisho requires personal details and rewards correctness and conformity. Neither approach is universal, and applying one in the other's market is the error — the same lesson as the [country-by-country resume differences](/blog/international-student-resume-differences-by-country), in a more extreme form.

## What the form contains

**Personal details.** Name, date of birth, address, contact details. Furigana readings are typically required alongside names.

**A photograph.** Conventionally expected, in a specific size, taken in business attire against a plain background. Photo booths producing compliant images are common in Japan. This is a genuine convention rather than an optional flourish.

**Education history.** Listed chronologically, typically from a defined point in your schooling onward, with entry and graduation entries.

**Employment history.** Chronological, with entry and departure entries for each employer.

**Licences and qualifications.** Listed with dates obtained. Language qualifications such as [JLPT levels](/blog/japan-jlpt-n2-n1-for-employment) belong here and matter considerably for foreign applicants.

**Motivation for applying.** A free-text section, and the main place where you can actually differentiate yourself on the form itself.

**Personal requests or notes.** Conventionally used for preferences about location or conditions, and frequently left with a standard phrase indicating you defer to the company.

## The conventions that matter

**Chronological order, no gaps unexplained.** The history is expected to be continuous and complete.

**Consistent date formatting.** Japanese applications may use either the Western calendar or Japanese era years, and the important thing is consistency throughout the document.

**Handwriting versus typing.** Traditionally these were handwritten, and neatness was itself part of the assessment. Typed submissions have become widely accepted, particularly for online applications, though some traditional employers still prefer handwritten. Check the employer's stated preference where one exists.

**No correction fluid.** If handwriting and you make an error, convention is to start again rather than correct.

**Formal, standard phrasing.** Conventional set phrases are used for entering and leaving education and employment, and using them correctly signals familiarity.

**The photograph is not optional in practice.** A missing or informal photograph is noticed.

**Accuracy above all.** Because the form is standardised and factual, errors are visible in a way they are not in a designed document.

## For foreign applicants specifically

**Submit a rirekisho, not a Western resume**, when applying to Japanese companies through Japanese channels. Submitting the wrong document type is the clearest possible signal that you have not researched the market.

**Foreign-affiliated companies frequently accept Western formats**, and some prefer them. Check what the specific employer expects — the [English-conducted job market](/blog/japan-english-speaking-jobs-guide) generally follows different conventions.

**Your language qualification belongs prominently in the qualifications section.** For foreign applicants it is frequently the single most consequential line on the form.

**Get a native speaker to check it** if you are writing in Japanese. Set phrases and formal register are conventional and errors are conspicuous.

**Explain overseas education clearly.** Institution names unfamiliar to a Japanese reader benefit from a brief clarification of what the qualification is equivalent to.

**Keep a master copy.** You will complete this repeatedly, and having your dates, readings and standard phrasings settled once saves considerable time.

## Common Mistakes

- **Submitting a Western-style resume instead.** The most visible signal of unfamiliarity with the market.
- **Omitting the photograph.** Conventionally expected and its absence is noticed.
- **Inconsistent date formats.** Mixing calendar systems within one document.
- **Leaving gaps in the chronology.** The history is expected to be continuous.
- **Using correction fluid on a handwritten form.** Convention is to start again.
- **Treating the motivation section as filler.** It is the one part of the form where you can genuinely differentiate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to handwrite it?

Typed versions are widely accepted now, particularly for online applications. Some traditional employers still prefer handwritten, so check where a preference is stated.

### Is the photograph really necessary?

By convention, yes, for applications to Japanese companies. It should be a formal photograph in business attire, and compliant photo booths are widely available in Japan.

### What if my education system does not map neatly onto the form?

List it clearly and chronologically, adding a brief clarification of what the qualification is equivalent to where the institution or degree would be unfamiliar.

### Can I use the same rirekisho for every application?

The factual sections stay the same. The motivation section should be written fresh for each company, exactly as the [why this company answer](/blog/singapore-cover-letter-and-application-guide) matters in any market.

### Do foreign-affiliated companies want one?

Frequently not — many accept or prefer Western-style resumes. Check the specific employer rather than assuming either way.

### What else do I submit alongside it?

Typically a [shokumu keirekisho](/blog/japan-shokumu-keirekisho-guide), the work history document where actual substance about your experience goes.
