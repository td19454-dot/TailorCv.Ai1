## Key Takeaways

- A US student resume is one page, reverse chronological, and contains no photo, date of birth, marital status or nationality.
- "Resume" and "CV" are not interchangeable in the US — a CV means an academic document and is used for research and faculty roles.
- Including personal details is not just unnecessary; it creates a legal problem for the employer, which is why some discard those resumes.
- Bullets are judged on outcome, not duty. The same fact written two ways screens completely differently.
- Formatting choices that look elegant — columns, tables, text boxes, headers — are the most common cause of a resume parsing badly.

## The hard rules

These are conventions rather than laws, but they are near-universal in US campus hiring.

**One page.** For a student or recent graduate this is expected, not a suggestion. Two pages signals either padding or an inability to prioritise. The exception is genuinely research-heavy academic applications.

**Reverse chronological.** Most recent first, in every section. Functional resumes that hide dates are read as concealment.

**No photograph.** This is the difference that catches most international students. In much of Europe, Asia and Latin America a photo is standard. In the US it is a problem.

**No personal details.** No date of birth, marital status, gender, nationality, religion, or number of dependents.

### Why the photo rule is stricter than it looks

This is not an aesthetic preference. US employers operate under anti-discrimination rules, and a resume showing your age, gender, race or national origin creates evidence risk in a hiring dispute.

Some companies have formal policies of discarding resumes containing photos, precisely so they cannot be accused of having considered protected characteristics. Others strip them before the hiring manager sees them.

So a photo does not merely fail to help. In some pipelines it removes you before anyone reads a word. If you are adapting a resume from another country, deleting the photo and personal details block is the first edit to make.

## Section order that works

For a student with limited work experience:

1. **Name and contact** — email, phone, LinkedIn, GitHub or portfolio if relevant. No full postal address; city and state is enough.
2. **Education** — while you are a student or within a year or two of graduating, this goes above experience. After that it moves down.
3. **Experience** — internships, part-time work, research, significant volunteering.
4. **Projects** — for technical fields this often carries more weight than the jobs.
5. **Skills** — concrete, named technologies. Not "hard-working" or "team player".

Once you have two or three years of full-time work, education drops below experience. Until then, it is your primary credential.

### What goes in education

Degree, institution, graduation date (actual or expected). GPA if it is strong — the convention is to include it above roughly 3.0-3.5 and omit it otherwise. Relevant coursework is acceptable for a student with little experience, but it is weak evidence and should never displace a project.

Do not include your high school once you are in university.

## Writing bullets that survive the screen

The single largest quality gap in student resumes is bullets describing duties instead of outcomes.

Compare:

> Responsible for maintaining the department's inventory spreadsheet.

with:

> Rebuilt the department's inventory tracking in Excel, cutting the weekly reconciliation from three hours to twenty minutes.

Same job. The second states what changed and by how much. The first states that you existed.

The pattern that works: **strong verb → what you actually did → the technology or method → the result.** Not every bullet will have a number, and inventing one is worse than not having it. But most student work has a scale, a frequency or a count available if you look — how many users, how many records, how many hours saved, how many people affected.

Avoid: "responsible for", "helped with", "worked on", "assisted in", "duties included". These describe proximity to work rather than performance of it. [Stopping your resume sounding generic](/blog/how-to-stop-sounding-generic-on-resume) is largely about eliminating this pattern.

## Formatting that parses

Almost every US employer of scale screens resumes through software before a human sees them. The document must survive being read by a machine.

**What breaks parsing:**

- **Multi-column layouts.** The parser reads across, mixing your job title with your skills list.
- **Tables** used for layout. Cell contents get flattened unpredictably.
- **Text boxes and graphics.** Frequently ignored entirely — text inside them can vanish.
- **Headers and footers.** Often stripped. Putting your contact details there means losing them.
- **Icons instead of labels.** A phone glyph does not parse as "phone".
- **Uncommon fonts** that may not embed correctly.

**What works:** a single column, standard section headings ("Experience", "Education", "Skills"), a common font, and a PDF exported from a word processor rather than scanned or image-based.

The reliable test is mechanical: export your PDF, select all the text, copy it, paste it into a plain text file. That is roughly what the parser sees. If your job titles are jumbled with your skills, or your contact details are missing, fix the layout before you fix the wording. A [free ATS score check](/ats-analysis) does the same job more thoroughly, including whether the keywords the posting asks for actually appear.

Formatting for the parser is separate from [matching the posting's keywords](/blog/how-to-match-resume-keywords-to-job-description) — you need both, and the format problem is the one people miss because the document looks fine on screen.

## Resume vs CV in American usage

In the US these are different documents.

**Resume** — one to two pages, targeted, used for essentially all industry roles.

**CV** — long, comprehensive, listing publications, conference presentations, teaching, grants and references. Used for academic positions, research roles, and some medical and scientific work.

If a US employer asks for a resume and receives a six-page CV listing every course you have taken, it reads as not understanding the market. If a research programme asks for a CV and receives a one-page resume, it reads as thin. Ask which they mean if a posting is ambiguous, particularly at universities where both are used.

## Adapting a resume from another country

The most common edits when converting a European, Indian or East Asian resume for US applications:

- Delete the photo.
- Delete date of birth, marital status, gender, nationality, passport details, father's name.
- Delete the full postal address; keep city and state.
- Cut to one page.
- Replace "Curriculum Vitae" as a title — a US resume needs no title at all beyond your name.
- Remove references and "references available on request", which is assumed and wastes a line.
- Convert grades to a recognisable format, or state the scale. "First Class" and "8.4 CGPA" mean nothing to most US screeners without context.
- Remove declaration statements and signatures, which are a convention in some countries and read as odd in the US.

For international students, none of this touches [work authorisation](/blog/usa-opt-cpt-explained-students) — visa status does not belong on the resume at all. It comes up later in the process, and putting it on the document takes space from evidence while volunteering information the screen does not need.

## Common Mistakes

- **Keeping the photo.** The single most damaging carry-over from non-US formats.
- **Two pages as a student.** Reads as an inability to prioritise, and the second page often goes unread.
- **Listing duties rather than outcomes.** The most common quality problem in student resumes.
- **Using a designed template with columns.** It looks better than it parses, and parsing comes first.
- **Contact details in the header.** Frequently stripped by parsers, leaving no way to reach you.
- **Including references or "available on request".** Assumed, and wastes space you need.
- **One resume for every application.** The screen matches language against the posting, and a generic document matches nothing specifically. [Tailoring per role](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) is where the interview rate actually moves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I really keep it to one page?

As a student or recent graduate, yes. The convention is strong enough that exceeding it is itself a signal. If you cannot fit, the problem is usually that you are including things that do not earn their space.

### Do I include my GPA?

Include it if it is strong — commonly above 3.0, and certainly above 3.5. Omit it otherwise. Omitting is normal and does not itself raise suspicion; some [employers screen on GPA](/blog/usa-gpa-requirements-employers) and most do not.

### Is a PDF or Word document better?

PDF, unless the posting explicitly asks for Word. Export from a word processor rather than printing to image, so the text remains selectable and machine-readable.

### Can I put projects above work experience?

Yes, and for technical students it is often the right call. If your projects are more impressive and more relevant than your part-time retail job, lead with them.

### Should I mention that I need visa sponsorship?

Not on the resume. It will be asked during the application or interview process and should be [answered directly when it comes up](/blog/usa-visa-sponsorship-question-interview). Volunteering it on a screened document costs you space and offers no benefit.

### What about a summary or objective at the top?

An objective stating what you want is outdated and wastes the most valuable space on the page. A short summary can work if it is specific to the role and carries real evidence, but a generic one is worse than nothing.
