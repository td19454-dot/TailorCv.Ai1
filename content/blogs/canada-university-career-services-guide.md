## Key Takeaways

- The most valuable thing a career centre holds is employer relationships and postings that never appear publicly.
- Co-op portals operate on their own calendar with ranking and matching systems that reward early, deliberate applications.
- Go in your penultimate year, not your final semester — most services are designed to be used before the search is urgent.
- Resume reviews vary in quality; use them for structure and Canadian conventions, not as a substitute for tailoring.
- International students should ask specifically which employers have hired international graduates recently.

## What they actually hold

**Exclusive job postings.** Employers post to university portals when they specifically want that school's students. These roles are less contested than public postings because the applicant pool is capped at your institution. This is the single most valuable and most underused thing on offer.

**Co-op portals.** For programmes with co-op, the portal is the primary hiring channel — see the [co-op student guide](/blog/canada-co-op-student-guide) for how work terms operate. Postings, applications, interview scheduling and employer matching all run through it, often with ranking systems.

**Employer relationships.** Career centres know which employers hire from your programme, who the recruiters are, and what they look for. That knowledge is not published anywhere.

**Campus events.** Employer information sessions, career fairs, on-campus interviews. Attendance is often lower than it should be, which works in your favour.

**Alumni access.** Many centres maintain alumni databases or mentorship programmes. For a student building a network from a standing start, this is the fastest available shortcut — the mechanics of [reaching out effectively](/blog/international-student-networking-guide) apply once you have the names.

## The co-op portal specifically

If your programme includes co-op, understand the mechanics before your first cycle, because they are not intuitive.

**Separate calendar.** Co-op applications run several times a year on the academic cycle, not on the autumn graduate calendar. Summer term applications typically run in the preceding winter.

**Ranking and matching.** Many portals use a system where students rank employers and employers rank students, then a matching algorithm assigns placements. Understanding how the ranking works materially affects your result — ranking strategically is a different exercise from applying enthusiastically to everything.

**Application limits.** Some portals cap how many roles you can apply to per round, which forces prioritisation.

**Deadlines are hard.** Unlike rolling public postings, portal deadlines close and do not reopen.

Ask the co-op office to walk you through the matching process before your first round. Students who understand it do better than students who do not, independent of ability.

## When to go

**Penultimate year, ideally the term before recruiting starts.**

This is the right time and most students miss it. The [Canadian recruiting calendar](/blog/canada-campus-recruiting-timeline) means autumn applications for the following year, so a resume built in spring is the one screened in September.

**Final semester** is late for structured programmes but still useful for off-cycle roles and job-search strategy.

**First and second year** is early for jobs but the right time to understand co-op, meet advisors and learn what your programme's graduates actually do.

## Using the resume review well

Quality varies. Some advisors are excellent; some give generic advice that has not moved in a decade.

Use them for what they reliably know:

- **Canadian conventions.** No photo, no personal details, appropriate length, spelling conventions. Genuinely useful if you are adapting a resume from another country — the [country-by-country differences](/blog/international-student-resume-differences-by-country) are larger than most students expect.
- **Structure and clarity.** A second reader catches what you cannot see.
- **What local employers expect** in your field.

Do not use them for:

- **Tailoring to a specific posting.** That is per-application work and an advisor cannot do it for forty roles. [Tailoring per posting](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) is where interview rates actually move.
- **ATS verification.** Advisors generally cannot tell you how your document parses. An [automated check](/ats-analysis) will.

Bring a specific posting to the appointment rather than asking for a general review. The advice will be sharper.

## For international students

Ask directly and specifically:

**"Which employers on the portal have hired international graduates recently?"**

Career centres often know, and the answer saves you weeks of applications to employers who will not.

**"Does the co-op office place international students, and with which employers?"**

Co-op placements sometimes have constraints, and knowing them early changes your strategy.

**"Who advises on the post-graduation work permit?"**

Career services and the [international student](/blog/international-student-jobs-canada) office are usually separate. The [PGWP](/blog/canada-pgwp-for-new-grads) is the international office's territory, and getting the timing wrong is expensive.

One thing worth doing yourself: many employers do not realise the PGWP is an open permit requiring no sponsorship. Saying so in your first conversation with a recruiter removes an objection that does not exist.

## What they cannot do

Setting expectations honestly:

- **They cannot get you a job.** They provide access and advice; applications and interviews are yours.
- **Their employer list is finite.** It reflects existing relationships, not the whole market. Most Canadian employers are small and hire on demand, and none of them appear on a portal.
- **Advice quality varies.** Weigh it against what you observe from actual postings and outcomes.

Where the portal is thin for your field, the off-campus channel becomes primary, and it works differently — sourcing, referrals and tailoring replace the portal entirely.

## The co-op portal, mechanically

If your programme has co-op, this system is your primary hiring channel and it works nothing like applying on a company website. Misunderstanding the mechanics costs placements.

**A separate calendar.** Co-op cycles run several times a year — typically for winter, summer and fall work terms — with their own opening dates, deadlines and matching rounds. It does not align with the [structured autumn graduate calendar](/blog/canada-campus-recruiting-timeline), and students who plan around one miss the other.

**Ranking and matching.** After interviews, you rank employers and employers rank students, and an algorithm matches. This is the part that changes your behaviour: **ranking an employer you would not accept is dangerous**, because a match is usually binding. Rank honestly, in genuine preference order, and leave off anything you would decline.

**Application limits.** Most portals cap how many postings you can apply to per round. This is the single biggest difference from the open market: volume is not available to you, so selection quality matters far more. Spend the applications deliberately rather than using them up in the first week on whatever appeared first.

**Deadlines are hard.** Missing one by an hour means waiting for the next round. There is no equivalent of a late application being considered.

**Interviews are scheduled through the system** and are often clustered into a short window. Clear your calendar for it.

**Postings appear in waves.** Not everything is up on day one. Checking two or three times a week through the round is worth doing, because strong postings appear late and get fewer applicants.

**Ask the co-op office how the ranking actually works at your institution.**

The details vary — some use multiple rounds, some allow continuous applications after the main match, some have different rules for the first work term. Ten minutes with the coordinator is worth more than any general advice, including this.

## When to go, and what to ask for

Timing determines how useful the office is, and most students arrive at exactly the wrong moment.

**First and second year: go once, for orientation.** Find out what exists — the portal, the co-op calendar, the events, the alumni network. You are not looking for a job yet; you are finding out where things are so that you are not learning the system in a panic later. Twenty minutes.

**Penultimate year: this is the year that matters.** The structured programmes you would apply to in your final autumn have internship pipelines that recruit now, and the co-op cycles that lead to conversions run now. Most career services are designed around being used at this point, and most students do not appear until much later.

**Final year, autumn: use it heavily.** Resume review before applications open, mock interviews before your first real one, employer events throughout.

**Final semester: the least useful moment, and when most students arrive.**

By then the structured programmes have closed, the co-op cycles for that year have matched, and the office can offer general advice rather than access. They will still help; it is simply the smallest version of the help available.

**What to actually ask for, in order of value:**

- **The list of employers who hired from your programme recently** — turns a vague search into targets
- **Which of them have hired international graduates**, if it applies
- **An introduction to alumni** through whatever mentoring or directory scheme exists
- **A mock interview with feedback**, which is the highest-value free thing they offer
- **A resume review**, for structure and Canadian conventions specifically
- **Notice of upcoming employer events**, and which are worth attending

**Book appointments early in the term.** Career centres get overwhelmed in October and again in February, and a slot that takes two days to get in September takes three weeks in October.

## The one-hour visit

If you go once, this is what to get out of it, and it is worth booking a proper appointment rather than dropping in.

**Bring:** your resume, a rough idea of what you want to do, and this list of questions.

**Ask, in order:**

1. **"Which employers have hired from my programme in the last two years?"** — produces your target list
2. **"Which of them have hired international graduates?"** — if it applies to you, the highest-value answer in the building
3. **"What is on the portal that is not posted publicly?"** — the exclusive postings are the least contested applications you will find
4. **"What are the co-op cycle dates and how does the ranking work here?"** — if you have co-op, this determines your whole calendar
5. **"Is there an alumni mentoring scheme or directory I can use?"** — the warmest outreach channel available
6. **"Can I book a mock interview?"** — free, and worth more than reading about interviews
7. **"Which upcoming employer events are actually worth attending?"** — they know which ones are branding and which ones lead somewhere

**Leave with:** a target list, the portal login, the co-op dates in your calendar, a mock interview booked, and the name of one person you can email follow-up questions to.

That is an hour, it is free, and it produces more usable structure than a month of searching alone. The reason so few students do it is not that the service is poor — it is that they wait until the final semester, by which point most of the seven answers no longer help.

## Common Mistakes

- **First visiting in the final semester.** Most services are built to be used a year earlier.
- **Ignoring the portal because the public boards are bigger.** Portal postings are less contested precisely because they are smaller.
- **Not learning how co-op matching works.** It affects outcomes independently of your qualifications.
- **Treating a resume review as tailoring.** They are different jobs.
- **Not asking about international hiring specifically.** Career centres often know and are rarely asked.
- **Assuming the portal is the whole market.** It is one channel among several.

## What they actually hold, and how to use it

Career centres are under-used because students imagine they offer generic advice. The valuable things are less obvious.

**Exclusive job postings.** Employers post to university portals specifically to reach that institution's students. These roles are seen by a fraction of the number who see a public listing, which makes them among the least contested applications available to you. This alone justifies checking the portal weekly.

**Employer relationships.** The office knows which employers hire from your programme, which have hired international students recently, and which are expanding this year. This information is not published anywhere and they will tell you if you ask directly.

**Campus events.** Employer information sessions, panels and fairs. Attendance is frequently low, which means the ratio of students to employer representatives is much better than at a large public event.

**Alumni access.** Many offices maintain alumni networks or mentoring programmes with people who have volunteered to talk to students. This is a warm channel — the highest-yield outreach available — and it sits unused at most institutions.

**Interview practice.** Mock interviews with feedback, free. Worth more than reading about interviews, because the [gap between a written answer and a delivered one](/blog/canada-new-grad-interview-preparation) is only visible when you say it aloud to someone.

**Three questions worth asking explicitly**, because the answers are specific and useful:

**"Which employers have hired from my programme in the last two years?"**

Turns a generic search into a target list.

**"Which of them have hired international graduates?"**

If it applies to you, this is the highest-value question in the building.

**"Who advises on the post-graduation work permit?"**

Career services and the international office are usually separate, and you need both. The [permit timeline](/blog/canada-pgwp-for-new-grads) has deadlines the career centre may not track.

## Using the resume review well, and what they cannot do

**What a resume review is genuinely good for:**

**Canadian conventions.** Length, what to include, what to leave off, how to present education and experience. These differ from US, UK and other national norms in small ways that matter, and an adviser who reads Canadian resumes daily knows them.

**Structure and readability.** A second pair of eyes on whether the document is scannable and whether your strongest evidence is visible in the first third.

**Obvious errors.** Inconsistent dates, formatting problems, things that read badly and that you have stopped seeing.

**What it is not a substitute for:**

**Tailoring per posting.** An adviser reviews a general resume. Every application still needs the language matched to that specific posting, and [tailoring](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) does more for your interview rate than a polish pass on a generic document.

**Parsing.** Advisers assess how a document reads to a person. Employers of size parse it with software first, and a resume that looks correct on the page can extract badly. Run an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) as well — the two checks catch different problems.

**Deciding your content.** An adviser cannot tell you which of your projects matters most for a role they do not know. That judgement is yours.

**And what the office cannot do at all**, which is worth being realistic about:

**They cannot get you hired.** They hold access and advice. The applications, the outreach and the evidence are yours.

**Quality varies.** Some offices are excellent and well-resourced; others are stretched across thousands of students. If your first appointment is unhelpful, that is information about that adviser rather than about the whole service — ask for someone who specialises in your field.

**They may not know immigration.** Career services and the international office are usually separate functions. For anything touching authorisation, timelines or permit eligibility, go to the international office, and go [before your final semester](/blog/canada-pgwp-for-new-grads) rather than after.

**They cannot replace your own network.** The alumni introductions they facilitate are a starting point; the [relationships you build from there](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students) are what actually produce referrals.

The honest summary: a career centre is a channel and a set of free services, not a solution. Students who use it as one of five channels do well. Students who use it as their only channel and then conclude it did not work have usually not run a search at all.

## For international students specifically

The office is more valuable to you than to a domestic classmate, and for reasons that are not obvious.

**Ask which employers have hired international graduates recently.**

The highest-value question available to you in that building. The answer produces a target list of employers with a demonstrated willingness, which is worth more than any amount of general job-search advice.

**Ask them to explain the open permit to you well enough that you can explain it to an employer.**

Many Canadian employers, particularly smaller ones, assume any international candidate carries a sponsorship burden. A [one-sentence correction](/blog/canada-new-grad-interview-preparation) changes outcomes, and you need to be able to deliver it confidently.

**Use them for [Canadian resume](/blog/canadian-resume-format-guide-2026) conventions.** Small national differences — what to include, what to leave off, how to present overseas education and experience — are exactly what an adviser who reads these daily can fix quickly.

**Ask about employers outside the largest cities.** Regional employers are frequently more open, less contested, and better positioned for provincial immigration streams. Career services often know which ones actively recruit international graduates.

**And go to the international office as well, separately.** The two functions are usually distinct. Career services advises on jobs; the international office advises on status, eligibility and permit timelines. You need both, and the permit timeline is the one with a hard deadline attached.

**Go in your penultimate year.** Permit eligibility rules have tightened and now depend on your specific programme and institution. Discovering a problem with a year to go leaves you options; discovering it in your final semester frequently does not.

## What it cannot do

Worth being clear about, so you use it for what it is.

**It cannot get you hired.** The office holds access and advice. The applications, the outreach, the interview preparation and the evidence are yours, and no amount of career centre engagement substitutes for them.

**It cannot replace a network.** The alumni introductions are a starting point. What turns an introduction into a referral is what you do with it, and that is the [outreach work](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students) nobody can do for you.

**It cannot advise on immigration.** Career services and the international office are usually separate functions with separate expertise. Anything touching status, eligibility or permit timelines goes to the international office.

**It cannot tailor your applications.** An adviser reviews a general document. Every posting still needs its own version, and that is where the interview rate actually moves.

Use it as one channel of five, early, with specific questions. Students who do that get real value. Students who treat it as the search itself, and start in their final semester, conclude that it did not help — and they are right, but not for the reason they think.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are career centre postings really less competitive?

Usually, because the applicant pool is limited to your institution. That is the main structural advantage they offer.

### Can I use career services after graduating?

Many institutions allow alumni access for a period, sometimes indefinitely. Ask before you graduate rather than assuming it ends.

### Is co-op worth doing?

For most programmes offering it, yes. It provides Canadian work experience, references and often a direct hiring pipeline — frequently the strongest thing on a graduate resume.

### What if my advisor's advice contradicts what I have read?

Weigh both against what actual postings ask for. Advisors know local conventions well; specific-industry practice sometimes moves faster than general advice.

### Do employers really recruit through these portals?

Yes, particularly for co-op and structured graduate programmes. Smaller employers often do not, which is why the portal is a channel rather than the whole market.

### When should I book my first appointment?

The term before recruiting starts in your penultimate year. That timing gives you a working resume before applications open rather than during them.
