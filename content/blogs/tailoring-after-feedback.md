## Key Takeaways

- Feedback is rare and valuable - but it usually describes a symptom, not the fix. "Not enough leadership" might mean the evidence exists but is buried.
- Sort feedback into three kinds: evidence you have but did not show, evidence you have but framed weakly, and experience you genuinely lack.
- Only the first two are fixable on the resume. The third needs a different plan - more experience, a different role, or an honest acknowledgement.
- When a recruiter asks you to update your resume before submitting it, they are telling you exactly what the hiring manager screens for. Do it, and do it quickly.
- Change only what the feedback touches. Rewriting everything after one comment usually weakens parts that were working.
- Apply lessons from repeated feedback to your base resume; apply one-off feedback only to that company's version.

You got feedback: the recruiter said the hiring manager wanted "more hands-on experience with X", or "more evidence of stakeholder management", or asked you to "strengthen the leadership side" before they submit you. That is useful - but it is usually a description of what the reader did not see, not an instruction. The fix is to work out whether the evidence exists, then move it up, reframe it, or accept the gap honestly.

This guide covers decoding common feedback, three types of fix, and when to change your base resume versus a single version.

## Why Feedback Needs Decoding

Hiring managers and recruiters rarely give precise, resume-level feedback. They compress their impression into a phrase.

### Feedback describes the reader's impression

"Not senior enough" does not always mean you lack seniority. It can mean your resume shows tasks rather than decisions, or that your most senior work sits in the fourth bullet of your second job. The reader formed an impression in a short skim; the feedback reports that impression.

### Recruiters relay it second-hand

When feedback comes through an agency or internal recruiter, it has been summarised at least once. "They wanted more AWS" might have started as "we need someone who has run production workloads on AWS, not just used it". [Recruiter said "not a fit" - what it means](/blog/recruiter-said-not-a-fit-what-it-means) covers how to read these relayed messages.

### A single data point can mislead

One manager's preference is not a market signal. If one company says "too technical" and the next says "not technical enough", you are seeing two different roles, not a flaw in your resume.

## Decoding Common Feedback

| Feedback you hear | What it often means | Where to look first |
|---|---|---|
| "Not enough experience with [tool]" | The tool is missing or appears only in the skills list | Do you have bullets that show the tool in use? |
| "We wanted more leadership" | Your bullets show execution, not decisions or influence | Coordination, mentoring, ownership you have not described |
| "Not senior enough" | Scope and impact are unclear | Team size, budget, decision authority, cross-team work |
| "Too junior / too senior for the role" | Title and years mismatch the band | Headline, summary framing, which roles you emphasise |
| "Not enough stakeholder management" | No evidence of working across teams | Bullets involving other departments, clients, leadership |
| "Resume was hard to follow" | Structure, length or clarity problem | Layout, bullet length, section order |
| "Didn't see relevant industry experience" | Domain vocabulary missing | Translation of industry terms |
| "Other candidates were stronger" | Often no specific resume flaw | Possibly fit or timing, not wording |

The last row matters. Not all feedback points to a resume problem.

### Feedback that is really about another document

Sometimes the reader formed their impression from something other than your resume. "Didn't seem very interested in the role" often comes from a generic cover letter, not the resume - [common cover letter mistakes](/blog/cover-letter-mistakes-2026) covers the usual culprits. "Your experience seemed different from what we expected" can mean your LinkedIn and resume tell different stories; a hiring manager who checks both notices when titles or dates disagree. [Keeping LinkedIn and your tailored resume in sync](/blog/linkedin-resume-tailoring-sync) covers what should match. Before rewriting your resume, check whether the fix belongs in one of those.

## Three Kinds of Fix

Once decoded, sort each piece of feedback into one of three categories.

### Type 1: You have it, but it is not on the page

The evidence exists in your experience but is missing from your resume, usually because you cut it for space or never thought it mattered.

**Fix:** add it - as a bullet with evidence, in the role where it happened, near the top.

### Type 2: You have it, but it is framed weakly

The evidence is on the page but buried, vague, or phrased in terms the reader did not recognise.

**Fix:** move it up and reframe it. Change the verb, add scale, use the posting's vocabulary.

### Type 3: You genuinely do not have it

The experience is not in your history.

**Fix:** not a resume edit. Options are gaining the experience, targeting roles that do not need it, or acknowledging the gap honestly and showing how you are closing it. [Missing a required skill: what to do](/blog/missing-required-skill-what-to-do) covers the honest options.

| Type | Resume fixable? | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 - Have it, not shown | Yes | Add a bullet with evidence |
| 2 - Have it, weakly framed | Yes | Move up, rewrite with scale and vocabulary |
| 3 - Don't have it | No | Gain it, retarget, or acknowledge honestly |

## Worked Example 1: "We Wanted More Leadership"

**Situation:** Aakash, a senior software engineer, was rejected from a tech lead role. Feedback: "Strong technically, but we wanted to see more leadership."

**Decoding:** Does he have leadership evidence? He mentored two junior engineers, ran the team's design reviews for a year, and led the migration of a service - but none of this is on his resume. Type 1 and Type 2.

> **Before (top bullet):** "Developed microservices in Go handling 2M daily requests."
>
> **After (top bullets):**
> - "Led the migration of the payments service to Go - planned the rollout, split work across 4 engineers and ran weekly reviews; handled 2M daily requests with zero downtime at cutover."
> - "Ran the team's design review process for a year and mentored two junior engineers, both promoted within 18 months."

His summary shifts from "Senior engineer specialising in distributed systems" to "Senior engineer who leads service migrations and mentors the team - most recently the payments migration across 4 engineers."

For the fuller version of this move, see [moving from senior IC to first management role](/blog/first-management-role-resume).

## Worked Example 2: A Recruiter Asks for Changes Before Submitting

**Situation:** Pooja is applying through an agency for a marketing analytics role. The recruiter says: "The hiring manager really cares about attribution modelling and SQL. Can you make that more visible before I send it over?"

This is the most valuable feedback there is - it comes before the decision. Pooja has built a multi-touch attribution model in SQL, but it is her fourth bullet under her current role.

> **Before (4th bullet):** "Supported marketing measurement initiatives using data tools."
>
> **After (1st bullet):** "Built a multi-touch attribution model in SQL across 6 paid channels, replacing last-click reporting; the team reallocated about 15% of paid budget based on its findings."

She also moves SQL to the front of her skills line and adds "attribution modelling" to her summary. The whole update takes 15 minutes, and she sends it back the same day.

When a recruiter asks for a resume update, speed matters - they may be submitting several candidates. [Resume matching: recruiter vs direct application](/blog/resume-matching-recruiter-vs-direct-application) covers how agency submissions work.

## Worked Example 3: "Not Enough Hands-On Experience"

**Situation:** Rahul, an operations manager, was rejected from a supply chain analyst role. Feedback: "We need someone more hands-on with data - Python, forecasting models."

**Decoding:** He uses Excel extensively but has never used Python or built statistical forecasts. This is Type 3.

**What he does not do:** add Python to his skills or reframe Excel work as "forecasting models".

**What he does:**

- Keeps his strongest honest evidence visible: "Built the weekly demand-planning sheet in Excel for 400 SKUs, used by 3 warehouses."
- Starts a Python forecasting course and a small project using public retail data.
- Decides to target operations analyst roles that value Excel modelling for now, while building the Python skills for the next round.

A resume edit would not have fixed this. Recognising that quickly saved him from misrepresenting himself. [Tailoring for a stretch role](/blog/tailoring-for-a-stretch-role) covers applying to roles where you are partway there.

## Base Resume or Just This Version?

Not all feedback should change your base resume.

### Change the base when feedback repeats

If two or three companies give similar feedback - "we couldn't see your impact", "hard to tell what you owned" - that is a signal about your resume itself. Update the base so every future version benefits.

### Change only one version for company-specific feedback

If one company wants heavy emphasis on a tool or domain that others do not, apply it only to that company's version. Adding it everywhere can crowd out what other employers want.

| Feedback pattern | Where to apply it |
|---|---|
| Same theme from 2+ companies | Base resume |
| Structural ("hard to follow", "too long") | Base resume |
| Specific to one company's tech stack or domain | That company's version only |
| Recruiter asks for emphasis before submission | That submission's version |
| "Other candidates were stronger" | Neither - no resume change indicated |

For keeping base and company versions organised, see [managing multiple resume versions](/blog/managing-multiple-resume-versions).

## How to Ask for More Useful Feedback

If you get a vague rejection, a short, polite follow-up sometimes gets specifics - especially from recruiters you have spoken to.

### A message that works

> "Thanks for letting me know. If you're able to share, was there anything specific on my resume or in my experience that the team felt was missing? It would help me target future applications."

Keep it short, never argue, and accept "we can't share more" gracefully. Many companies have policies against detailed feedback.

### Questions to ask in a live conversation

If you are speaking to a recruiter by phone, specific questions get specific answers:

1. "Was it a particular skill or level of experience they were looking for?"
2. "Was there anything on my resume that raised a question?"
3. "Would you consider me for a different role on the team?"

## Common Mistakes

### Rewriting everything after one comment

If the feedback was about leadership, your technical bullets were probably fine. Change what the feedback touches; leave the rest.

### Adding claims to match feedback

"They wanted more Kubernetes" does not mean you should add Kubernetes to your resume if you have only read about it. That is the fastest route to a failed technical interview. See [tailoring without lying](/blog/tailoring-without-lying).

### Arguing with the feedback

Replying "but I do have leadership experience - it's in bullet four" rarely changes the decision. Use the feedback on your next application instead.

### Ignoring structural feedback

"Hard to read" or "too long" is easy to dismiss as preference, but it affects every application. Check length with [one page or two: the decision](/blog/resume-length-one-page-or-two-decision) and bullet length with [bullet points too long for ATS](/blog/bullet-points-too-long-ats).

### Treating a single rejection as a verdict

One manager's view is one data point. Look for patterns across applications before overhauling your base. A simple log - company, role, feedback in their words, your decoded type - makes patterns visible after five or six applications instead of leaving you to remember them.

## Reapplying After Feedback

If the feedback came from a company you want to reapply to, the rewritten resume should visibly answer it - that is exactly what the reviewer will look for next time. Timing, what to change and how to frame the reapplication are covered in [reapplying to the same company](/blog/reapplying-to-same-company).

### Show the gap closing

If the feedback named a Type 3 gap, a reapplication months later should show progress: a project, a course, new responsibilities at work. Put that evidence near the top, where a returning reviewer will see it first.

## How This Fits With Tailoring

Feedback-driven tailoring follows the same logic as normal tailoring, with the feedback acting as a very specific JD.

1. Write down the feedback exactly as you received it.
2. Decode it using the table above.
3. Sort each point into Type 1, 2 or 3.
4. For Types 1 and 2, add or reframe evidence near the top.
5. For Type 3, make a plan outside the resume.
6. Decide: base resume or single version?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I use feedback from a hiring manager to improve my resume?

Decode what the feedback means, then sort it: evidence you have but did not show, evidence you framed weakly, or experience you lack. Add or reframe the first two near the top of your resume; handle the third outside the resume.

### What should I do when a recruiter asks me to update my resume?

Do it quickly - ideally the same day. Move the evidence they asked about to the top of your most relevant role, add it to your summary, and make sure related skills lead your skills line.

### Should I change my resume after every rejection?

No. Change your base resume only when feedback repeats across companies or is structural. Apply one-off, company-specific feedback only to that company's version.

### How do I ask for feedback after a rejection?

Send a short, polite message asking whether anything specific in your resume or experience was missing, and accept it graciously if they cannot share more.

### What if the feedback is about experience I don't have?

That is not a resume problem. Gain the experience, target roles that do not require it, or acknowledge the gap honestly in applications and interviews. Do not add claims to match the feedback.

### What does "not senior enough" mean on a resume?

Often that your resume shows tasks rather than decisions and scope. Add team size, budget, decision authority and cross-team impact to your recent bullets before assuming you truly lack seniority.

### Can I reapply to a company after getting feedback?

Usually yes, after a reasonable gap, with a resume that visibly addresses the feedback. See [reapplying to the same company](/blog/reapplying-to-same-company).

### Is recruiter feedback reliable?

It is useful but often summarised and second-hand. Treat it as a strong clue about what the reader did not see, and look for patterns across multiple companies.

## What to Do Now

Write down the feedback you received word for word. Decode it with the table above and sort each point into Type 1, 2 or 3. For the fixable types, find the evidence in your history and move it to the top of the relevant role.

Then decide whether the change belongs in your base resume or just this version.
