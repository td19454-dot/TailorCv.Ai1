## Key Takeaways

- **The decision is largely made before you apply**, which means the preparation matters more than the application.
- **Take work at the next level** deliberately rather than waiting to be given it.
- **Document as you go.** Evidence reconstructed later is weaker and frequently incomplete.
- **Visibility is not self-promotion.** Decision-makers cannot advocate for work they never saw.
- **Ask what the bar is** explicitly, rather than assuming you know.

## Building the evidence

What to do in the months beforehand.

**Ask directly what the criteria are.** A conversation with your manager about what separates your level from the next one is the single highest-value step, and most people never have it.

**Volunteer for work above your grade**, particularly the unglamorous coordination and ownership tasks that nobody else wants, since those are where the next level actually lives.

**Take responsibility for outcomes**, not just tasks, which is usually the real difference between adjacent levels.

**Build relationships across teams**, because promotion decisions frequently involve people outside your immediate line.

**Solve a visible problem** that someone senior cares about, which does more for a case than a year of steady delivery.

**Mentor or support someone junior**, which is expected at higher levels and easy to arrange.

**And write it down as it happens**, in a running document, because six months of accumulated evidence is impossible to reconstruct from memory afterwards.

## Making the work visible

The part people find uncomfortable.

| Approach | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Regular written updates** | Creates a record others can see |
| **Credit others publicly** | Makes visibility feel collaborative |
| **Present your own work** | Direct exposure to decision-makers |
| **Document decisions, not just output** | Shows judgement rather than throughput |
| **Ask for feedback in writing** | Produces evidence in someone else's words |
| **Share what you learned** | Positions you as a source rather than a doer |

**Visibility is a requirement rather than a vanity.** A manager arguing your case in a calibration meeting needs specific examples, and they can only use what they know about.

**Written updates are the least awkward mechanism**, because they are routine, they are useful to the reader, and they create a record without any moment of self-advocacy.

**And crediting others generously** makes the whole thing easier, since a person who consistently names collaborators is not read as self-promoting when they describe their own work.

## What separates adjacent levels

The distinctions that actually appear in criteria.

**Scope of ownership.** Junior levels own tasks, the next own outcomes, and above that own areas with ongoing responsibility for their health.

**Ambiguity tolerance.** Higher levels are given less defined problems and expected to define them, which is frequently the real test.

**Influence without authority.** The ability to move work forward through people who do not report to you is a consistent marker of seniority.

**Judgement under uncertainty.** Being trusted to decide without escalating, and knowing when escalation is correct.

**Developing others**, formally or informally, which is expected at most senior levels regardless of whether the role is a management one.

**And the horizon you work on.** Junior roles deliver this sprint, senior roles shape this quarter, and above that people are working on next year.

**Read your organisation's actual framework** where one exists, since these general patterns take specific forms and the specific form is what you will be assessed against.

## When the answer stays no

Recognising an honest ceiling.

**Ask for the specific gap** rather than accepting general encouragement, since vague positivity without progress is the most common way people lose years.

**Check whether the role above exists.** In small or flat organisations there may simply be no position to move into, which is a structural fact rather than a judgement on you.

**Notice whether others are moving** while you are not, which is genuine information about how you are seen.

**Test the market.** An external offer clarifies your value quickly, though using one as leverage carries real risks and frequently damages the relationship.

**Consider a lateral move first**, since a sideways step into a growing team is a common and effective route to a promotion that was blocked elsewhere.

**And accept that leaving is sometimes the answer.** Many people reach the next level fastest by changing employer, and treating that as failure rather than as a normal career mechanism costs time.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: There is no clear promotion process

Ask for one, or at least for the criteria. In organisations without a formal ladder, promotions go to people who made the case explicitly rather than to those who waited.

### Scenario 2: You have been at the level for years

Something is not working, and continuing unchanged will not fix it. Ask directly what is missing, and if the answer is unsatisfying, the honest conclusion may be that the move is external.

### Scenario 3: A budget freeze blocks all promotions

Keep building the evidence and get the agreement in principle documented. Freezes end, and the person with a written case already accepted moves first.

> **Tip:** Keep a running document with a dated entry every time you do something above your level. It takes two minutes a week, and it converts the hardest part of a promotion case — remembering what you actually did — into a solved problem.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Ask what the criteria actually are
- Take next-level work deliberately
- Own outcomes rather than tasks
- Keep a dated evidence record
- Establish regular written updates
- Agree a timeline with your manager

### Don't

- Wait to be noticed
- Assume good work speaks for itself
- Reconstruct evidence from memory
- Avoid visibility out of discomfort
- Accept vague answers about criteria
- Stay indefinitely without progress

## Common Mistakes

- **Waiting to be noticed.** Decision-makers use what they know.
- **No documented evidence.** Memory produces a weaker case.
- **Avoiding visibility.** It is a requirement, not vanity.
- **Never asking the criteria.** You optimise for the wrong things.
- **Tasks rather than outcomes.** Ownership is the level difference.
- **Staying too long.** At some point external is the honest answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I build a case for promotion?

Ask what the criteria are, take work at the next level deliberately, own outcomes rather than tasks, and document everything as it happens rather than reconstructing it later.

### Does good work get noticed automatically?

Rarely. Managers argue promotion cases using specific examples they know about, so work nobody saw cannot be used however good it was.

### How do I make my work visible without self-promoting?

Regular written updates are the least awkward mechanism, since they are routine and useful to the reader. Crediting collaborators generously makes the rest feel natural.

### What if I have been at my level for years?

Ask directly what is missing. If the answer is vague or unchanging, the honest conclusion is frequently that the next move is external rather than internal.

### How long should building a case take?

Around six months of deliberate work is a realistic minimum for most levels, since you need enough examples of operating above your grade to be convincing.

### What if promotions are frozen?

Keep building evidence and get an agreement in principle documented. Freezes end, and the person whose case is already accepted moves first.
### What actually separates one level from the next?

Scope of ownership, tolerance for ambiguity, influence without authority, judgement under uncertainty, developing others, and the time horizon you work on. Read your organisation's own framework for the specific form.

### What if I keep being told no?

Ask for the specific gap rather than accepting encouragement, check whether the role above actually exists, notice whether others are moving, and consider a lateral move or an external one.
