## Key Takeaways

- Mock tests build the two skills untimed study cannot — pacing under pressure, and deciding what to skip.
- Doing the test is a fraction of the value; the analysis afterwards is where improvement comes from.
- Categorise every wrong answer by cause, because concept gaps, calculation slips, misreadings and timeouts need different fixes.
- Simulate real conditions properly — timed, no phone, no calculator, no pausing.
- Two or three full mocks a week in the final month beats daily topic practice at that stage.

Most students treat mock tests as a way to measure preparation. They are better understood as a way to build it — specifically the parts that topic-wise study cannot touch.

You can know how to solve every question in a paper and still fail it, because knowing and knowing-under-a-clock are different skills. Mocks are how you develop the second one.

The other half of the value comes after the test, and it is the half most people skip entirely.

This guide covers running them and analysing them.

## What mocks actually build

**Pacing.** Knowing when you are behind, and adjusting, without checking the clock every question.

**The skip decision.** Recognising within about ten seconds that a question will take three minutes, and leaving it. This is the highest-value skill in any aptitude round and it can only be built under time pressure — see [the campus placement aptitude test guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/campus-placement-aptitude-test-guide).

**Stamina.** Sustained concentration across a full paper is genuinely tiring, and the last section suffers if you have never practised the whole thing.

**Section switching.** Moving between quantitative, logical and verbal without carrying frustration from one into the next.

**Calm.** The first real test feels different. It feels less different after ten mocks.

## Running them properly

Simulation only works if it is actually simulated.

**Full length, in one sitting.** No pausing, no breaks between sections unless the real test has them.

**Strictly timed**, including sectional limits if the real test uses them.

**No calculator**, since most placement tests do not permit one.

**Phone in another room.** Notifications destroy the concentration you are trying to train.

**Same time of day** as your actual test where you know it, because performance varies through the day more than people expect.

**Rough paper only**, as the real test provides.

**Do not look anything up mid-test**, however tempting. A mock you paused to check a formula measures nothing.

## The analysis that makes it work

This is where the improvement actually happens, and it takes longer than the test itself.

**Categorise every wrong answer by cause:**

**1. Concept gap.** You did not know how to solve it. → Study that topic.

**2. Calculation error.** You knew the method and got the arithmetic wrong. → Practise mental calculation speed and accuracy, not the topic.

**3. Misread the question.** You solved a slightly different problem. → Slow down on reading, underline what is actually asked.

**4. Ran out of time.** You could have solved it. → A pacing problem, not a knowledge problem.

**These four need completely different fixes**, and students who do not separate them respond to every wrong answer by studying more theory — which only addresses the first category.

**Also review the ones you got right by guessing.** A correct answer you were unsure of is a concept gap the score does not show.

**Keep an error log.** One line per mistake with its category. Reviewing your own recurring errors is worth more than another hundred fresh questions.

## When to do them

**Four months out:** topic-wise practice, untimed, building accuracy first. Mocks are premature.

**Two to three months out:** start mixed practice, timed at the question level. Recognising which topic a question belongs to is itself a skill that topic-wise study does not build.

**One month out:** two or three full mocks per week, with full analysis after each.

**Final week:** one or two, plus error log review. No new topics.

**Do not do mocks daily in the final month.** Analysis takes longer than the test, and a mock you do not analyse is largely wasted time.

## Company-specific practice

**Ask your placement cell what specific companies asked.** Many maintain records of test patterns from previous years, and this is the most underused resource on campus.

**Ask seniors directly.** Section composition, timing, whether there was negative marking, whether the test was adaptive.

**Match your practice to the pattern.** Preparing for a heavy quantitative test when your target company weights logical reasoning is wasted effort.

**Know the negative marking rule before the day**, because it completely changes your guessing strategy — see [the campus placement coding round guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/campus-placement-coding-round-guide) for the equivalent in coding rounds.

## Common Mistakes

**Doing mocks without analysing them.** The analysis is where improvement happens, and it takes longer than the test itself.

**Treating every wrong answer as a concept gap.** Calculation slips, misreadings and timeouts need entirely different fixes.

**Pausing or looking things up mid-test.** It measures nothing and trains nothing.

**Practising with a calculator.** Most placement tests do not allow one, so you are building a habit you cannot use.

**Ignoring questions you guessed correctly.** They are concept gaps your score is hiding.

**Starting mocks too early.** Before accuracy exists, timed practice mostly measures frustration.

**Doing them daily in the final month.** Without time to analyse, you are repeating mistakes rather than fixing them.

**Not checking the company's actual test pattern.** Placement cells and seniors frequently have it, and matching your practice to it is free advantage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many mock tests should I do?

Two or three full mocks per week in the final month, each with proper analysis afterwards. More than that leaves no time to fix what they reveal.

### When should I start doing mocks?

Around two to three months out, after topic-wise accuracy is reasonable. Before that, timed practice mostly measures frustration.

### What is the most important part of a mock?

The analysis. Categorising each wrong answer as a concept gap, calculation error, misreading or timeout is what turns a score into an improvement plan.

### Should I use a calculator while practising?

No. Most placement tests do not permit one, so practising with it builds a habit you cannot use on the day.

### How do I improve my speed?

Mental calculation practice — tables, squares, fraction-to-percentage conversions — plus the skip decision, which only develops under real time pressure.

### Should I review questions I got right?

Review the ones you guessed. A correct answer you were unsure about is a concept gap your score does not reveal.

### How do I find out a company's test pattern?

Ask your placement cell, which frequently keeps records, and ask seniors who sat the test last year. It is the most underused resource available to you.

## Make This Practical

Run mocks as actual simulations — full length, strictly timed, no calculator, phone in another room, nothing looked up mid-test. A paused mock trains nothing, and the conditions are the point.

Then spend longer on the analysis than on the test. Categorise every wrong answer as a concept gap, a calculation error, a misreading or a timeout, because those four need entirely different responses and treating them all as "study more" only fixes the first.

Finally, aim your practice at what you will actually face. Ask your placement cell and your seniors for the specific test pattern of your target companies — section composition, timing, negative marking — and match your mocks to it rather than practising generically.
