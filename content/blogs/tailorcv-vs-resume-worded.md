## Round 1: General Score vs Job-Specific Match

This is the distinction that decides everything else, so it is worth being careful about.

**Resume Worded scores your resume on its own terms.** It checks for things that are broadly true of strong resumes — quantified achievements, strong action verbs, section completeness, readability, common weaknesses. It does this without knowing what job you are applying to, and it does not need to.

**TailorCV scores your resume against one posting.** You paste the job description, and the [ATS analysis](/ats-analysis) reports which of that posting's stated requirements your resume evidences and which it does not. Change the posting and the score changes, because the target changed.

Neither is more correct. They answer different questions:

- *"Is my resume any good?"* → a general score
- *"Will this specific application get filtered?"* → a match score

Our [ATS score vs resume score explainer](/blog/ats-score-vs-resume-score) goes deeper on why these two numbers disagree and why that is expected rather than a bug.

## Round 2: Feedback vs Rewriting

Resume Worded tells you what is weak — a bullet lacks a metric, a verb is passive, a section is thin. Acting on that advice is your job, and doing it well takes time and judgement.

TailorCV does the rewriting step. The [tailoring flow](/modify-cv) rewrites bullets to carry the posting's language, but only where your existing experience supports the claim. It will not add a skill your resume shows no evidence for — that constraint is deliberate and enforced, because a resume that claims something you cannot discuss in an interview is worse than one that omits it.

The honest trade: advice teaches you to write better resumes over time. Rewriting gets this application out today. Which you want depends on whether you are building a skill or meeting a deadline.

## Round 3: LinkedIn — Where Resume Worded Wins

Resume Worded reviews LinkedIn profiles, not just resumes. It scores the profile and gives specific feedback on headline, summary and experience sections.

**We do not have this.** If your problem is that recruiters are not finding you, that is a LinkedIn problem, not a resume problem, and Resume Worded addresses it directly while we do not. We have [written guidance](/blog/linkedin-profile-vs-resume-differences) on the differences between the two documents, but guidance is not the same as a tool that scores your profile.

If inbound recruiter interest is what you are missing, that alone may decide this comparison.

## Round 4: Where Each One Fits in a Job Hunt

A rough sequence, which is how we would actually recommend using them:

**Before you start applying** — run a general check. Is the resume structurally sound, are bullets quantified, is anything obviously weak? Resume Worded is built for exactly this, and a single pass will catch problems that would otherwise repeat across every application.

**While applying to a specific role** — the general questions are already answered. Now the question is whether *this* resume matches *this* posting, and that needs the job description in hand. That is [what we do](/ats-analysis).

**After several rejections** — the [match score](/blog/resume-job-description-match-percentage) usually explains more than a general score, because it shows the specific requirements you failed to evidence.

Using both in that order is a genuinely reasonable strategy, and we would rather say so than pretend one tool covers everything.
