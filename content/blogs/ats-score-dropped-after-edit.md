## Key Takeaways

- A lower score after editing is often an artefact of scoring mechanics, not evidence that your resume got worse.
- The most common causes are dilution, a changed comparison, a lost keyword you did not notice, and a broken layout.
- Scores are proportional - adding good content can lower the percentage while improving the document.
- Check what changed in the breakdown, not the headline number.
- If the edit made the resume clearer to a human and the drop is small, keep the edit.

You rewrote three bullets, tightened your summary, and the score went down four points. The instinct is to undo everything. Usually that is the wrong response.

Scores move for reasons that have little to do with document quality. Here are the six that account for nearly all of it.

## 1. You Diluted the Proportion

Most scoring is proportional: relevant content measured against total content. Add 80 words of good material and the relevant share of your resume falls, even though nothing relevant was removed.

This is the single most common cause of a confusing drop. You genuinely improved the resume; the denominator grew.

**How to tell:** your resume got longer and no specific terms disappeared. If so, the drop is arithmetic, not a warning.

## 2. You Removed a Word Without Realising

Rewriting for clarity often quietly deletes a term the scanner was matching.

> **Before:** Led stakeholder management across four business units, running monthly governance reviews.
>
> **After:** Led work with four teams, running monthly reviews.

The second is cleaner English. It also dropped "stakeholder management" and "governance" - and if the posting used both, the score falls accordingly.

**How to tell:** compare the missing-terms list from before and after. A term that moved from found to missing is your answer.

The fix is not to revert. It is to write the clean version and keep the specific term:

> **Better:** Led stakeholder management across four teams, running monthly governance reviews.

## 3. You Scored Against a Different Posting

Two postings for the same job title weight different things. If you re-scanned against a new posting after editing, you changed both variables at once and the comparison tells you nothing.

**How to tell:** you used a different job description. Re-scan the edited resume against the original posting to get a clean before-and-after.

## 4. Your Edit Broke the Layout

Adding a column, converting a list to a table, or moving contact details into the header can make whole sections unreadable to the parser. The text is on the page; the scanner no longer receives it.

This produces the dramatic drops - fifteen points or more, with several sections suddenly missing.

**How to tell:** copy-paste your PDF into a plain text editor. If a section you can see is absent from the paste, that is the cause, and it is worth fixing regardless of the score.

## 5. You Moved Evidence Down the Page

Some tools weight the top of the document more heavily. Relocating your skills section below your experience, or pushing a key achievement into a later role, can lower the score without removing anything.

**How to tell:** nothing is missing, the length is similar, and the only change was ordering.

Whether to revert depends on the reason you moved it. If the new order serves the human reader better, a small scoring cost is usually worth paying.

## 6. You Replaced a Specific Term With a General One

Scanners match specific named things more reliably than categories. "Managed cloud infrastructure" is better English than "Managed AWS EC2 and S3," and scores worse against a posting that names AWS.

**How to tell:** you generalised deliberately for readability. Keep the specific term where the posting names it; generalise elsewhere.

## A Short Diagnostic

In order:

**1. Did the resume get longer?** If yes and nothing vanished, the drop is dilution. Ignore it.

**2. Did any term move from found to missing?** If yes, reinstate that term inside your improved sentence.

**3. Did you change the posting?** If yes, re-scan against the original before concluding anything.

**4. Does the copy-paste test show missing sections?** If yes, fix the layout - this is a real problem.

**5. None of the above?** The drop is probably noise. A few points either way is within the range where the number tells you nothing useful.

## The Judgment That Matters

A score is a proxy. The question is not "did the number go up" but "is this resume more likely to get a human to call me."

A rewrite that made your achievements concrete, cut two lines of padding and cost you three points is a good trade. A rewrite that stuffed four extra tools into your skills list and gained eight points is a bad one, whatever the number says.

Keep edits that make the document clearer and better evidenced. Reinstate specific terms the posting actually uses. Then stop scoring and send it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I undo my edits if the score dropped?**
Only if you can identify a specific cause worth reverting - a lost keyword or a broken layout. A small unexplained drop is not a reason to discard genuine improvements.

**How big a drop is worth worrying about?**
A few points is usually noise. A drop of fifteen or more, especially with sections missing, points to a parsing problem worth fixing.

**Why does adding relevant experience lower my score?**
Because scoring is proportional. New content increases the total against which relevance is measured, so the percentage can fall while the resume improves.

**Does a lower score mean fewer interviews?**
Not reliably. The score approximates keyword relevance for one posting; interviews depend on that plus substance, competition and timing.

**Can I get back to my old score and keep the new wording?**
Usually yes - keep the clearer sentences and reinstate the specific terms the posting names inside them. That recovers most of the difference.

## Compare the Two Versions Properly

The fastest way to settle this is to scan both versions against the same posting and read the missing-terms lists side by side. That turns a mysterious number into a specific, fixable list.
