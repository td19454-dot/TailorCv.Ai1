## Key Takeaways

- Express Entry ranks candidates on the Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS), a 1,200-point scale split into core human capital factors (max 500), skill transferability (max 100), and additional points like provincial nomination (up to 600) or a job offer (50-200).
- Language testing is the single most improvable score component for most new grads — a jump from CLB 7 to CLB 9 in each of the four bands can add 40-50+ points, often more than a year of extra work experience.
- Canadian work experience only counts if the [job's NOC category qualifies](/blog/canada-noc-codes-explained-for-graduates) as TEER 0-3; a role outside that classification adds zero points no matter how long you hold it.
- Provincial nomination alone adds 600 points, which is enough to guarantee an invitation regardless of your base score — worth pursuing actively, not as a fallback.
- Draw cutoffs move with the pool, not on a fixed schedule — recent general draws have landed anywhere from the low 400s to mid 500s depending on category and timing, so check IRCC's current results before assuming a target.

## The actual point breakdown

Express Entry manages three federal programs — Federal Skilled Worker, Canadian Experience Class, and Federal Skilled Trades — and ranks everyone eligible for any of them on the same 1,200-point CRS scale.

**Core human capital factors (no spouse), max 500:**

| Factor | Max points | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 110 | Peaks at 20-29, then loses roughly 5-7 points per year, hits 0 at 45 |
| Education | 150 | PhD = 150, Master's = 135, two+ post-secondary credentials = 128, bachelor's = 120, one-year post-secondary = 90 |
| First official language | 136 | Full points require CLB 9 or higher in all four bands (reading, writing, listening, speaking) |
| Second official language | 24 | English/French bilingualism, capped much lower than the first language |
| Canadian work experience | 80 | 1 year = 40, 2-3 years = 53, 4-5 years = 64, 5+ years = 80 |

**Skill transferability, max 100** — combinations of education + language, or education + Canadian work experience, or foreign work experience + language. This is where a strong language score compounds: it feeds both the core factor above and this bucket.

**Additional points, up to 600** — a provincial nomination is worth 600 on its own; a valid job offer in a senior managerial NOC is worth up to 200 (most other qualifying offers are worth 50); Canadian post-secondary education adds 15-30; French-language ability with a CLB 7+ score adds 25-50; a sibling already living in Canada adds 15.

## Why language is the lever most people leave on the table

A 24-year-old new grad with a Canadian bachelor's degree, one year of qualifying Canadian work experience, and CLB 7 across the board scores roughly: 100 (age) + 120 (education) + 108 (first language at CLB 7, below the 136 max) + 40 (work experience) ≈ the low-to-mid 400s in core factors before transferability points.

The accepted English tests are IELTS General Training and CELPIP-General — not the Academic version of IELTS many students already have from university admissions, which isn't accepted for this purpose, a mismatch that trips up a surprising number of applicants who assume any IELTS score transfers over. **Retest at CLB 9 and that same profile's language points alone can rise by 25-30+**, before even counting the transferability-bucket boost from pairing a stronger language score with your education and work experience. That single retest, one weekend of preparation and a test fee, routinely moves a profile more than an entire additional year of work experience would (worth 13 points, from the 1-year to 2-3-year band). **Book the retest before assuming your existing score is good enough — check your actual band-by-band breakdown on your test results, not just the overall grade**, since CRS scores each of the four bands separately and one weak band caps your total even if the others are strong.

## Why your first job's NOC category is the whole game

Canadian work experience only adds points if it falls under TEER 0, 1, 2, or 3 in the National Occupational Classification — [covered in full with real examples in the NOC guide](/blog/canada-noc-codes-explained-for-graduates). A retail or food-service role, however many hours you work, adds zero to this factor because it sits outside that band. Before accepting an offer on your [post-graduation work permit](/blog/canada-pgwp-for-new-grads), check the actual NOC code the employer will report for the role — ask HR directly if the job posting doesn't state it — because this determines whether the year you're about to spend counts toward your score at all.

## Draws move — plan around a range, not a number

The score needed for an invitation is set by whoever ranks lowest among the candidates a given draw actually invites, so it rises and falls with how many people are in the pool and how the draw is targeted. General draws and category-specific draws (French-language proficiency, healthcare, trades, and similar targeted streams) run on separate cutoffs, and category draws have often invited candidates at meaningfully lower scores than general draws in the same period. **Check IRCC's published draw-history page for the current numbers when you're actually close to applying** — a figure that was accurate when you first researched this can be a hundred points off eighteen months later in either direction.

## Provincial nomination as an active strategy, not a backup

[Provincial nominee programs](/blog/canada-provincial-nominee-programs-graduates) add 600 points outright, which puts a nominated candidate above essentially every general-draw cutoff on record. If your field, your province of study, or your work experience lines up with a specific province's in-demand occupation list, applying to that stream in parallel with building your federal profile is usually a faster path than waiting on federal draws alone.

## Building your profile over your first two years

Run your own score on IRCC's official CRS calculator (searchable directly on canada.ca, not a third-party estimator) now, even if you're a year or two from applying — it turns an abstract future number into something concrete you can move. The three actions with the best return for a new grad are, in order: retest your weakest language band, confirm your job's NOC category qualifies before you accept it, and check whether a provincial stream matches your profile. Each is independent, and doing more than one compounds, so treat this as an ongoing checklist rather than a one-time calculation you run and forget about.

## Common misunderstandings worth clearing up early

**"Any Canadian job counts toward my score."** Only if its NOC falls in TEER 0-3 — [see the classification guide](/blog/canada-noc-codes-explained-for-graduates) before assuming an offer helps your case.

**"My overall language grade is what matters."** CRS scores each of the four bands (reading, writing, listening, speaking) separately and takes the lowest-scoring band as a real constraint — a high overall average with one weak band still caps your points.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not checking a job's NOC category.** Before accepting it. When the difference between a qualifying and non-qualifying role is the entire Canadian-work-experience factor.
- **Treating an overall language "pass" as sufficient.** Without checking the individual band scores CRS actually uses.
- **Not retesting a weak language score.** When it's typically the highest-return, lowest-effort action available.
- **Assuming a historical draw cutoff still applies.** Instead of checking IRCC's current draw-history page before applying.
- **Treating provincial nomination as a last resort.** When its 600 points make it worth pursuing in parallel from the start.
- **Waiting until close to applying to run the CRS calculator.** Losing years where language and job-category decisions could have shaped a stronger score.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the maximum possible CRS score?

1,200 total — up to 500 from core human capital factors, up to 100 from skill transferability, and up to 600 from additional points like provincial nomination.

### How much does Canadian work experience actually add?

Up to 80 points at 5+ years, but it scales: roughly 40 for one year, 53 for two to three, 64 for four to five. It only counts at all if the role's NOC classification is TEER 0-3.

### Is retesting my language score worth the fee and the weekend?

For most people, yes — moving from CLB 7 to CLB 9 across the bands can add 25-30+ points directly, plus more through the skill-transferability bucket, often outweighing what an extra year of work experience would add.

### Does a job offer guarantee points?

Only a valid offer meeting specific criteria adds points — up to 200 for a senior managerial NOC, 50 for most other qualifying offers. Not every job offer qualifies.

### What's a realistic score to target?

There's no fixed number — recent draws have invited candidates anywhere from roughly the low 400s to mid 500s depending on whether it's a general or category-specific draw. Check IRCC's current draw-history page rather than a number from when you first researched this.

### Is provincial nomination worth pursuing if my federal score is already decent?

Yes — it adds 600 points on top of whatever you already have, which clears essentially any general-draw cutoff on record, so it's worth applying to in parallel rather than treating it as a fallback.
