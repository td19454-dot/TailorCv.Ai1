## Key Takeaways

- Most large US campus applications either do not ask for a cover letter or mark it optional, and many recruiters never open it.
- Where it still matters: smaller employers, non-profits, government, academia, and career changers.
- "Optional" is worth doing when you have something the resume cannot carry — otherwise it adds risk without upside.
- A generic letter is worse than none. It signals mass application and gives a reason to reject.
- Half a page is enough, and the opening line does most of the work.

## Who actually reads them

Honest answer: fewer people than the advice industry suggests.

At large employers running structured campus hiring, the first pass is automated and the second is a recruiter working through hundreds of resumes. A cover letter is rarely part of either. Many campus application systems do not even have a field for one.

Where cover letters genuinely still count:

- **Small and mid-sized employers**, where a human reads every application
- **Non-profits and mission-driven organisations**, where motivation is a real screening criterion
- **Government and public sector**, which often have formal requirements
- **Academia and research**, where a statement of interest is standard
- **Anyone with a story the resume cannot tell** — a career change, a gap, an unusual path, a relocation

## When "optional" means write one

The optional field is a genuine judgement call. Use this test: **do you have something specific that the resume cannot carry?**

Write one when:

- You are applying to a smaller organisation where a person reads applications
- You have a real, non-generic reason for wanting *this* employer
- Your background needs a sentence of explanation — moving cities, changing fields, a non-obvious degree
- You have a connection worth naming: "I spoke with Priya on the platform team about..."

Skip it when:

- The application is one of forty you are sending this week and you have nothing specific to say
- You would be restating the resume in paragraphs
- It is a large employer with a structured process and a required online assessment

A generic letter actively hurts. "I am writing to express my strong interest in this exciting opportunity at your esteemed organisation" tells a reader you sent the same thing everywhere, which is a reason to deprioritise you.

## Structure that works

Half a page. Four short paragraphs at most.

**Open with the specific thing.** Not "I am writing to apply for". Start with the reason you are worth reading:

> I built a transit-delay dashboard last spring that pulled from the same GTFS feeds your routing team works with, and I would like to bring that to your data engineering internship.

**Second paragraph: the strongest evidence.** One piece of work, with what it did and what changed. Not a list — a single concrete thing, explained slightly beyond what the resume can fit.

**Third: why this employer.** Something a competitor's name could not be substituted into. A product, a team, a technical decision they published, a conversation you had.

**Close simply.** Availability, thanks, no elaborate sign-off.

Address a person if you can find one. "Dear Hiring Manager" is fine; "To Whom It May Concern" reads as dated.

## What not to do

**Do not restate the resume.** The reader has it. A letter that lists the same bullets in prose wastes the one chance to add something.

**Do not explain how much you would learn.** The employer is hiring for what you contribute, not for your development. Enthusiasm about learning reads as a request rather than an offer.

**Do not use the phrase "I am a hard worker and a fast learner."** It appears in a large share of student letters and carries no information.

**Do not run to a full page.** Nobody is reading 600 words from a candidate they have not met.

**Do not mention visa status.** Same reasoning as the [US resume format](/blog/usa-resume-format-for-students) — it comes up in the process and does not belong in the pitch.

## The one-per-application problem

The reason most cover letters are generic is that writing a fresh one for every application is impossible at volume, and volume is necessary in [autumn recruiting](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline).

The workable compromise: a fixed structure with two genuinely variable parts — the opening line and the "why this employer" paragraph. Everything else can be stable. That makes a real letter a ten-minute job rather than an hour.

If you cannot write a specific opening and a specific third paragraph for an employer, that is a signal you do not know enough about them to be applying with a letter at all — and probably a signal to spend the time on [researching them properly](/blog/how-to-research-company-before-interview) instead.

## Where the effort is better spent

For most US campus applications, the resume carries almost all the weight. Time spent on cover letters that nobody opens is time not spent on the document that is definitely screened.

The higher-return work: making sure the resume [parses cleanly](/ats-analysis), and [matching its language to each posting](/blog/how-to-match-resume-keywords-to-job-description). That is what determines whether a human ever sees your application.

Use cover letters where they are read. Do not use them as a substitute for a resume that is not working.

## Common Mistakes

- **Sending a generic letter.** Worse than sending none.
- **Restating the resume in prose.** Adds length without information.
- **Writing a full page.** It will not be read to the end.
- **Focusing on what you will gain.** The employer is buying, not donating.
- **Skipping it for small employers.** This is exactly where it counts.
- **Spending hours on letters while the resume fails the ATS screen.** Wrong order of operations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I write one if it says optional?

Only if you have something specific to say — a real reason for this employer, a connection, or context the resume cannot hold. Otherwise skip it.

### How long should it be?

Half a page. Three or four short paragraphs.

### Do I need one for big tech?

Usually not. Those processes are resume, assessment and interview loop. Effort is better spent on the [technical preparation](/blog/usa-technical-interview-newgrad-prep).

### What if I have no relevant experience?

Lead with a project. Something you built and can explain is stronger evidence than employment history for an entry-level role.

### Can I reuse the same letter?

Reuse the structure, not the content. The opening and the "why this employer" paragraph must be specific or the letter works against you.

### Should I attach it or paste it into the email?

If emailing directly, put it in the body — attachments add friction. If uploading through a system, follow whatever the form asks for.
