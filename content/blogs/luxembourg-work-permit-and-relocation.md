## Key Takeaways

- **EU, EEA and Swiss citizens** need no permit — they register with their commune after arriving.
- **Third-country nationals must obtain authorisation to stay before entering Luxembourg**, which is a genuine sequencing difference from many countries.
- The **EU Blue Card** is available and is the main route for qualified graduates with a suitable offer.
- **Cross-border commuting** from France, Belgium and Germany is mainstream and brings its own tax and social security rules.
- **Housing is the binding constraint** for anyone intending to live inside the country.

## The two positions

**EU, EEA and Swiss citizens** have free movement. You arrive, you take a job, and you register your arrival with the commune within the required period, followed by a declaration of registration. There is no permit [application](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day) in the substantive sense.

**Third-country nationals** face a specific and important sequencing rule: **you must apply for and receive an authorisation to stay before entering Luxembourg**. You cannot arrive as a visitor and regularise afterwards. Getting this order wrong is the single most common and most expensive mistake.

**The [employer](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes)'s role.** For salaried work, the employer must generally have advertised the position with ADEM, the national employment agency, and obtained a [certificate](/blog/campus-placement-document-checklist) before the authorisation can be granted. This is Luxembourg's labour market test.

**After the authorisation**, you obtain a [visa](/blog/graduate-work-visa-comparison-usa-uk-canada-singapore) where required, enter, make a declaration of arrival at your commune, undergo a medical check, and apply for your residence permit.

## The routes available

**Salaried worker authorisation.** The general route, tied to a specific employer and occupation initially, with the ADEM advertising requirement.

**EU Blue Card.** For highly qualified workers with a higher education qualification or equivalent [experience](/blog/resume-matching-experienced-professionals) and a salary above the applicable [threshold](/blog/resume-for-h1b-visa-sponsorship). It carries advantages including easier family reunification and mobility within the EU, and it is the route most qualified [graduates](/blog/first-resume-for-gen-z-2026) should be aiming at.

**Highly qualified and specific categories** exist for researchers, intra-corporate transfers, investors and others.

**Graduates of Luxembourg institutions** have a route to remain and seek employment after completing their studies — check the current duration and conditions with the immigration directorate.

**Students** who studied in Luxembourg are generally in a better position than external applicants, as everywhere else in this series.

## The frontalier arrangements

If you commute in rather than living in Luxembourg, a different set of rules governs your life.

**You are a Luxembourg employee living abroad.** Social security is generally payable in Luxembourg as the country of employment, under EU coordination rules.

**Tax is more complicated.** Bilateral treaties between Luxembourg and France, Belgium and Germany determine where employment income is taxed, and each treaty differs.

**Remote working days matter enormously.** Days worked from your country of residence rather than in Luxembourg can, beyond a [threshold](/blog/international-resume-tailoring-guide) set in each treaty, shift taxation of that portion of income to your home country. Those thresholds have been renegotiated in recent years and differ by country.

**This is genuinely complex** and it is one of the few situations in this whole series where professional advice is straightforwardly worth paying for. Getting it wrong produces retrospective tax bills.

**Your employer's HR will know the basics** because a large share of their staff are in the same position — ask them.

**[Healthcare](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist), family [benefits](/blog/questions-to-ask-in-an-interview) and pensions** are all coordinated under EU rules, and generally follow the country of employment, but the detail depends on your family's situation.

## Relocating in practice

The sequence once the permit question is settled.

**Declaration of arrival at your commune** within the required period after arriving. This is the first step and everything else follows it.

**Medical examination**, required for third-country nationals as part of the residence permit process.

**Residence permit application** at the immigration directorate, with the biometrics appointment that follows.

**Social security registration**, giving you access to healthcare through the CNS.

**Bank account**, which generally requires proof of address and your registration.

**Tax card**, issued once you are employed and registered, determining your withholding.

**[Housing](/blog/remote-job-search-guide).** Start early and budget properly — deposits are typically several months' rent and agency commission is common in the private market. Many employers offer temporary accommodation support for international hires; ask, because it is frequently discretionary rather than advertised.

**Register a vehicle** if you bring one, within the required period, which has its own process and cost.

## Family and the longer view

**Family reunification** is available, and Blue Card holders have more favourable conditions than the general salaried route — a concrete reason to aim for that card.

**A spouse's right to work** depends on the permit issued to them; Blue Card family members generally have good access.

**Schooling** is genuinely multilingual and the public system teaches in Luxembourgish, German and French, which is demanding for a child arriving mid-education. International and European schools exist as alternatives.

**Long-term residence** follows a qualifying period of legal residence, with an integration requirement.

**Citizenship** requires longer residence plus a Luxembourgish language test and a civics course. Luxembourg permits dual nationality, which is more generous than several neighbours.

**Check everything with the Guichet.lu portal and the immigration directorate**, which are the authoritative sources and are unusually clear as government sites go.

## Comparing Luxembourg to its neighbours

Useful if you are choosing between the region's options.

**Against Belgium.** Luxembourg pays considerably more and taxes labour less heavily, with worse housing costs. Brussels has the institutions and a broader policy market; Luxembourg has the funds.

**Against France.** Luxembourg [salaries](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) are substantially higher, the process for third-country nationals is stricter on sequencing, and many people resolve it by working in one and living in the other.

**Against Germany.** Germany's market is vastly larger with more variety; Luxembourg is narrower, better paid within its niche, and more English-friendly.

**Against Switzerland.** Switzerland pays more still and is far harder to enter for third-country nationals. Luxembourg, as an EU member, offers Blue Card access and EU mobility that Switzerland does not.

**Against Ireland.** Both are English-working EU fund and services centres. Ireland has a larger and more varied economy and an open [post-study route](/blog/best-countries-international-graduates-2026); Luxembourg pays better within funds and is smaller in every other respect.

**The honest summary:** Luxembourg is exceptional if your field is funds, audit, compliance or the institutions, and thin if it is not.

## Timelines to plan around

**EU citizens:** effectively immediate. Arrive, start, register.

**Third-country authorisation:** allow months, not weeks. The ADEM advertising requirement, the authorisation decision, the visa and the arrival formalities all stack.

**Blue Card applications** follow the same shape but with the qualification and salary conditions assessed.

**Housing:** start looking as early as you can. In a market this tight, a delay here delays everything else.

**Do not resign before the authorisation is granted.** This is the standard advice everywhere and it matters more in a country where you cannot enter and regularise afterwards.

## Costs to budget for

Relocation to Luxembourg has specific upfront costs that surprise people.

**Rental deposit** of typically two to three months, held in a blocked account or via a bank guarantee.

**Agency commission** in the private rental market, commonly around a month's rent plus VAT, paid by the tenant.

**First month's rent** on top of both.

**Vehicle registration and roadworthiness testing** if you bring a car, plus possible customs formalities for non-EU imports.

**Furniture**, since a substantial share of Luxembourg rentals are unfurnished, and unfurnished in continental Europe frequently means no kitchen appliances.

**Health insurance gap cover** for any period before your CNS registration is active.

**Deposits for utilities** in some cases.

**Add it up before you accept.** The total can easily exceed a first month's net salary, and international hires who did not plan for it start their Luxembourg life under avoidable pressure. Ask the employer about relocation support explicitly — many offer it and few advertise it.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are an EU citizen with an offer

Straightforward. Move, start, declare your arrival at the commune within the required period. Say your EU status in the first line of applications so employers know there is nothing to solve.

### Scenario 2: You are a third-country national

Do not travel first. The authorisation to stay must be obtained before you enter, and arriving as a visitor to look for work then regularising is not available. Work backwards from that constraint.

### Scenario 3: You will commute from France or Germany

Get advice on the tax treaty and the remote-working thresholds before you agree a working pattern. This is one of the genuinely complicated areas where a mistake produces a retrospective bill.

> **Tip:** If you are a third-country national, remember the sequence: authorisation first, then entry. Luxembourg does not let you arrive and regularise afterwards, and this ordering catches out people who assume it works like other European countries.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Obtain authorisation before entering if non-EU
- Check Blue Card eligibility for your qualification and salary
- Cost the frontalier option honestly
- Get professional advice on cross-border tax
- Understand remote working day thresholds
- Start the housing search early

### Don't

- Arrive as a visitor intending to regularise
- Assume cross-border tax is simple
- Ignore the ADEM advertising requirement's effect on timelines
- Assume Luxembourg housing is affordable on a high salary
- Agree a remote working pattern before checking the tax effect
- Overlook the graduate route if you studied there

## Common Mistakes

- **Wrong sequence.** Authorisation must come before entry for third-country nationals.
- **Ignoring the ADEM requirement.** It adds time to the employer's side of the process.
- **Assuming frontalier tax is simple.** Three treaties, each different, with thresholds that have changed.
- **Agreeing remote days casually.** Beyond a threshold, taxation of that income can shift countries.
- **Underestimating housing.** It is the binding constraint even on Luxembourg salaries.
- **Missing the Blue Card.** It is more favourable than the general salaried route for qualified graduates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I move to Luxembourg to look for work?

If you are an EU, EEA or Swiss citizen, yes. Third-country nationals must obtain authorisation to stay before entering — arriving as a visitor and regularising is not available.

### Does Luxembourg issue EU Blue Cards?

Yes, and for qualified graduates with a suitable offer it is generally the more favourable route, with easier family reunification and EU mobility advantages.

### What is the ADEM requirement?

Luxembourg's labour market test — employers must generally advertise the position with the national employment agency and obtain a certificate before an authorisation can be granted.

### Where am I taxed if I commute in from France?

It depends on the bilateral treaty and on how many days you work outside Luxembourg. Remote working days beyond a treaty threshold can shift taxation, and this is worth professional advice.

### Is social security paid in Luxembourg or my home country?

Generally in Luxembourg as the country of employment, under EU coordination rules — but family and [pension](/blog/first-90-days-new-job) situations add complexity worth checking.

### Can I stay after studying in Luxembourg?

There is a route to remain and seek employment after completing studies. Check the current duration and conditions with the immigration directorate.
### What do I need to do on arriving in Luxembourg?

Declare your arrival at your commune, undergo the medical examination if you are a third-country national, apply for your residence permit, register for social security, then bank account and tax card.

### Is Luxembourg citizenship realistic?

It requires a qualifying residence period, a Luxembourgish language test and a civics course. Luxembourg permits dual nationality, which is more generous than several of its neighbours.
### How does Luxembourg compare to Belgium or Ireland?

Luxembourg pays better within funds and audit and taxes labour less than Belgium, with worse housing. Ireland is a larger, more varied English-working EU economy with an open post-study route.

### How long does the third-country process take?

Months rather than weeks. The ADEM advertising requirement, the authorisation decision, the visa and arrival formalities all add up — and you cannot enter and regularise afterwards.
### What upfront costs should I budget for in Luxembourg?

A two to three month deposit, agency commission of around a month's rent plus VAT, first month's rent, furniture for an unfurnished flat, and vehicle registration if you bring a car. The total often exceeds a first month's net salary.
