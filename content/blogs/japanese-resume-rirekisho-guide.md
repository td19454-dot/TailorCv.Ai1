Applying for a job in Japan means learning a resume system unlike almost anywhere else. Japanese hiring traditionally uses **two documents**: the *rirekisho* (履歴書), a standardised personal-history form, and the *shokumu-keirekisho* (職務経歴書), a more detailed work-history document. The rirekisho is highly formatted with specific conventions — down to how you write dates and attach your photo — and getting these details right signals respect for Japanese business culture.

This guide explains how to write a Japanese resume in 2026: what the rirekisho and shokumu-keirekisho are, how they differ, the formatting rules that matter, and how foreigners can navigate the system (including when a Western-style resume is acceptable).

## Key Takeaways

- Japanese hiring typically uses **two documents**: the *rirekisho* (standardised personal-history form) and the *shokumu-keirekisho* (detailed work history).
- The **rirekisho follows strict conventions** — a formal photo, specific date formats, and a fixed structure.
- The **shokumu-keirekisho** is where you detail your experience and achievements, more like a Western resume.
- Foreign-facing and global companies in Japan may accept an **English (or Western-style) resume** — see [ATS and English resumes for Japan](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ats-english-resume-japan).
- Neatness, accuracy, and cultural fit matter as much as content.

## The Two Documents: Rirekisho and Shokumu-keirekisho

### Rirekisho (履歴書) — The Standard Form

The rirekisho is a standardised form (available as templates and increasingly filled digitally) that captures your personal and educational history in a fixed layout. It typically includes:

- **Personal details:** name (with furigana reading), date of birth, address, contact, and a formal photo.
- **Education and work history:** listed chronologically in a prescribed format.
- **Qualifications and licences:** [certifications](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/best-free-online-certificates-for-resume), language tests (e.g. JLPT for Japanese, TOEIC for English), driving licence.
- **Personal section:** motivation for applying, commuting time, and sometimes personal requests.

Because it's standardised, the rirekisho is less about standing out and more about completeness, neatness, and correctness.

### Shokumu-keirekisho (職務経歴書) — The Work-History Document

The shokumu-keirekisho complements the rirekisho with a fuller account of your career: your roles, responsibilities, achievements, and skills. This is closer to a Western resume and is where you can demonstrate your value with specifics — projects delivered, results achieved, and expertise gained. It's especially important for mid-career and professional roles.

## Rirekisho Formatting Rules That Matter

Japanese employers notice details. Key conventions:

- **The photo:** a formal, recent headshot in business attire against a plain background, attached in the designated spot. Neat and professional is essential.
- **Dates:** Japan often uses the Japanese era calendar (e.g. Reiwa) alongside or instead of the Western calendar — be consistent throughout the document.
- **Handwriting vs typed:** historically handwritten rirekisho were common (and neat handwriting was valued); today typed/digital versions are widely accepted, especially at global companies. Follow the employer's preference.
- **No errors or corrections:** on a handwritten form, mistakes mean starting over — accuracy and neatness signal diligence.
- **Formal language:** polite, professional Japanese where the document is in Japanese.

## The Personal/Motivation Section

The rirekisho includes a section for your motivation for applying (*shibō dōki*) and self-promotion. Here you briefly explain why you want to work for *this* company and what you bring. Keep it sincere, specific to the employer, and aligned with Japanese business values — teamwork, diligence, and long-term commitment are well-received.

## For Foreigners: Which Resume Do You Need?

Your approach depends on the employer:

- **Traditional Japanese companies:** usually expect a rirekisho (in Japanese) plus a shokumu-keirekisho. Japanese-language ability is often required.
- **Global companies and foreign-facing roles in Japan:** frequently accept an **English resume** in Western format, particularly in tech, finance, and international business.
- **Recruitment agencies:** many that place foreigners will advise which format each employer wants, and some help you prepare both.

## What Japanese Employers Value

Beyond format, Japanese employers look for:

- **Reliability and commitment:** a history that shows stability and dedication.
- **Fit with company culture:** teamwork, humility, and respect.
- **Relevant skills and qualifications:** including language tests (JLPT, TOEIC) that certify your ability.
- **Sincere motivation:** genuine interest in the specific company.

Present yourself as diligent, respectful, and genuinely interested, and you align with what the market rewards.

## Language Qualifications to Highlight

- **JLPT (Japanese Language Proficiency Test):** N1–N5 levels; higher levels (N2, N1) strongly boost your prospects at Japanese companies.
- **TOEIC:** widely used to certify English ability, valued at many Japanese firms.
- List these clearly — they're concrete, trusted credentials in the Japanese market.

## Common Japanese Resume Mistakes

- **Skipping the shokumu-keirekisho.** For professional roles, the detailed work history matters.
- **A casual or missing photo.** The rirekisho photo must be formal and neat.
- **Inconsistent dates.** Mixing calendars carelessly looks sloppy.
- **Errors on a handwritten form.** Accuracy and neatness are read as diligence.
- **Generic motivation.** Tailor the *shibō dōki* to the specific company.
- **Assuming one format fits all.** Confirm whether the employer wants Japanese or English documents.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a rirekisho?**
A standardised Japanese personal-history form covering your details, education, work history, and qualifications, usually with a formal photo. It's paired with the shokumu-keirekisho for a full application.

**What's the difference between rirekisho and shokumu-keirekisho?**
The rirekisho is a fixed-format personal-history form; the shokumu-keirekisho is a detailed work-history document (closer to a Western resume) where you show achievements and skills.

**Can I use an English resume in Japan?**
Often, yes — at global companies and foreign-facing roles, especially in tech and finance. Traditional Japanese companies usually expect Japanese-language rirekisho and shokumu-keirekisho.

**Do I need a photo on a Japanese resume?**
On the rirekisho, yes — a formal, recent business headshot is standard. Western-style resumes for global roles do not require a photo.
