## Key Takeaways

- A vague posting still has real requirements — they are usually buried in the job title, seniority language, or a "day in the life" paragraph rather than a clean bullet list.
- The generic filler ("fast-paced environment," "wear many hats") carries almost no information and should be ignored.
- Company size and industry, even unstated, narrow down what the role actually needs.
- If a posting is vague because it was copy-pasted from a template, that is itself useful information about the hiring process.
- When genuinely uncertain, apply with your strongest general match and let an interview clarify the rest.

Some job postings read like they were assembled from a template with the specifics never filled in: "seeking a dynamic self-starter to join a fast-paced, collaborative environment." Here is how to find the actual job hiding inside that.

## Why Postings Are Vague

Understanding the cause helps you read past it. Vague postings usually come from one of a few sources:

**A template that was never customised.** Many companies use a standard posting shell and the hiring manager filled in only the title and a few lines, leaving generic boilerplate everywhere else.

**A role that is genuinely still being defined.** Sometimes the company knows they need someone but has not fully scoped what the person will do day to day — common at fast-growing companies filling a new function.

**Deliberate broadness to widen the applicant pool.** Some postings are intentionally loose to avoid discouraging candidates who might be a good fit but do not check every box.

None of these mean there is no real job underneath. It means you have to find the requirements somewhere other than a clean bullet list.

## What to Ignore

These phrases appear in nearly every posting and carry almost no differentiating information:

- "Fast-paced environment"
- "Wear many hats"
- "Self-starter"
- "Team player"
- "Excellent communication skills"
- "Passionate about [industry]"
- "Competitive salary"

Do not spend time trying to address these in your resume or cover letter. They apply to every posting equally and reading meaning into them wastes effort.

## Where the Real Signal Hides

**1. The job title itself.** "Senior" vs no modifier tells you the expected experience level even if the body text is vague. A title change from your current one — "Analyst" to "Associate Analyst" — can indicate scope, even without explicit years-of-experience language.

**2. The first two sentences of the description.** Even a templated posting usually has one or two genuinely written sentences near the top, describing why the role exists. This is often the most information-dense part of an otherwise generic posting.

**3. Any "day in the life" or "what you'll do" section**, even if short. These tend to be more specific than the requirements list, because they describe actual tasks rather than aspirational qualities.

**4. The team or department name, if given.** "Growth Marketing" versus "Brand Marketing" tells you real things about the role even without further detail.

**5. Company size and stage.** A Series A startup and a public company posting an identically vague "Marketing Manager" role need different things — the startup version likely means broader, more hands-on ownership; the enterprise version likely means narrower scope within a larger structure. See [resume tailoring for startup vs enterprise](/blog/resume-tailoring-startup-vs-enterprise).

**6. Salary range, if disclosed.** Compensation bands are one of the more reliable, concrete signals in an otherwise vague posting, and they narrow down seniority expectations more precisely than most adjective-heavy text.

## Reading Between the Lines: A Worked Example

> "We're looking for a dynamic Marketing Manager to join our fast-paced team. You'll wear many hats and help drive growth across multiple channels. Must be a self-starter with excellent communication skills."

Almost entirely filler — but not completely useless:

- **"Multiple channels"** suggests breadth over specialisation — likely not a role narrowly focused on one channel like paid search or SEO alone.
- **"Wear many hats"** at a company this vague about the role often signals an early-stage or under-resourced team, where the actual job may extend well beyond "marketing manager" as narrowly defined.
- **"Manager" with no direct-report language elsewhere** in the posting often means the title reflects seniority or scope of ownership rather than people management — worth clarifying at interview stage rather than assuming either way.

You cannot extract a precise requirements list from this, but you can extract enough to write a summary that speaks generally to breadth and ownership rather than guessing at specifics that were never given. ## When Vagueness Itself Is a Signal

A consistently vague, unedited-looking posting is sometimes useful information about the hiring process itself, separate from the role. It can suggest the hiring manager has not invested much time in the requisition, which occasionally correlates with a less structured interview process — not necessarily a red flag, but worth factoring into your expectations rather than assuming the vagueness reflects a fully thought-out role.

## What to Do With Genuine Uncertainty

If a posting genuinely tells you almost nothing beyond title and company, the reasonable approach is:

**1. Apply with your strongest general-purpose resume**, tailored toward the job title and industry rather than specific requirements you cannot identify.

**2. Do not over-invest tailoring time** in a posting this vague — the return is lower because you are guessing at what to emphasise.

**3. Save detailed tailoring for postings that actually specify requirements**, where the effort has a clearer target.

**4. Treat the interview as the place to learn the actual scope**, and prepare a few clarifying questions of your own about day-to-day responsibilities.

## Checking Your Guess

If you have inferred a likely emphasis from a vague posting — say, breadth over specialisation — you can still [scan your resume against the posting text as written](/ats-analysis) to see what little structure the parser does find. Even a vague posting usually contains a handful of specific nouns (a tool, an industry term, a team name) that are worth confirming your resume addresses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I apply to a posting this vague?**
Generally yes, if the title and company interest you — vagueness in the posting does not necessarily mean the role itself is undefined, just that it was not well described.

**How much time should I spend tailoring for a vague posting?**
Less than for a detailed one. Focus on general title and industry alignment rather than guessing at specific requirements.

**Is a vague posting a red flag about the company?**
Not necessarily on its own. It is one data point among many, worth weighing alongside other signals like company reviews and interview structure once you engage further.

**Should I ask for more detail before applying?**
Rarely necessary at the application stage — save clarifying questions for a recruiter screen or interview, where you can ask directly.

**Can I write a strong tailored resume for a vague posting?**
Somewhat — you can tailor toward title, industry, and any specific nouns present. Full tailoring works best against a posting with real requirement detail to target.
