## Key Takeaways

- Spring weeks are short insight programmes for first-year students, and at many firms they feed directly into summer internship offers.
- Applications typically open in the autumn of your first year — earlier than most students realise a search has started.
- They are concentrated in investment banking, law and to a lesser extent consulting and technology.
- A spring week place can convert to a summer internship, which converts to a graduate offer, so the sequence starts two years before graduation.
- Missing them is recoverable, but it means entering the summer internship round without the pre-selection advantage others have.

## What a spring week is

A short programme, usually one week during the Easter or spring vacation, aimed at first-year undergraduates. Sometimes called insight weeks, spring insight programmes, or discovery days.

The content is a mixture of presentations, division overviews, networking sessions, group exercises and shadowing. You will not do meaningful work in a week — that is not the point.

The point, from the firm's perspective, is pre-selection. They are identifying students early, building loyalty before competitors do, and creating a pool they can draw the following year's summer interns from.

## Why they matter more than they look

At many firms, particularly in banking, a spring week is the main pipeline into the summer internship. Attendees are invited to apply for the summer programme with a shortened process, or in some cases receive an offer directly.

The summer internship then converts into the graduate offer — the same conversion dynamic that dominates [US campus hiring](/blog/usa-internship-return-offer-conversion).

Follow the chain: spring week in first year → summer internship in penultimate year → graduate offer for after graduation. **The sequence begins roughly two and a half years before you start the job.**

Students who discover this in their second year have not lost, but they are entering the summer internship competition against people the firm already knows.

## Timing

Applications for spring weeks typically open in the **autumn of your first year**, with deadlines running through to around January. Many operate on a rolling basis, meaning places are filled as strong applications arrive rather than after the deadline.

Two consequences:

**Apply early in the window.** With rolling assessment, an application in October is genuinely stronger than the identical one in January, because more places remain.

**You are applying weeks after starting university.** That is the part that surprises people. If you arrive in September and start looking in February, you have missed the cycle.

This is the earliest calendar of any UK graduate pathway. The [graduate scheme timeline](/blog/uk-graduate-scheme-guide) is the equivalent for final-year students, and it is later.

## Who runs them

**Investment banks.** The most established and the most competitive. Nearly every major bank runs one.

**Law firms.** Vacation schemes and open days, structured similarly, and often the primary route to a training contract.

**Consultancies.** Fewer formal spring weeks, more insight days and workshops.

**Technology and engineering.** Less common, though some large employers run first-year insight programmes.

**Accountancy and professional services.** Widespread, and often less contested than banking.

## What the application involves

Usually lighter than a full internship process, but not trivial:

- **Application form** with motivational questions
- **Psychometric tests** — numerical, situational judgement, sometimes gamified. The same formats used later, so [preparing now](/blog/uk-psychometric-tests-guide) pays off twice.
- **Video interview**, often one-way and recorded
- Occasionally a short assessment centre

Because you are a first-year with little experience, the assessment weights motivation and potential more heavily than track record. The written questions matter disproportionately.

**"Why this firm" is the deciding question.** With little to distinguish candidates on experience, the quality of your reasoning about the firm and the industry is what separates applications. Generic answers are the most common rejection, and they are obvious when a recruiter reads two hundred of them.

## What to write when you have nothing yet

The honest difficulty of a first-year application is that you have almost no relevant experience. Firms know this.

What works:

**Evidence of genuine interest, with follow-through.** Not "I have always been fascinated by finance" — what you did about it. A society you joined, something you read and can discuss, a model you built, a competition you entered.

**School and pre-university achievements.** Legitimate material at this stage. Leadership, competitions, part-time work, sustained commitments.

**Specificity about the firm.** A division, a deal, a recent piece of news, something a person told you. This is where preparation shows.

**Clarity about why this industry over the adjacent one.** Being able to say why banking rather than consulting, or why this practice area, demonstrates thinking rather than enthusiasm.

## If you missed it

Not fatal. Options:

**Apply for summer internships in your penultimate year directly.**

Most places are still filled this way, and spring week attendees do not take all of them.

**Look for insight days and shorter events.** Many firms run one-day events with much later deadlines and lighter competition.

**Build evidence instead.** Societies, competitions, part-time work and projects all count in the penultimate-year application, and you now have a year to accumulate them.

**Target firms with less rigid pipelines.** Smaller firms, boutiques and companies outside banking do not run spring weeks and hire on merit later.

## The pipeline, laid out

The reason spring weeks matter is that they sit at the front of a chain most first-year students do not know exists.

**Spring week, first year (Easter).** One week, sometimes less. Talks, shadowing, a group exercise, networking. You learn very little about the job and the firm learns quite a lot about you.

**Fast-track to summer internship, first year (May to July).** Many firms convert spring week attendees directly, or place them into a shortened process that skips the online tests and the first interview. This is the actual prize, and it is why the week matters disproportionately relative to what happens during it.

**Summer internship, second year (June to August).** Eight to ten weeks, paid, and effectively an extended interview.

**Graduate offer (September, second year).** Conversion rates from summer internships at these firms are high — frequently a majority of the graduate intake comes from the intern class.

Follow the arithmetic backwards and the consequence is uncomfortable: at some firms, a meaningful share of graduate places are effectively allocated during the autumn of a student's first year, before most of their peers know that recruitment exists.

This is not a reason to panic if you missed it. It is a reason to understand what you are entering the summer round against — a pool where some candidates were pre-selected eighteen months earlier.

## The application, when you have done nothing yet

The obvious objection: you have been at university for six weeks and have no experience. Everyone applying is in the same position, which means the application is assessed on different things.

**What they are actually looking at:**

**School results.** These carry unusual weight here simply because they are the only academic record that exists. Many firms filter on them explicitly.

**Pre-university activities.** Sport, music, part-time jobs, volunteering, anything with duration and progression. A student who worked weekends in a shop for two years has evidence of reliability; a student with a list of one-term memberships does not.

**The "why this firm" answer.** The deciding question, at every stage, in every one of these processes.

**Evidence of follow-through.** Any activity you stuck with past the point of novelty. This is the quality being tested, and it is visible in duration more than in title.

**What to write when your evidence is thin:**

Use what you have and describe it seriously. A Saturday job is real work with real constraints — dealing with difficult customers, being counted on to turn up, handling money. Students routinely omit it as unimpressive and then have nothing to write about.

For "why this firm", you cannot yet have industry insight, and nobody expects it. What you can have is specificity: attend the firm's virtual events, which are open to first years and lightly attended, and reference what you heard. "At your insight session in November, one of your analysts described the difference between the coverage and product sides, and the coverage side is what interests me because..." is achievable in your first term and is far stronger than a paragraph about the firm's reputation.

For "why this industry rather than the adjacent one" — banking rather than consulting, law rather than banking — have a real answer. This question separates candidates more than any other at this stage, because most first years have not thought about it at all.

## The week itself, used well

Attendance is not the outcome. Conversion is, and the firms are assessing throughout even when nothing is labelled an assessment.

**Turn up prepared for the group exercise.** Nearly every spring week includes one, it is scored, and it is the closest thing to a formal assessment in the week. The [assessment centre mechanics](/blog/uk-assessment-centre-guide) apply — summarise the group's position at the halfway point, bring in whoever has not spoken, watch the clock.

**Ask questions that are not about the firm's reputation.** "What did you find hardest in your first six months?" gets a real answer and is remembered. "What makes your firm different?" gets a recruiting answer and is not.

**Collect names and follow up within forty-eight hours.** A short message to two or three people you actually spoke with, referencing something specific from the conversation. This is the single most differentiating behaviour of the week, because approximately nobody does it.

**Be normal with the other attendees.** You are being observed with peers, and the candidates who treat the week as a competition against the other students are visible and score badly.

**Say you want the summer internship, explicitly, to someone senior.**

Firms convert people who asked. It is a one-sentence thing to do and a large share of attendees never do it.

## If you missed the window

Recoverable, and the recovery is not "apply anyway next year" — the spring week window has closed permanently once you are past first year.

**Insight days and shorter events** run throughout the year, are open to later-year students at many firms, and are considerably less contested. They do not carry the same fast-track, but they produce the specific material that makes a "why this firm" answer credible.

**Apply directly to summer internships in your second year.** This is the main route for the majority of students, spring week or not. You will be competing against pre-selected candidates for some of the places and against the general pool for the rest.

**Build the evidence instead.** A society position held for a full year, a part-time job, a project with a result. The [competency story bank](/blog/uk-competency-questions-guide) is built from exactly this material, and building it deliberately in second year is a real substitute for a week of shadowing.

**Target firms with less rigid pipelines.** Mid-sized firms, boutiques and regional offices frequently do not run spring weeks at all and hire their summer interns from the open pool. Their graduate outcomes are often excellent and their processes are much less contested.

**Consider a placement year.** Where your degree allows it, a [year-long placement](/blog/uk-internship-placement-year-guide) is a stronger credential than any spring week and converts at high rates.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not knowing they exist until second year.** The single most common way students miss the earliest pipeline.
- **Applying at the deadline.** Rolling assessment means late applications compete for fewer places.
- **Generic motivation answers.** With little experience to assess, this is what decides.
- **Ignoring the tests.** They filter before a human reads anything.
- **Applying to only the famous names.** Professional services and mid-tier firms are less contested for comparable exposure.
- **Treating it as a holiday.** Attendees are being assessed continuously, including socially.

## What the week is actually assessing

Nothing is labelled an assessment, and everything is one. Understanding what is being watched changes how you spend the days.

**Whether people want to work with you.** The single largest factor, and it is assessed continuously by everyone you meet, including people whose job title has nothing to do with recruitment. Analysts get asked afterwards which of the students they would want on their desk, and their answers carry real weight.

**Whether you asked anything real.** Recruiters remember the two or three students who asked something they had to think about. Most questions are variations on "what makes your firm special", which the answerer has responded to forty times.

**Whether you were present.** Phones out during talks, arriving late, disappearing during the networking part. These sound like trivial complaints and they are exactly what gets mentioned in the debrief.

**Group exercise behaviour.** Scored formally where one runs.

**Whether you followed up.** Not universal, but at firms where it is noticed it is a large differentiator precisely because so few students do it.

What is not being assessed: technical knowledge, market views, or anything you could not possibly know six weeks into a degree. Students spend their preparation time on the wrong half of this and then perform nervously at the part that actually counts.

## Choosing which firms to apply to

Fifteen applications with October deadlines requires a list built in September, and the list is worth thinking about rather than copying.

**Apply across the tiers, not only the top.** The most prestigious firms take the fewest students and receive the most applications. Mid-tier and boutique firms run genuinely good programmes, convert at similar or better rates, and are dramatically less contested.

**Include at least three firms in a second industry.** A student who applies exclusively to investment banks has one outcome or none. Adding professional services, law or technology firms — which run comparable programmes — roughly doubles the pool at almost no extra cost, since the applications share most of their material.

**Prefer firms whose events you can actually attend.** Because "why this firm" is the deciding question, and attending an event is the cheapest way to answer it credibly. A firm running open virtual sessions in October is a firm where you can build a real answer by November.

**Check the eligibility criteria before writing anything.** Some programmes are restricted by year of study, by degree discipline, or to students from particular backgrounds. Discovering a restriction after writing an application is avoidable and demoralising.

**Note which firms fast-track and which do not.** Some convert spring week attendees directly to summer internships; others treat it purely as insight with no formal link. Both are worth doing, and knowing which is which tells you where the pipeline value actually is.

## What a first-year application timeline looks like

Concrete, because "apply in the autumn" is not actionable when you have just arrived at university.

**Late September, week one or two of term.** Find out that spring weeks exist, which is the step most students miss. Attend your careers service's first-year session if there is one.

**Early October.** Build the list — twelve to fifteen firms across at least two industries, deadlines noted. Write your CV. It will be short, one page, mostly school results and pre-university activities, and that is correct for this stage.

**Mid-October.** Attend the virtual events. Firms run these constantly in October and November, they are open to first years, and attendance is low. Take notes with names in them.

**Late October to November.** Applications. Many deadlines fall here, and several firms review on a rolling basis, so early submissions get considered against a smaller pool.

**November to January.** Online tests and video interviews. The [psychometric formats](/blog/uk-psychometric-tests-guide) are standard across firms, so practice transfers.

**January to February.** Assessment centres and offers.

**March to April.** The spring weeks themselves, over the Easter vacation.

The startling part of this timeline is the first line: the decisive research happens in your second week at university, before you have sat a single exam. Nobody tells first years this, which is exactly why the students who find out early have such an advantage.

## Being realistic about the odds

These programmes are competitive — the ratios at the best-known firms are unfavourable, and a first year who applies to fifteen and receives one offer has done well.

Two things follow from that.

**Volume is not optional.** Fifteen applications is a floor, not a stretch target, and the marginal cost of the twelfth application is low because most of the material is shared.

**Rejection at this stage means very little.** You are being assessed on school results and a paragraph about a firm you have known about for three weeks. It is not a judgement on your career prospects, and the number of successful people in these industries who got nowhere as first years is very large.

The students who handle this well treat first-year applications as a free option: cheap to buy, occasionally very valuable, and no real loss when it expires. The ones who handle it badly treat one round of rejections as a verdict and stop applying for two years — which is the only version of this that actually costs them something. [Handling rejection during placement season](/blog/placement-season-rejection-resilience) is a skill worth building now, because the summer round is larger and the graduate round larger still.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who can apply for a spring week?

Generally first-year undergraduates on a three-year degree, or first and second years on a four-year course. Check each firm, as eligibility definitions differ.

### Are they paid?

Many are, at least partially, and firms often cover travel and accommodation. Some are unpaid but expenses-covered.

### Does a spring week guarantee a summer internship?

No, but it frequently gives a shortened process or direct consideration, which is a substantial advantage.

### What if my university is not a target for these firms?

Apply directly — the online application is open regardless. Networking matters more for you, in the same way it does for [non-target candidates in the US](/blog/usa-non-target-school-strategy).

### How competitive are they?

Very, at the major banks. Professional services and mid-tier firms are considerably more accessible for similar exposure.

### Should I apply if I am unsure about the industry?

Yes — that is precisely what they are for. A week of exposure is a cheap way to discover you do not want it before committing two more years.

### Can second-year students apply for spring weeks?

Usually not for the standard programmes, which are explicitly first-year. Some firms run equivalent events for later years under different names, and four-year degree students are sometimes eligible in second year — check each firm rather than assuming.

### How many should I apply to?

As many as you would genuinely attend, which for most students is between eight and fifteen. Deadlines cluster in October and November, so build the list in September rather than discovering it in December.

### Do I need to know exactly what division I want?

No, but you need a direction and a reason. "I am interested in markets rather than advisory because I want faster feedback on decisions" is entirely sufficient at this stage and beats an answer that keeps every option open.

### Are spring weeks worth it if I am not sure about the industry?

Yes — that is arguably their best use. A week inside a firm is the cheapest way to discover you do not want the job, and finding that out in first year is worth far more than finding it out during a summer internship.
