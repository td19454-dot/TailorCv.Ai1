## Key Takeaways

- Household registration status affects real, practical access to certain local services, most significantly housing purchase eligibility, schooling, and some benefits, in the city where you actually live and work.
- Major first-tier cities generally operate stricter, points-based systems for obtaining local settlement than smaller cities, which often actively compete to attract new graduates.
- Many major cities have specific, and sometimes genuinely favourable, settlement pathways designed for recent graduates specifically.
- This is a decision worth understanding before committing to a first job in a specific city, not something to research only once a long-term complication has already emerged.
- Foreign nationals and Chinese citizens without local registration in a given city both navigate a broadly similar underlying system, though the specific mechanics differ meaningfully by category.

## What household registration status actually affects

**This is a system of household registration tied to a specific place**, and it genuinely affects practical, everyday access to certain services and rights specifically in the city where you are registered, rather than wherever you happen to be currently living and working.

**Property purchase eligibility** in many major cities is directly tied to local registration status, or requires meeting specific alternative criteria, such as a defined period of local social insurance contributions, without it — a real, concrete constraint worth understanding well before it becomes relevant to an actual decision.

**Access to local public schooling for children**, and in some cases access to specific local subsidies or benefits, is frequently tied to registration status in the specific city.

**Living and working in a city without local registration status is entirely normal and extremely common**, particularly for graduates moving to a major city for their first job, it does not prevent you from living and working there, it specifically affects the particular practical matters described above, most of which become relevant later in a career rather than immediately upon graduation.

## Why major cities restrict it more tightly

The largest and most desirable first-tier cities generally operate stricter, points-based systems for obtaining local settlement status, reflecting genuine capacity and planning considerations at that scale, meeting the threshold typically requires a combination of factors including education level, professional qualifications, and sometimes years of local contribution. Beijing and Shanghai are the two most-discussed examples — Shanghai in particular has run a points calculation covering age, education, salary, and social insurance contribution history, with graduates of a defined set of top domestic universities historically receiving especially favourable treatment; Beijing's own system runs on broadly similar logic with its own specific weighting. Both are reviewed and adjusted periodically, so treat any specific numeric threshold you find online as a starting point for research, not a number to plan a multi-year decision around without verifying it's current.

Smaller and lower-tier cities frequently operate considerably more open systems, and many actively compete to attract skilled graduates specifically, sometimes offering direct settlement pathways, financial incentives, or genuinely simplified processes as part of a deliberate strategy to attract young talent, this is a real and sometimes underappreciated factor when [comparing a first-tier city offer against a lower-tier one](/blog/china-graduate-salary-expectations).

## Graduate-specific pathways

**Many major cities maintain settlement pathways designed specifically for recent graduates**, sometimes with meaningfully more favourable criteria than the standard points-based system used for the general working population, worth investigating the specific current criteria for your target city directly rather than assuming the general system's difficulty applies equally to a graduate-specific route.

**Criteria commonly considered include your degree level**, the reputation tier of your graduating institution, your specific field of study relative to the city's stated priorities, and sometimes your age at the point of application.

**These pathways and their specific criteria are set by individual city policy and are reviewed and adjusted periodically**, verify current, specific requirements directly with the relevant city authority rather than relying on a general description, since this is exactly the kind of local, frequently updated detail where an outdated summary is genuinely unhelpful.

## For foreign graduates specifically

Foreign nationals navigate a related but distinct system, generally tied to your residence permit status rather than the household registration system that applies to Chinese citizens, though some of the underlying practical considerations, particularly around long-term settlement in a specific city, share a similar logic.

If you are a foreign graduate planning to build a longer-term career in a specific Chinese city, understanding both your work authorisation status, covered in the [work visa guide](/blog/china-work-visa-z-visa-for-graduates), and any city-specific considerations for foreign residents is worth investigating directly with the relevant authorities, since this is a genuinely distinct system from the domestic household registration framework.

## Deciding where to take your first job

**This is worth factoring into your decision deliberately rather than discovering the practical implications later**, once they have become more consequential.

**If long-term settlement in a specific major city is a genuine priority**, research that city's specific current graduate settlement pathway before accepting an offer there, rather than assuming settlement will be straightforward once you are already established, since criteria and points requirements are set by policy and are worth understanding in advance.

**If a lower-tier city is genuinely offering a settlement pathway or incentive as part of its recruitment strategy**, weigh this seriously as a real factor alongside salary and career considerations, not merely as a minor administrative detail, since it can materially affect your practical life over the following years.

**This is a long-term consideration**, not an urgent one for most new graduates in their first year, though understanding the landscape early means you can make a more informed decision about where to build a career if settlement status matters to your longer-term plans.

> **Tip:** Check your target city's graduate-specific settlement pathway, not just its general points system. The graduate route is frequently far more accessible, and many applicants never discover it exists.

- **Confusing hukou with the foreigner system.** Confusing the domestic household registration system with the separate framework that applies to foreign nationals. Related in spirit and distinct in mechanics.
- **Relying on outdated information.** Relying on outdated information about a specific city's criteria when these are reviewed and adjusted periodically by local policy.
- **Thinking no hukou means no job.** Assuming registration status prevents you from living and working in a city without it. It does not, it specifically affects certain practical matters described above.

No, major first-tier cities generally operate stricter, points-based systems, while many smaller and lower-tier cities operate considerably more open systems and actively compete to attract graduates.

No — both run their own points-based calculations with distinct specific factors and weightings, though the broad logic (education, contribution history, and similar factors) is comparable. Check each city's current specific criteria directly rather than assuming one city's system tells you about the other's.
