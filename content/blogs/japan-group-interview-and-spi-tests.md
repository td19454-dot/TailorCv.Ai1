## Key Takeaways

- Standardised aptitude testing is used widely and is frequently an early, mechanical filter before any human reads your application.
- The tests cover language and numerical reasoning plus a personality component, and the difficulty is speed rather than content.
- Group discussions assess how you work with others, not whether you win the argument.
- Both stages are trainable, and Japanese students practise for them extensively, which is the standard you are competing against.
- Foreign candidates face an additional language load on the tests, making practice more important rather than less.

## The aptitude tests

**What they are.** Standardised assessments used widely in Japanese graduate recruiting, typically combining a language section, a numerical reasoning section and a personality inventory. Several test families exist and formats vary, with the general shape consistent.

**Where they sit.** Usually early, after the written application and before interviews. At high-volume employers this is a genuine mechanical filter — fail it and no human reads what you wrote.

**The language section** tests vocabulary, reading comprehension and logical relationships between words and statements.

**The numerical section** tests arithmetic reasoning, ratios, percentages, probability and data interpretation. The mathematics is not advanced; the time pressure is severe.

**The personality inventory** assesses working style and traits. There is no right answer and consistency matters — answering to project an image rather than honestly produces contradictory patterns that are visible.

**Format varies.** Some are taken at testing centres, some online at home, some at the company's offices. Check which applies.

## Preparing for them

**Practice is the whole strategy**, and it works because the difficulty is format and speed rather than knowledge.

**Practice books are standard.** Japanese students work through them extensively — this is normal, expected preparation, and it is the standard you are measured against.

**Practise under time pressure from the start.** Untimed practice builds accuracy, and accuracy is not what is being tested. The [same principle applies to psychometric testing anywhere](/blog/uk-psychometric-tests-guide), and it is the single most common preparation error.

**Learn the recurring question types.** Both sections use a limited set of patterns, and recognising a pattern instantly is most of the speed advantage.

**For foreign candidates, the language section carries an additional load.** You are reading and reasoning in a second language under time pressure, which is genuinely harder. This is an argument for more practice, not for skipping it.

**Answer the personality section honestly and consistently.** Inconsistent patterns are detectable and reflect worse than an ordinary profile would.

## Group discussions

**The format.** Several candidates given a topic and a time limit, asked to discuss and frequently to reach a conclusion, while assessors observe without participating.

**What is assessed.** How you operate with other people — whether you contribute substance, listen and build on others, help the group manage its time, and handle disagreement constructively. The group's conclusion matters far less than your conduct reaching it.

**Behaviours that score well.** Contributing early so you are visible. Building explicitly on what someone else said. Bringing in a quieter participant. Watching the clock and saying so. Summarising where the group has reached partway through — almost nobody does this and every assessor notices.

**Behaviours that fail.** Dominating, which reads as poor collaboration. And disappearing, which is more common and equally fatal, because an assessor cannot score what you did not do.

**Roles.** Groups sometimes assign a facilitator, timekeeper or presenter. Any of these is fine and none is necessary — the assessment is on how you contribute, not on which label you hold.

**For foreign candidates**, the challenge is participating fluently in a fast Japanese discussion. Practising in a group beforehand is worth considerably more than reading about it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not practising the aptitude tests.** Japanese students prepare extensively and it is the standard you are measured against.
- **Practising untimed.** Speed under pressure is the actual test.
- **Answering the personality section strategically.** Inconsistency is visible and reflects poorly.
- **Trying to win the group discussion.** It assesses collaboration, not debate.
- **Staying silent in the group discussion.** An assessor cannot score what you did not contribute.
- **Underestimating the language load as a foreign candidate.** It is genuinely harder and it is trainable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much practice do the aptitude tests need?

Enough to be comfortable with the format and fast under time pressure — typically weeks of consistent work with practice materials rather than a single session.

### Can I retake a test if I do badly?

Generally not within the same application. Some test results can be reused across applications depending on the format, which makes the first attempt more consequential.

### Is the group discussion competitive against other candidates?

No. Assessment is generally against criteria rather than by ranking, which means helping the group costs you nothing and behaving competitively is marked down.

### What if my Japanese is not fast enough for the discussion?

Practise in groups beforehand, prepare useful phrases for contributing and summarising, and contribute early rather than waiting for a perfect moment. Some employers, particularly [English-operating ones](/blog/japan-english-speaking-jobs-guide), run these in English.

### Do all companies use these stages?

Widely used in conventional graduate recruiting, and less so at [technology companies](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates) and foreign-affiliated firms hiring for specific roles — see the [technology route](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates).

### Does the personality test have right answers?

No, and it does have consistency. Answer honestly rather than constructing a profile you think they want.
