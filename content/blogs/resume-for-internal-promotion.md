## Key Takeaways

- **They know your current work**, so the resume must prove readiness for the next level rather than competence at this one.
- **Familiarity cuts both ways.** Your reputation is already formed and your resume cannot override it.
- **Evidence of scope beyond your role** is the thing that decides these applications.
- **A resume is still required**, and treating the process casually is the most common internal mistake.
- **Talk to your manager first** in almost every case.

## What an internal application must prove

The question is different from an external one.

**That you can operate at the next level**, which is not demonstrated by doing your current job well.

**That you have already been doing parts of it**, which is the strongest available evidence and the reason stretch work matters.

**That your scope has grown**, in team, in complexity or in influence, over the period you have been in the role.

**That you understand the new role's problems**, not just its title, which comes from having engaged with them already.

**That your reputation supports it**, since internal hiring weighs known behaviour far more heavily than any document.

**And that you will handle not getting it well**, which is assessed informally and matters more than candidates realise.

**Competence in your current role is assumed rather than credited.** Making the case on that basis alone is why strong performers are sometimes passed over.

## Where internal candidates lose

The recurring failure modes.

| Mistake | Why it costs the role |
|---|---|
| **Treating the process casually** | Signals you want the title, not the job |
| **Listing current duties** | Proves the level you are already at |
| **No evidence of stretch work** | Nothing indicates readiness |
| **Skipping the manager conversation** | Creates avoidable friction |
| **Assuming tenure entitles you** | Time served is not evidence |
| **Poor handling of the process** | Watched more closely than the application |

**A casual application is read as entitlement.** Internal candidates who submit a stale resume and skip preparation frequently lose to external ones who did neither.

**Duties describe your current level.** The bullets that win an internal promotion describe work that belonged to the level above.

**And tenure is not a qualification.** Long service without expanded scope argues against promotion rather than for it, which is uncomfortable but consistently true.

## Writing the internal resume

What changes when the reader knows you.

**The summary states the case for the next level**, not your history, since your history is already known to everyone reading it.

**Lead with work above your grade**, even if it occupied less of your time than your core duties, because that is the evidence the decision turns on.

**Name the outcomes the organisation cares about**, using its own internal language for goals and metrics, which is an advantage external candidates do not have.

**Include cross-team work prominently**, since influence outside your immediate line is usually what separates the levels.

**Keep the routine duties brief.** One line covering your core responsibilities is enough; the reader knows them already.

**[Reference](/blog/reference-check-process-guide) things they can verify easily**, which is uniquely powerful internally because a reader can confirm your claims in a two-minute conversation.

**And update it properly rather than reusing an old version**, since a resume that stops two years ago suggests you have not thought about your own progression.

## The conversation with your manager

Handling it well, before anything else happens.

**Have it early**, before you apply, so nothing arrives as a surprise through the system.

**Ask for support explicitly** rather than merely informing them, since a manager who has agreed to advocate behaves differently from one who was told.

**Ask what they see as the gap**, and take the answer seriously even when it is uncomfortable.

**Agree what evidence would settle it**, which converts a vague ambition into a checkable plan.

**Set a revisit date** if the answer is not yet, because an open-ended not yet becomes indefinite without one.

**And handle a lack of support carefully.** Applying against a manager's stated view is possible but costly, and it is usually better to understand the objection first.

## Mini checklist

- [ ] Manager consulted before applying
- [ ] Resume updated properly rather than reused
- [ ] Evidence of work at the next level identified
- [ ] Scope growth documented over your tenure
- [ ] The new role's actual problems understood
- [ ] Internal advocates identified
- [ ] Process treated as seriously as an external one
- [ ] Response prepared for an unsuccessful outcome

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have been doing the role informally already

This is the strongest possible case. Document it specifically — what you took on, when, and what resulted — because informal scope is invisible unless you make it explicit.

### Scenario 2: Your manager is not supportive

Find out why before applying. Either there is a gap you can close or there is a relationship problem, and both are better addressed directly than discovered through a [rejection](/blog/how-to-handle-job-rejection).

### Scenario 3: You are competing against an external candidate

Your advantage is context and relationships; theirs is fresh perspective. Compete on what you can do from day one, since that is the genuine internal advantage.

> **Tip:** The internal promotion is usually decided before the application. Spend the six months beforehand taking work at the next level, and the application becomes a formality documenting what everyone already saw.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Speak to your manager first
- Update the resume properly
- Document work above your current level
- Show how your scope has grown
- Treat the process seriously
- Prepare for an unsuccessful outcome

### Don't

- Rely on tenure as an argument
- List your current duties
- Submit a stale resume
- Skip [interview](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-2026-complete-guide-for-every-industry) preparation
- Assume the outcome is decided in your favour
- React badly if you are passed over

## Common Mistakes

- **Casual treatment.** It reads as wanting the title only.
- **Current duties as evidence.** They prove your existing level.
- **Tenure as an argument.** Time served is not readiness.
- **No manager conversation.** It creates avoidable friction.
- **Undocumented stretch work.** Invisible unless stated.
- **Poor handling of rejection.** Watched, and remembered.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a resume for an internal promotion?

Yes, in almost every formal process. Treating it casually is the most common internal mistake and it is read as wanting the title rather than the work.

### What should an internal resume emphasise?

Work you have already done at the next level. Competence in your current role is assumed rather than credited, so evidence of stretch scope is what decides these applications.

### Should I tell my manager before applying?

In almost all cases, yes. Finding out through the system creates friction, and a supportive manager is frequently the strongest advocate available to you.

### Does long service help?

Not on its own. Tenure without expanded scope argues against promotion rather than for it, because it suggests the role has not grown with the person.

### How do I compete with an external candidate?

On what you can deliver immediately. Context, relationships and understanding of the organisation are the genuine internal advantages, and they are worth stating explicitly.

### What if I do not get it?

Handle it well, since that is watched closely. Ask for specific feedback, agree what would change the answer, and set a timeframe to revisit it.
### How is an internal resume different?

The summary makes the case for the next level rather than recounting history the reader already knows. Lead with work above your grade and keep routine duties to a single line.

### What should I say to my manager?

Ask for support explicitly rather than just informing them, ask what gap they see, agree what evidence would settle it, and set a date to revisit if the answer is not yet.
