## Key Takeaways

- Legal, HR and marketing language routinely gets copy-pasted into requisitions and is not written by the hiring manager evaluating candidates.
- Certain phrases recur across nearly every posting in a category regardless of the actual role — that repetition is itself a tell.
- The real requirements are usually the specific, technical, or numerically bounded ones; the filler is usually broad and aspirational.
- Chasing every keyword equally wastes tailoring time on words that carry little weight in the actual decision.
- This is different from ignoring genuine requirements — the skill is telling the two apart, not dismissing the whole list.

Job postings contain a mix of language written by the hiring manager describing the actual role, and language inserted by HR, legal, or copied from a template. Both look identical on the page. Learning to tell them apart saves real tailoring time.

## Why This Happens

Job postings pass through more hands than people expect before publication. A hiring manager writes a rough draft; HR adds standard boilerplate for compliance and consistency; a template contributes generic phrases nobody reviews closely; sometimes a posting is copied wholesale from a similar prior role and only partially updated.

The result is a document where genuine, specific requirements sit alongside filler that exists for reasons unrelated to what the job actually needs.

## The Phrases That Are Usually Filler

**"Fast-paced, dynamic environment."** Appears in nearly every posting across every industry. Tells you nothing specific about this particular role.

**"Wear many hats" / "no task too small."** Common at smaller companies, signals broad scope in the abstract but rarely maps to a specific, checkable requirement.

**"Passionate about [industry]."** Unfalsifiable and unverifiable — nobody screens this in, and few candidates would self-report lacking it.

**"Excellent communication skills," "strong interpersonal skills," "team player."** Universal claims that every posting includes regardless of role, and every candidate claims regardless of truth. See [soft skills nobody can verify](/blog/soft-skills-nobody-can-verify) for why these carry little weight either way.

**"Must be able to work independently and as part of a team."** Simultaneously requiring both traits is a tell that this is boilerplate rather than a specific assessment of the role's actual working style.

**Equal Employment Opportunity language, benefits summaries, and legal disclaimers.** Genuinely important as information, but not requirements to address in your resume — these exist for compliance reasons, not candidate screening.

**"Other duties as assigned."** A legal catch-all, not a real task to prepare for or mention.

## The Phrases That Are Usually Real

**A specific tool or platform named exactly.** "Salesforce," "SAP," "Tableau," "AWS" — these are concrete, checkable, and almost always reflect what the hiring manager actually needs day one.

**A specific years-of-experience number.** Even if somewhat negotiable in practice — see [required vs preferred qualifications](/blog/required-vs-preferred-in-practice) — a stated number reflects a real seniority expectation someone deliberately set.

**A named credential, licence, or certification.** These exist for genuine, often regulatory reasons and are rarely filler.

**Specific responsibilities described in a "day in the life" style section.** "You will build and maintain the weekly reporting pipeline" is a real task description, distinct from a generic bullet list.

**Team size, reporting structure, or specific stakeholder groups mentioned.** "Report to the VP of Marketing, managing two direct reports" is specific enough that it was almost certainly written deliberately, not copied.

## The Repetition Test

A fast way to sanity-check a suspicious phrase: search for it across a few other postings in the same category. If "fast-paced environment" appears in the marketing manager posting you are looking at, and also in the accountant posting from an unrelated company, and also in the warehouse supervisor posting from a third — that phrase is boilerplate, not a genuine signal about any one of those specific roles.

Specific, real requirements tend not to repeat this way, because they are tied to the actual role rather than a template.

## Where This Gets Tricky: Soft Skills Named as Requirements

Some postings do genuinely mean it when they list "strong communication skills" — client-facing roles, people management, teaching. The distinction is not the phrase itself but whether the role's actual function depends on it.

A generic listing of "excellent communication skills" on an individual-contributor data analyst posting is likely filler. The same phrase on a customer success manager posting, where client relationships are the core function, likely reflects a genuine expectation — worth addressing, though still better shown through evidence than claimed directly. See [soft skills nobody can verify](/blog/soft-skills-nobody-can-verify) for how to evidence rather than assert these when they matter.

## What This Means for Tailoring

Do not spend equal time addressing every line in a posting. Sort quickly:

**Chase these:** named tools, specific years, credentials, specific described responsibilities, team/reporting structure details.

**Skip these:** generic environment descriptions, universal soft-skill claims, legal boilerplate, "other duties as assigned."

This sorting is the difference between tailoring that targets what actually matters and tailoring that pads a resume with language addressing filler nobody is actually screening on. See [which missing skills actually matter](/blog/which-missing-skills-actually-matter) for the same triage applied to your scan results specifically.

## A Worked Example

> "We're looking for a passionate, detail-oriented Financial Analyst to join our dynamic, fast-paced team. You'll work independently and collaboratively to support month-end close. 3+ years of experience with Excel and SAP required. Must have excellent communication skills. Other duties as assigned."

Sorted:

- **Filler:** "passionate," "dynamic, fast-paced," "independently and collaboratively," "excellent communication skills," "other duties as assigned"
- **Real:** "3+ years of experience," "Excel," "SAP," "support month-end close"

Out of a sentence-heavy posting, the actual actionable content is short: years of experience, two named tools, one specific responsibility. That is what deserves your tailoring time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I ignore soft-skill language entirely?**
Not entirely — evidence it in a bullet where genuinely true and relevant to the role's actual function, rather than listing it as a bare claim. Do not spend significant tailoring time chasing it as if it were a hard requirement.

**How do I know if a specific-sounding phrase is actually boilerplate?**
Check whether it repeats identically across unrelated postings. Genuine, role-specific language rarely does.

**Is legal and benefits language worth reading at all?**
Yes, as information about the job and company — just not as something to address in your resume or cover letter.

**What if a posting is entirely filler with no specific content?**
See [decoding a vague job description](/blog/decode-a-vague-job-description) for how to extract what little real signal exists.

**Does this affect how an ATS scores my resume?**
Indirectly — matching systems generally weight specific, technical terms more than generic language, which broadly aligns with this same distinction.
