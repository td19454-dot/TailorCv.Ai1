## Key Takeaways

- Mid-career hiring — tenshoku — is now common in Japan, though it still carries more weight than in Western markets.
- Recruitment agents dominate the process and will manage negotiation on your behalf.
- Employers ask why you are leaving, and the answer is assessed more seriously than in most markets.
- Frequent job changes are viewed more critically here, so the narrative connecting your moves matters.
- On a work visa, notify immigration within 14 days and confirm the new role falls within your status.

Changing jobs in Japan used to be unusual enough to require explanation. It no longer is — mid-career hiring is a mature market with its own agencies, platforms and conventions — but the residue of the old norm survives in how employers interpret your history.

For foreign professionals there is an additional layer: your status of residence, and whether the new role fits inside it.

This guide covers the process, the questions, and the immigration steps.

## How tenshoku works

**Recruitment agents dominate.** Far more than in the US or UK, mid-career hiring in Japan runs through agents. Bilingual agencies serving the foreign professional market are particularly useful, because they know which employers actually hire non-Japanese candidates.

**What agents do here:** present you to clients, coordinate the process, and — importantly — **handle salary negotiation on your behalf**. That last point is a genuine advantage in a culture where negotiating directly can read as pushy, as [the Japan salary negotiation guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-salary-negotiation-guide) covers.

**Direct applications** are possible through job platforms and company sites, though agents carry a large share of the mid-career market.

**Referrals** are growing, particularly at startups and gaishikei.

**Timeline:** typically one to three months from first contact to offer, faster at foreign-owned companies.

## The documents

Japan expects two documents, not a resume:

**Rirekisho** — the standardised personal history form with photo, education and employment dates.

**Shokumu keirekisho** — the free-format career history where you actually make your case. This is the one hiring managers read and the one that decides interviews — see [the shokumu keirekisho guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/shokumu-keirekisho-guide) and [the rirekisho guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japanese-resume-rirekisho-guide).

**For mid-career applications the shokumu keirekisho matters most**, because your track record is what you are being hired for. Lead with a summary block, quantify achievements, and give context for any employer a Japanese hiring manager would not recognise.

## Why are you leaving?

Asked in essentially every process, and weighted more heavily than in most markets.

**What the interviewer is assessing:** whether you leave when things get difficult, and whether you will do the same to them.

**What works:** a forward-looking reason grounded in what you want to do next.

> I've spent four years building payment infrastructure and I want to work on it at larger scale. My current company's platform is mature and the interesting problems there are largely solved.

**What does not work:**

- Criticising your current employer, however justified — it reads as something you will repeat about them
- Money as the primary stated reason
- Vagueness — "I wanted a new challenge" is heard constantly and says nothing

**If you have changed jobs frequently**, address it before you are asked. Japanese employers view a pattern of short tenures more critically than Western ones, so a narrative connecting the moves — a consistent direction, deepening specialism — matters. Silence invites the least generous interpretation.

## The visa steps foreign workers miss

Three things, and people get caught by all of them.

**1. Check the new role fits your status.** Your status of residence covers a category of work. Moving between software roles is generally fine; moving into a different field may require a change of status. You can request a **certificate of authorised employment** from immigration confirming the new role qualifies — worth doing before accepting rather than after. See [the Japan engineer visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-engineer-visa-guide).

**2. Notify immigration within 14 days** of leaving and of joining. This is a legal obligation, not a courtesy, and missing it creates problems at renewal.

**3. Time your renewal carefully.** If your period of stay expires soon after a job change, immigration will assess the new employment. A very short tenure at the new employer at renewal time can complicate things, so where possible avoid changing jobs immediately before a renewal.

**Also worth knowing:** a "Designated Activities" status can cover a job-hunting period after leaving a role in some circumstances, which matters if you resign before securing the next position.

## Resigning

**Notice.** Japanese law provides for a relatively short statutory notice in many cases, but **company rules and convention frequently expect one to three months**, and leaving abruptly damages relationships in a market where reputation travels.

**The conversation.** Tell your direct manager first, in person, before anyone else hears it. Expect them to try to retain you.

**Written notice** follows the verbal conversation, usually a short formal letter.

**Handover is taken seriously.** Thorough documentation and a proper transfer are expected, and doing it well is remembered — see [the resignation letter guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/resignation-letter-guide-2026) and [notice period negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/notice-period-negotiation-guide).

**Do not resign before the new offer is confirmed in writing**, including any conditions.

## Common Mistakes

**Criticising your current employer when asked why you are leaving.** It is heard as something you will later say about them, regardless of how justified it is.

**Giving money as the stated reason.** It lands poorly even where it is the truth; a forward-looking reason about the work performs far better.

**Not addressing a pattern of short tenures.** Japanese employers weight it more critically than Western ones, and silence invites the least generous reading.

**Sending a Western resume.** Applications expect a rirekisho and shokumu keirekisho, and the latter is what actually gets you interviewed.

**Changing jobs without checking your status covers the new role.** Moving outside your category may require a change of status, and a certificate of authorised employment resolves it in advance.

**Missing the 14-day immigration notifications.** They are legal obligations and missing them causes problems at renewal.

**Changing jobs immediately before a visa renewal.** A very short tenure at the new employer complicates the assessment.

**Resigning abruptly.** Convention frequently expects one to three months regardless of statutory minimums, and reputation travels in this market.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is changing jobs normal in Japan now?

Yes — mid-career hiring is a mature market with its own agencies and platforms, though frequent moves are still viewed more critically than in Western markets.

### Should I use a recruitment agent?

Generally yes. Agents carry a large share of mid-career hiring, and bilingual agencies know which employers hire foreign professionals. They also negotiate salary on your behalf.

### What do I send instead of a resume?

A rirekisho and a shokumu keirekisho. The latter is free-format and is what hiring managers actually read.

### How should I answer "why are you leaving"?

With a forward-looking reason about the work you want to do next. Avoid criticising your employer and avoid citing money as the primary reason.

### Do I need to tell immigration when I change jobs?

Yes, within 14 days of both leaving and joining. It is a legal obligation and missing it causes problems at renewal.

### Can I change to a different type of work?

Only if it falls within your status of residence, otherwise you need a change of status. Request a certificate of authorised employment to confirm before accepting.

### How much notice should I give?

Statutory minimums are relatively short, but company rules and convention frequently expect one to three months. Leaving abruptly damages your standing.

## Make This Practical

Confirm your status covers the new role before you accept anything. Request a certificate of authorised employment from immigration if the job sits anywhere near the boundary of your category — it converts a risk into a document, and it is far easier to do before resigning than after.

Then prepare the question that decides mid-career interviews here. Write a forward-looking answer to "why are you leaving" that names what you want to build next, never criticises your current employer, and — if your history shows short tenures — supplies the narrative connecting them before anyone has to ask.

Finally, handle the mechanics properly. Use a bilingual agent to manage the process and the salary conversation, submit a rirekisho and shokumu keirekisho rather than a Western resume, notify immigration within 14 days of both dates, and avoid changing jobs immediately before a visa renewal.
