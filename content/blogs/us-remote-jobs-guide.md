## Key Takeaways

- Remote postings attract several times the applications of equivalent onsite roles, so referrals matter even more.
- Many "remote" roles are restricted to specific states, because employers must register for payroll in each one.
- Geo-adjusted pay is now common — the same role can pay materially differently by your location.
- "Remote-first" and "remote-friendly" are genuinely different, and the distinction predicts your experience.
- Working from another country on a US payroll is usually not permitted, whatever the posting implies.

Remote work stopped being novel and became a normal option with its own rules — rules that are less obvious than they look. A posting saying "Remote (US)" can mean anything from work anywhere in the country to work in one of eleven approved states at a salary adjusted to where you live.

This guide covers how to read those postings, where to find genuinely remote roles, and how to compete when hundreds of others are applying.

## The three kinds of remote

**Remote-first.** The company was built distributed. Meetings assume video, decisions are written down, and there is no headquarters where the real conversations happen. This is the version that works well long-term.

**Remote-friendly / hybrid.** There is an office, most people are in it some days, and remote employees are a minority. Workable, but you will miss context that circulates informally, and promotion can be slower for people who are never in the room. Ask directly how many people on the team are fully remote.

**Temporarily remote.** The role is expected to return onsite, or "remote for now". Ask explicitly, in writing, because this changes the job.

The distinction matters more than the salary. A remote-friendly role at a company with a strong office culture is a different job from a remote-first one, and the difference shows up in your career trajectory rather than your first month.

## Why "Remote (US)" often is not

**State payroll registration.** An employer must register with the tax authorities of every state where it has an employee. Registering in a new state costs money and creates ongoing compliance obligations, so many companies limit hiring to states where they are already set up.

That is why postings say "Remote — must reside in CA, NY, TX, WA, CO, or IL". It is not arbitrary; it is administrative.

**What to do:** check the state list before applying, and if your state is excluded, it is generally not negotiable for a junior or mid-level role. It occasionally is for a senior hire the company particularly wants.

**Working from abroad is a separate question**, and the answer is usually no. Tax residency, permanent establishment risk, and employment law all make it complicated. Roles that genuinely allow working from anywhere internationally exist but are rare, and usually contractor arrangements rather than employment — see [W2 vs 1099 explained](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/us-w2-vs-1099-explained).

## Geo-adjusted pay

Increasingly standard, and worth understanding before you negotiate.

Many employers set salary by your location rather than by the role — the same job pays a San Francisco band to someone in San Francisco and a lower band to someone in a cheaper metro. Some companies publish their tiers; others do not.

**Practical consequences:**

- Moving from a high-cost to a low-cost area can trigger a pay cut at the same employer. Ask about the policy before relocating.
- A remote role paying a national band regardless of location is the best version of this and is worth seeking out specifically.
- When negotiating, know whether the band is location-based — it changes what "the range" means. See [US salary negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/us-salary-negotiation-guide).

This is the arithmetic behind choosing where to live while working remotely, and it interacts with state income tax — no-tax states like Texas and Washington look better, though geo-adjustment may claw some of it back. ## Finding genuinely remote roles

**Remote-specific job boards** filter out hybrid roles mislabelled as remote, which the general boards do not.

**LinkedIn's remote filter**, with the caveat that it is unreliable — many results are hybrid. Read the posting itself.

**Company career pages** at known remote-first employers. Several maintain public lists of the states they hire in.

**Referrals matter more here than anywhere.** Remote postings attract several times the application volume of equivalent onsite roles, because the candidate pool is national rather than local. ## Competing for remote roles

**Evidence of remote competence.** Employers screen for whether you can work without supervision. Prior remote experience is the obvious signal; if you lack it, demonstrate the adjacent skills — written communication, self-direction, working across time zones.

**Written communication is a hiring criterion**, not a soft skill. Your application email and your interview follow-ups are being read as work samples. A remote-first company is assessing whether you can write clearly, because that is how their organisation runs.

**Overlap hours.** State your time zone and your availability. "Available 9-5 ET" or "can overlap until 1pm PT" removes a question the employer would otherwise have to ask.

**Résumé signal.** If you have worked remotely before, label it — "Remote" next to the role. It answers the screening question immediately.

## Interviewing remotely

Every stage will be on video, and the setup is part of the assessment for a remote role in a way it is not for an onsite one.

- **Test the platform beforehand**, including screen sharing if there is a technical exercise
- **Camera at eye level**, neutral background, good front lighting
- **Wired connection** if possible, and a backup — tether to your phone if the connection drops
- **Look at the camera** when speaking, not at the faces
- **Have water and notes** just off-camera

## Common Mistakes

**Not checking the state list.** Many "Remote (US)" roles are restricted to states where the employer already runs payroll, and applying from outside that list is wasted effort.

**Assuming remote means work from anywhere.** Working abroad on a US payroll is usually not permitted for tax and employment-law reasons, whatever the posting seems to imply.

**Ignoring geo-adjustment before relocating.** Moving to a cheaper metro can trigger a pay cut at the same employer, and the policy is much easier to ask about beforehand.

**Treating remote-friendly as remote-first.** At a company with a real office, remote employees miss informal context and can progress more slowly — ask how many on the team are fully remote.

**Relying on cold applications.** Remote postings draw a national candidate pool and several times the volume, which makes referrals disproportionately important.

**Neglecting written communication in the application.** At a remote-first company your emails are work samples, and clumsy writing is a genuine screening signal.

**Not stating your time zone.** Leaving overlap hours unspecified adds a question the employer has to resolve, and ambiguity loses to specificity.

**Skipping the technical setup check.** For a remote role the quality of your video call is part of the assessment, not a neutral background detail.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do remote jobs list specific states?

Employers must register for payroll and tax in every state where they have an employee, which costs money and creates ongoing obligations. Most limit hiring to states where they are already registered.

### Can I work from another country on a US remote job?

Usually not. Tax residency, permanent establishment risk and employment law make it complicated, and most US employers prohibit it. Genuinely international arrangements are typically contractor roles.

### What is geo-adjusted pay?

Salary set by your location rather than by the role, so the same job pays differently in different metros. Ask whether the band is location-based before negotiating or relocating.

### Are remote jobs harder to get?

More competitive, yes — the candidate pool is national rather than local, so volume per posting is much higher. Referrals matter correspondingly more.

### How do I show I can work remotely without prior remote experience?

Demonstrate the underlying skills: clear written communication, self-direction, and experience coordinating across locations or time zones. Your application writing itself is evidence.

### Should I label remote roles on my resume?

Yes. Marking a role "Remote" answers a screening question immediately and signals you have done it successfully before.

### What is the difference between remote-first and remote-friendly?

Remote-first companies were built distributed, so nothing important happens only in a room. Remote-friendly companies have an office where informal context circulates, and remote staff can miss it.

## Make This Practical

Read the posting properly before applying. Check the state list, check whether pay is geo-adjusted, and check whether the role is remote-first or an office job that currently allows remote — those three facts determine both your eligibility and what the job will actually be like.

Then compete on the things remote employers screen for. Label prior remote work on your resume, state your time zone and overlap hours explicitly, and treat every email in the process as a writing sample, because at a distributed company it is one.

Finally, use referrals rather than volume.
