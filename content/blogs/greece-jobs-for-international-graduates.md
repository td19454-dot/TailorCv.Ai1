## Key Takeaways

- Greece went through a **severe decade-long crisis** and has been recovering; salaries remain low and the market is meaningfully better than it was.
- **Shipping is Greece's world-leading industry** — Greek owners control one of the largest shares of global merchant tonnage, and Piraeus is a genuine international maritime centre.
- **Tourism is the largest employer**, and it has professionalised considerably beyond seasonal work.
- **Athens has a growing technology and shared services sector**, with international companies establishing operations.
- **Greek is required for most of the domestic market**; shipping, technology and tourism management have substantial English-working segments.

## The honest starting point

Greece deserves a direct account because the story is genuinely mixed.

**The crisis was severe.** A decade of contraction, austerity and very high unemployment left deep marks, and a large number of educated young Greeks emigrated — a brain drain that is well documented and still partly ongoing.

**Recovery has been real.** Growth returned, unemployment fell substantially from its peak, and international investment has picked up meaningfully.

**Salaries remain low.** Greek pay is among the lowest in the eurozone, and this is the central constraint for anyone considering it.

**And yet specific sectors are world-class.** Shipping in particular is an industry where Greece is genuinely dominant globally, not merely present.

**The practical resolution:** Greece works if you are in shipping, in a specific international sector, or bringing your income with you. A generalist search in the domestic market is one of the harder propositions in the EU.

## Shipping, the industry that matters

This is Greece's genuine world-leading sector and it is chronically under-known by graduates.

**Greek owners control an extraordinary share of world merchant tonnage** — in tankers, bulk carriers and increasingly containers. This is not a regional industry; it is a globally dominant one run from Athens.

**Piraeus is the operational centre**, with shipowners, managers, brokers, insurers, classification societies, lawyers and technical services all concentrated there.

**The roles are varied.** Chartering, operations, technical management, marine engineering, ship finance, insurance and claims, sale and purchase broking, and increasingly maritime technology and decarbonisation work.

**It runs in English.** Shipping is an international industry with English as its working language, and Greek shipping offices operate accordingly.

**It pays well by Greek standards** and, at senior levels, internationally.

**And it hires internationally**, though the industry is relationship-driven and entry usually comes through a graduate scheme, a specialist qualification or a connection.

**For a graduate with an interest in maritime, logistics, commodities or shipping finance, Athens is one of the most interesting places in the world** — and almost nobody outside the industry knows it.

## Money and cost of living

The financial picture, stated plainly.

**Salaries are among the lowest in the eurozone.** Greek minimum and median wages sit near the bottom of the currency union, and graduate pay reflects that.

**Fourteen payments are standard** in many arrangements — twelve monthly plus holiday bonuses — so clarify what an annual figure includes.

**Social contributions are substantial** on both sides, funding healthcare and pensions.

**Costs are genuinely low** outside central Athens. Food, transport, eating out and daily life all cost noticeably less than in Northern Europe, which offsets a meaningful part of the salary gap.

**Athens housing has risen**, driven partly by short-term rentals and international demand, and it is the main pressure point.

**Thessaloniki and the smaller cities are considerably cheaper.**

**Healthcare is public** through contributions, with a private sector widely used alongside.

**And the honest arithmetic:** a Greek salary supports a comfortable life outside central Athens and a constrained one inside it, which is the same asymmetry that shapes decisions in Portugal and Spain.

## Setting up

The administrative sequence.

**AFM** — the tax registration number, needed for essentially every transaction including a lease and a bank account.

**AMKA** — the social security number, required for employment and healthcare.

**Bank account**, which needs the AFM.

**Residence registration** or the EU registration certificate depending on your nationality.

**Tax registration**, mostly handled through payroll for employees.

**And expect paperwork.** Greek administration has digitised considerably through the gov.gr platform, which has genuinely improved things, but it remains more document-heavy than Estonia or the Nordics.

## Where roles are advertised

Greece's channels.

**Kariera.gr** is the dominant national job board.

**Skywalker.gr** carries substantial volume including public and NGO roles.

**LinkedIn works** for the international sector, shipping and Athens generally.

**Shipping-specific channels** matter enormously if that is your target — industry publications, the Greek shipping associations, and specialist recruiters who place across Piraeus.

**Company career pages** for the large employers and international companies with Greek operations.

**University career services** if you studied in Greece.

**And referrals above all**, given how much personal recommendation matters in this market.

## Working culture

Warm, relationship-led and more hierarchical than Northern Europe.

**Relationships come before transactions.** Time spent building rapport is part of the work.

**Hierarchies are respected** and decisions travel upward, particularly in family-owned businesses which are a large share of the Greek economy.

**Hours can be long** in the private sector, and the working day starts and finishes later than in Northern Europe.

**August largely stops.** The summer holiday period genuinely empties workplaces.

**Family businesses are everywhere**, and the dynamics of working in one differ from a corporate environment — decisions can be fast or opaque depending on the family.

**Shipping is its own culture**, international in outlook, English-speaking and considerably more corporate than the domestic norm.

**And warmth is genuine.** Greek colleagues are notably welcoming to foreigners, and the social barrier is low compared with Northern Europe.

## Building a career from Greece

Where the market leads, which matters given the salary reality.

**Shipping experience travels globally.** Piraeus is one of a handful of world maritime centres, and experience there is recognised in London, Singapore, Hamburg, Dubai and Hong Kong. This is by a wide margin the most portable credential Greece offers.

**International company experience** in Athens makes an internal transfer abroad considerably easier later.

**Tourism management experience** transfers across the Mediterranean and beyond, and the sector has professionalised enough that senior roles are genuine careers.

**Greek is useful beyond Greece** in Cyprus and in the shipping diaspora.

**And the common pattern** is a few years in Greece building sector-specific expertise, then either an international move within the same industry or a decision to stay and accept the salary trade for the life. Both are legitimate, and shipping in particular makes the first genuinely easy.

## Common misreadings of the Greek market

Four beliefs that mislead people.

**"Greece is still in crisis."** The acute phase ended years ago. Growth returned, unemployment fell substantially from its peak, and investment picked up. Salaries remain low, which is a different problem from a collapsing economy.

**"There is nothing but tourism."** Shipping is a globally dominant Greek industry, and the tourism sector itself has professionalised well beyond seasonal work.

**"Everything is in Athens."** Thessaloniki has a real market, and Piraeus — while part of greater Athens — is a distinct professional world.

**"I can get by with English."** In shipping and the international sector, yes. In the domestic market, no, and this is the constraint most people underestimate.

## Thessaloniki and the second cities

Worth a specific look, since Athens dominates the conversation.

**Thessaloniki** is Greece's second city, with Aristotle University producing a large graduate population, a growing technology scene, and a logistics role serving the Balkans.

**Costs are meaningfully lower** than Athens, and the city is compact and liveable.

**International companies have established operations** there, including technology and shared services.

**Patras and Heraklion** have university bases and smaller professional markets.

**And the islands** are tourism-dominated with strong seasonality, though hotel groups increasingly offer year-round management careers.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are interested in shipping, maritime or commodities

Athens is genuinely one of the best places in the world for you, and this is the single strongest argument for Greece. Piraeus concentrates an industry that Greece leads globally, and it runs in English.

### Scenario 2: You work remotely for a foreign employer

Greece introduced a digital nomad route and a favourable tax provision for newcomers. Southern European costs against a foreign salary is the strongest financial arrangement available here.

### Scenario 3: You want a general graduate job in the domestic market

Be realistic. Greek salaries are among the lowest in the eurozone and the domestic market requires Greek. Target the international sector or a specific specialism instead.

> **Tip:** If you have any interest in shipping, logistics or commodities, look at Piraeus seriously. Greek owners control one of the largest shares of the world's merchant fleet, the industry runs in English, and almost no international graduate thinks to apply there.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Target shipping if the sector interests you at all
- Consider the digital nomad route if you work remotely
- Look at Athens' growing international sector
- Learn Greek for the domestic market
- Compare salary against Athens rent specifically
- Consider Thessaloniki as a lower-cost alternative

### Don't

- Run a generalist domestic search
- Expect eurozone-average salaries
- Assume tourism means only seasonal work
- Overlook Piraeus and the maritime cluster
- Assume the crisis-era picture still applies
- Underestimate how much Greek the domestic market needs

- **Ignoring shipping.** Greece's genuinely world-leading industry and the least-known thing about its economy.

- **A generalist domestic search.** One of the harder propositions in the EU.
- **Dismissing tourism.** It has professionalised well beyond seasonal work.

- **Overlooking the tax provision** for newcomers, which is genuinely favourable.

For shipping, the international sector or remote work, genuinely yes. For a generalist search in the domestic market it is among the harder propositions in the EU, with salaries near the bottom of the eurozone.

Greek owners control one of the largest shares of the world's merchant fleet, run from Athens and Piraeus, with the full ecosystem of managers, brokers, financiers and technical services around it.

### Do I need to speak Greek?

For the domestic market, yes. Shipping runs in English as an international industry, and technology, shared services and tourism management have substantial English-working segments.

Substantially. Growth returned, unemployment fell a long way from its peak, and investment picked up. Salaries remain low, which is the enduring constraint.

Thessaloniki has a university base, a growing technology scene and a logistics role, at meaningfully lower cost than Athens.
