## Key Takeaways

- Most body language advice is overstated — interviewers are assessing your answers, not decoding your posture.
- A handful of things genuinely register: eye contact, visible nervous habits, energy and how you handle silence.
- Nervous fidgeting is distracting rather than damning, and it is the most fixable item.
- On video, looking at the camera and having your face lit matter more than posture.
- Recording yourself is the only reliable way to find your own habits.

Body language occupies more space in interview advice than it deserves. Interviewers are not analysing your posture for hidden meaning; they are trying to work out whether you can do the job.

That said, a small number of things genuinely affect how you come across, mostly by distracting from what you are saying. Those are worth fixing, and the rest is worth ignoring.

This guide separates the two.

## What actually registers

**Eye contact.** Not staring — natural contact while speaking and listening. Consistently looking away reads as discomfort or evasion, whether or not it is.

**In a panel**, address whoever asked the question primarily, with periodic contact across the others. Speaking only to the most senior person is noticed.

**Nervous habits.** Clicking a pen, jiggling a leg, touching your face repeatedly, playing with your hair. These are distracting rather than damning — the interviewer starts watching the habit instead of hearing the answer.

**Energy.** Speaking with some animation reads as interest. Flat delivery reads as disengagement, even from a candidate who genuinely wants the role. This is worth more than any posture adjustment.

**Handling silence.** Filling every pause with "um" while thinking is more noticeable than a brief silence. A short pause before answering reads as considered.

**Speaking pace.** Nervousness speeds people up, and fast delivery costs comprehension. Deliberately slowing down improves clarity more than any other single change — see [campus placement communication skills](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/campus-placement-communication-skills).

## What to ignore

**Power poses and dominance postures.** Not assessed, and forcing them makes you uncomfortable, which is assessed.

**Mirroring the interviewer.** Attempting it consciously is distracting for you and occasionally visible to them.

**Precise handshake technique.** Firm, brief, done. Nobody scores it.

**Elaborate rules about hand placement.** Natural gestures while speaking are fine and better than rigid stillness.

**Trying to project confidence through posture** while your answers are disorganised. Structure fixes far more than posture does.

## The realistic version

**Sit reasonably upright** — leaning back reads as disengaged, hunching forward reads as tense. Comfortable and alert.

**Let your hands do what they naturally do** while talking, but keep them off your face and away from objects you can fiddle with.

**Remove the fidget objects.** Do not hold a pen you do not need. Put your phone away rather than in your hand. This solves most nervous habits mechanically rather than through willpower.

**Nod while listening**, which signals engagement without interrupting.

**Smile at the start and end**, naturally rather than continuously.

## On video

Different priorities, because the frame changes what is visible.

**Look at the camera when speaking**, not at the faces on screen. Looking at their video appears as looking down — see [remote interview setup](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/mock-interview-remote-setup).

**Your face must be lit.** A dark silhouette makes expression invisible, which removes most of your nonverbal communication entirely.

**Camera at eye level.** An upward angle reads as less engaged regardless of your actual posture.

**Sit slightly further back** than feels natural, so your hands are visible when you gesture. Gestures cropped out of frame are lost.

**Posture below the shoulders is invisible**, so the effort belongs in framing and lighting rather than in how you sit.

## Nerves

Body language problems are usually nerves, so treating the nerves is more effective than managing the symptoms.

**Preparation reduces them most.** Knowing your six stories cold removes the underlying source — see [how to practise mock interviews](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/mock-interview-how-to-practice).

**Arrive early enough to settle**, in person or on the call.

**Slow your breathing** before starting. Two slow breaths measurably steadies your voice.

**Accept some nervousness.** Interviewers expect it and largely discount it. Trying to appear completely relaxed usually produces something stranger than mild visible nerves.

**Nerves fade once you are talking about something you know**, which is another argument for a well-prepared introduction.

## Finding your own habits

You cannot fix what you have not seen.

**Record yourself** answering two questions and watch it back. Your habits will be immediately obvious to you and were invisible before — this is the same mechanism that makes recorded practice the highest-return preparation available.

**Ask a practice partner** to note any repeated movement.

**Fix one at a time.** Correcting several simultaneously produces someone visibly managing themselves rather than answering questions.

## Common Mistakes

**Overthinking posture while under-preparing answers.** Structure and content are what is assessed; posture only matters when it distracts.

**Holding a pen or phone.** Removing the object solves most fidgeting mechanically, which works better than trying to stop.

**Flat, low-energy delivery.** It reads as disinterest even from candidates who want the role badly.

**Looking at the screen rather than the camera on video.** It appears as looking down for the whole interview.

**Sitting in the dark on a video call.** An unlit face removes your expression entirely, which is most of your nonverbal communication.

**Filling every pause with "um".** A brief silence reads as considered; verbal filler reads as uncertain.

**Attempting power poses or deliberate mirroring.** Neither is assessed, and forcing them creates visible discomfort that is.

**Trying to appear entirely relaxed.** Interviewers expect nerves and discount them; overcorrection looks stranger than the nerves would have.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does body language really affect interview outcomes?

Less than most advice suggests. It matters mainly when it distracts from your answers — visible fidgeting, flat energy or avoided eye contact.

### How much eye contact is right?

Natural contact while speaking and listening, without staring. In a panel, address whoever asked while periodically including the others.

### What should I do with my hands?

Whatever they naturally do while you speak. Remove pens and phones so there is nothing to fidget with, and keep hands away from your face.

### Do interviewers notice nervousness?

Yes, and they largely discount it. Attempting to appear completely relaxed usually reads stranger than mild visible nerves.

### What matters most on video?

Looking at the camera rather than the screen, and having your face properly lit. Posture below the shoulders is not even visible.

### How do I find my own nervous habits?

Record yourself answering two questions and watch it back. Habits invisible from the inside are immediately obvious on playback.

### Should I smile throughout?

No — naturally at the start and end, and where it fits. Continuous smiling looks forced.

## Make This Practical

Record yourself answering two questions and watch it back. Your own nervous habits are invisible from the inside and immediately obvious on playback, and this is the only reliable way to find them.

Then fix the mechanical things rather than the abstract ones. Put the pen down and the phone away so there is nothing to fidget with, deliberately slow your speaking pace, and — on video — look at the camera rather than the screen and make sure your face is lit.

Finally, redirect the effort. Energy and clear structure affect how you are received far more than posture does, so treat the underlying nerves by preparing your six stories properly rather than by managing symptoms during the interview itself.
