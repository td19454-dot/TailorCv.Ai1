## Key Takeaways

- **EPSO open competitions** recruit permanent officials and are extremely competitive, running over roughly a year with multiple stages.
- **Contract agent (CAST) posts, traineeships and temporary agent roles** are far more accessible and are how a large share of people actually enter.
- The **Blue Book traineeship** at the Commission is the single best-known graduate entry point and runs twice yearly.
- **Two EU languages are required** for most roles, and language is a genuine filter rather than a formality.
- The **wider Brussels ecosystem** — associations, consultancies, NGOs, law firms, corporate affairs — employs far more people than the institutions themselves.

## The institutions and who works in them

Worth being concrete about what exists.

**The European Commission** is the largest [employer](/blog/how-recruiters-spot-generic-resumes), with directorates-general covering every policy area.

**The Council of the European Union** and the **European Parliament** each have substantial secretariats, and the Parliament additionally has political groups and individual MEP offices that hire on their own terms.

**The Court of Justice** in Luxembourg, the **European Central Bank** in Frankfurt, and the **European Investment Bank** in Luxembourg.

**Agencies across Europe** — the European Medicines Agency in Amsterdam, ENISA, EFSA, Frontex, EASA and dozens more, each recruiting separately.

**The European External Action Service**, the EU's diplomatic arm.

**Staff categories matter.** Administrators (AD) do policy and management work and typically require a [degree](/blog/how-to-list-education-on-resume). Assistants (AST) do support and technical work. Contract agents (CA) are employed on fixed terms in defined function groups. Temporary agents (TA) fill specific posts, particularly in agencies.

## EPSO competitions, realistically

The permanent official route, and an honest account of it.

**Competitions are announced periodically** for specific profiles — generalist administrators, lawyers, economists, translators, and so on.

**The process runs over roughly a year** and includes computer-based tests, situational judgement assessment, and an assessment centre.

**Success rates are low.** Thousands apply for a reserve list of a few hundred, and being on the reserve list is not a job — it means you may be recruited from it.

**[Language requirements](/blog/bilingual-language-skills-on-resume) are real.** You compete in one language and are tested in a second, and the combinations available are specified in the notice.

**It is worth doing if you want a permanent [career](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide) there**, and it is a poor plan if you need employment this year.

**Prepare properly.** The tests are specific and practising the format genuinely improves scores — this is not a competition where raw intelligence substitutes for familiarity.

## The routes people actually take

This is the section that is usually missing from advice about EU careers.

**The Blue Book traineeship.** The European Commission runs paid traineeships twice a year, five months each, for [graduates](/blog/first-resume-for-gen-z-2026). It is competitive but far more accessible than a competition, it pays a monthly grant, and it is the single most common first step into the Brussels ecosystem.

**Traineeships at Parliament, Council, agencies and the EEAS** run on their own calendars with their own [applications](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day).

**CAST — contract agent selection.** A permanent open call where you submit a profile that [recruiters](/blog/how-recruiters-read-resumes) search when they need someone. Function groups run from FG I (manual and administrative) to FG IV (administrative, advisory, linguistic and technical tasks requiring a degree). This is a genuine, ongoing, accessible route and a great many people are unaware it exists.

**Temporary agent posts** are advertised individually, particularly by agencies, and often require specific technical expertise rather than generalist ability.

**Seconded National Experts** come from national administrations for a defined period.

**Interim and agency staff** fill short-term needs, and it is a real if precarious way in.

**The realistic path for most graduates:** traineeship, then contract agent or a job in the surrounding ecosystem, then possibly a competition later once you know the institutions from inside.

## Preparing for EPSO tests

If you do pursue a competition, format familiarity is the difference between passing and not.

**Verbal, numerical and abstract reasoning** tests come first, in your chosen language, under time pressure. These are learnable and practice materially improves scores.

**Situational judgement tests** assess how you would respond to workplace scenarios against the institutions' competency framework. Understanding what they are actually testing for changes your answers.

**The competency framework matters.** Analysis and problem solving, communicating, delivering quality and results, learning and development, prioritising and organising, resilience, working with others and leadership. Every stage assesses against these, and framing your examples around them is not gaming the system — it is answering the question asked.

**The assessment centre** includes a case study, a group exercise, a structured [interview](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-2026-complete-guide-for-every-industry) and often an oral presentation.

**Language testing is real throughout.** You work in one language and are assessed in a second.

**Practise with the official sample tests first**, then commercial materials. The official ones tell you the format; the commercial ones give you volume.

## Life and pay in the institutions

Worth knowing before you invest a year in a competition.

**Pay is set by grade and step**, published openly, with allowances for expatriation, household and dependent children where applicable.

**The expatriation allowance** is significant and applies to staff working outside their own country under defined conditions.

**A distinct tax arrangement applies** — EU staff pay a Community tax rather than Belgian income tax, which is a substantial difference from working in the Brussels private sector.

**European Schools** exist for staff children, teaching in multiple language sections.

**Job security is high** for permanent officials and considerably lower for contract and temporary agents, whose contracts have defined durations and renewal limits.

**Mobility within the institutions** is a genuine feature — people move between directorates-general, agencies and duty stations across a career.

**And the work is genuinely policy work**, which is either exactly what you want or slower and more procedural than you would like. Knowing which you are is worth more than any test preparation.

## The wider Brussels policy ecosystem

Larger than the institutions and considerably easier to enter.

**Trade associations.** Every industry in Europe maintains a Brussels presence to engage with the institutions — pharmaceuticals, chemicals, technology, agriculture, energy, [finance](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs). They hire policy officers, analysts and communications people continuously.

**Corporate EU affairs offices.** Large companies run their own Brussels operations doing the same work.

**Public affairs [consultancies](/blog/case-interview-preparation-guide-2026).** Firms advising clients on EU policy, from large international agencies to small specialist shops.

**Law firms** with EU competition, regulatory and trade practices.

**NGOs and think tanks.** Environmental, development, digital rights, consumer and human rights organisations, plus policy research institutes.

**Media.** Politico Europe, EURACTIV, Agence Europe and the Brussels bureaux of international outlets.

**Why this matters:** these organisations hire on ordinary commercial timelines rather than through year-long competitions, they value the same [skills](/blog/resume-skills-match-job-description), and moving between them and the institutions is entirely normal over a career. For most graduates this is the realistic entry point into EU policy work.

## Building a Brussels career from outside

Concrete steps if you are not already there.

**Do a traineeship.** Blue Book, Parliament, an agency, or one at an association or NGO. It puts you inside the network.

**Learn the policy area, not just the institutions.** Employers hire people who understand energy regulation or data protection or agricultural policy, not people who have read about how the Commission works.

**Follow the legislative calendar.** Knowing which files are moving tells you where hiring is about to happen.

**Attend the events.** Brussels runs a dense calendar of policy conferences, most of them open, and turning up repeatedly gets you known in a small professional world.

**Write something public.** A blog on a specific policy file, a substack, [contributions](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer) to a specialist outlet. In a town that runs on expertise and visibility, being findable on a topic works.

**And build a second language properly.** French for Brussels social and professional life, and one more EU language for institutional routes.

## Realistic timelines

What to expect, so you can plan rather than hope.

**A traineeship application to selection** runs several months, with two intakes a year at the Commission and separate calendars elsewhere.

**A CAST profile can sit for a long time** before a recruiter contacts you, or forever. It costs an afternoon to create and there is no downside to having one.

**A temporary agent vacancy** runs on a normal recruitment timeline — weeks to a few months.

**An EPSO competition** runs roughly a year from notice to reserve list, and recruitment from the list can take a further year or more.

**Which means:** if you need employment within six months, target traineeships, contract agent posts, agencies and the surrounding ecosystem. If you are planning a decade-long career, start a competition in parallel and treat it as a background process.

**Nothing about this is fast**, and treating the institutions as your only option while a competition runs is how people spend two years underemployed.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are a recent graduate who wants in

Apply for the Blue Book traineeship, and submit a CAST profile at the same time. Those two together are the realistic entry, and a competition is a thing to consider two years later.

### Scenario 2: You have specific technical expertise

Look at agency temporary agent posts rather than generalist competitions. Agencies hire for specific expertise — cyber security, aviation safety, medicines regulation — and the competition is far narrower.

### Scenario 3: You want to work in EU policy but not in an institution

The surrounding ecosystem is larger than the institutions and hires continuously — trade associations, corporate EU affairs, consultancies, law firms and NGOs. Many people move between it and the institutions repeatedly.

> **Tip:** Submit a CAST contract agent profile even while you pursue other routes. It is a permanently open call, recruiters search it when they need someone, and a great many people who would qualify simply never create one.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Apply for traineeships as the realistic first step
- Create and maintain a CAST profile
- Monitor agencies separately from the institutions
- Practise the specific test formats for competitions
- Include the wider Brussels ecosystem in your search
- Build a second EU language seriously

### Don't

- Treat EPSO competitions as your only route
- Assume a reserve list place is a [job offer](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email)
- Ignore contract agent and temporary agent posts
- Apply to a competition without practising the format
- Overlook agencies outside Brussels
- Underestimate the language requirement

## Common Mistakes

- **Only trying competitions.** They take a year and admit very few.
- **Never creating a CAST profile.** It is permanently open and recruiters search it.
- **Assuming a reserve list is employment.** It means you may be recruited, not that you will be.
- **Ignoring the agencies.** They hire for specific expertise with far less competition.
- **Underpreparing for the tests.** Format familiarity genuinely changes scores.
- **Missing the surrounding ecosystem.** It is larger than the institutions and hires continuously.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an EPSO competition?

The selection process for permanent EU officials, running roughly a year with computer-based tests, situational judgement assessment and an assessment centre. Success rates are low.

### What is the Blue Book traineeship?

The European Commission's paid five-month traineeship for graduates, run twice yearly. It is the most common first step into the Brussels ecosystem.

### What is a contract agent and how do I become one?

Staff employed on fixed terms in defined function groups. You submit a profile to the permanently open CAST call, and recruiters search it when they have a need.

### How many languages do I need?

Two EU languages for most roles, with the specific combinations set out in each notice. It is a genuine filter rather than a formality.

### Are the agencies easier to get into?

Frequently, yes. They hire for specific technical expertise rather than generalist ability, which narrows the field considerably if you have the right background.

### Do I have to work in Brussels?

No. The Court and the EIB are in Luxembourg, the ECB in Frankfurt, and agencies are spread across the whole Union.
### How should I prepare for EPSO tests?

Start with the official sample tests to learn the formats, then use commercial materials for volume. Understand the competency framework, because every stage assesses against it explicitly.

### How are EU institution staff paid and taxed?

Pay follows published grade and step scales with expatriation and household allowances. Staff pay a Community tax rather than Belgian income tax, which differs substantially from the Brussels private sector.
### What is the Brussels policy ecosystem?

Trade associations, corporate EU affairs offices, public affairs consultancies, law firms, NGOs, think tanks and specialist media — collectively far larger than the institutions and hiring on ordinary commercial timelines.

### How do I build a Brussels career from outside?

Do a traineeship to get inside the network, specialise in a policy area rather than in institutional procedure, follow the legislative calendar, attend the open events, and publish something on a specific file.
### How long do EU institution routes take?

A traineeship runs several months from application to start; a temporary agent vacancy [takes weeks](/blog/how-long-to-hear-back-after-applying) to months; an EPSO competition takes roughly a year to a reserve list, with recruitment from it potentially a year more.
