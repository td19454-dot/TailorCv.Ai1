## Key Takeaways

- Parsers extract dates to build a timeline. Inconsistent formats break that reconstruction silently.
- The safest format is consistent throughout: `Month Year - Month Year`, applied identically to every entry.
- Mixing "Present" and "Current" across different roles is a small inconsistency with an outsized parsing effect.
- Table-based date columns are a common, invisible cause of scrambled employment history.
- A real employment gap is far less costly than a poorly formatted one that reads as an error.

Dates seem like the simplest part of a resume, and they are one of the more common places where formatting quietly breaks parsing. Here is what actually goes wrong and the format that avoids it.

## Why Dates Matter So Much to a Parser

An applicant tracking system builds a structured timeline from your resume — this job, these dates, this job, those dates — and uses it to calculate total experience, check for gaps, and order your history correctly. Unlike free text, dates are expected to follow a predictable pattern, and when they do not, the parser's confidence in what it extracted drops.

A parsing failure here does not usually produce an error message you would see. It produces a silently wrong timeline: a role assigned the wrong dates, an employment gap that appears where none exists, or a miscalculated total years of experience — all invisible to you, all potentially costly.

## The Safe Format

**Pick one format and use it identically for every single entry:**

> Mar 2022 - Present
> Jun 2019 - Feb 2022
> Aug 2016 - May 2019

Or the numeric equivalent, equally fine as long as it is consistent:

> 03/2022 - Present
> 06/2019 - 02/2022
> 08/2016 - 05/2019

What breaks parsing is not either format individually — it is switching between them mid-resume.

## The Five Common Date Mistakes

**1. Mixing formats across entries.** "March 2022" in one role and "03/22" in another. Individually fine, together inconsistent in a way parsers do not always handle gracefully.

**2. Mixing "Present" and "Current."** Both mean the same thing to a human. To a parser expecting a consistent end-date marker, using both across different sections of the same resume adds unnecessary ambiguity.

**3. Omitting the day or assuming month precision when only years are given.** "2022 - 2023" without months can understate your actual tenure — that range could be eleven months or twenty-three months, and a parser calculating experience length will likely assume the shorter interpretation.

**4. Dates in table cells.** This is the highest-risk formatting mistake for dates specifically. When dates sit in a table column separate from the role and company, some parsers lose the association entirely, attaching the wrong dates to the wrong job or dropping them altogether. See [ATS formatting mistakes](/blog/ats-resume-formatting-mistakes).

**5. Dates in a different visual position per entry.** If some roles have the date on the same line as the title and others have it below the company name, the inconsistent structure makes it harder for a parser to reliably locate the pattern.

## How to Format an Employment Gap

A real gap is not the problem — an unclearly formatted one is. Do not blur or omit dates to hide a gap; this often reads worse to a parser than a clean, honest gap, because ambiguous dates can produce timeline errors that look like something is being concealed even when nothing is.

**If the gap needs no explanation on the resume itself:**
> Marketing Coordinator, Acme Inc — Jun 2019 - Feb 2022
> Marketing Manager, Beta Corp — Nov 2022 - Present

The eight-month gap is simply visible and unremarked upon — this is normal and does not require justification on the document itself.

**If you want to address a longer gap directly:**
> Career Break — Mar 2022 - Oct 2022
> Family caregiving

A single honest line, formatted with the same date convention as everything else, reads as controlled information rather than an error. See [explaining an employment gap](/blog/how-to-explain-employment-gap-on-resume-2026) for the fuller approach to framing gaps in your narrative, separate from the formatting question covered here.

## Checking Your Own Resume

**1. List every date range on your resume in a plain text file, in order.** Do they all follow the identical format?

**2. Check "Present" appears in only one place** — your current role — and consistently as "Present," not sometimes "Current" or "Ongoing."

**3. Confirm no dates sit inside a table.** If they do, this is worth fixing regardless of any other issue, since it is a common cause of scrambled employment history.

**4. Run the [copy-paste test](/blog/resume-formatting-broke-after-export)** — paste your exported file's text into a plain editor and check the dates extracted in the correct order, attached to the correct roles.

## Month-Year vs Year-Only

Month-year precision (`Mar 2022 - Feb 2024`) is generally preferable to year-only (`2022 - 2024`), for two reasons: it gives a more accurate picture of actual tenure, and it makes small gaps visible rather than hidden inside a year boundary — which reads as more transparent, not less.

The exception is very old roles, more than ten years back, where year-only is a widely accepted convention and month-level precision adds little value to either a parser or a human reader.

Gaps are often self-inflicted when people delete an unrelated job; [cutting irrelevant experience without creating a gap](/blog/cutting-experience-without-gaps) shows how to condense or group roles instead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an employment gap actually hurt my ATS score?**
Not directly — most matching focuses on skills and requirements, not gap detection specifically. The risk is a poorly formatted gap producing a scrambled or miscalculated timeline, not the gap itself.

**Should I write "Present" or an actual end date for my current job?**
"Present," consistently. It is the standard convention and any parser expecting a date range is built to recognise it.

**What if I don't remember the exact month I started a job?**
Use your best accurate estimate. Being off by a month rarely matters; a fabricated precise date that turns out wrong on a background check does.

**Is year-only ever the better choice?**
For roles more than a decade old, yes — it is a normal convention there. For anything recent, month-year is safer.

**Should dates be left-aligned or right-aligned?**
Either is fine visually, but avoid using a table or tab-stop structure to achieve the alignment — see [ATS formatting mistakes](/blog/ats-resume-formatting-mistakes) for why.
