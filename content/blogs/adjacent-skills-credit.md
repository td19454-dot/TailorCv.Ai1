## Key Takeaways

- Adjacency has three forms: a different tool, a different label, and a different context. Each needs handling differently.
- Name your actual tool or title first, then the shared capability. Never claim theirs.
- The test is whether a practitioner would accept the comparison. Redshift for Snowflake, yes. Docker for Kubernetes, no.
- Adjacency works best in bullets, where the work is described, and worst in skills lists, where it reads as a claim.
- Beyond two degrees of separation it stops being adjacency and becomes a career-change argument, which needs a different structure.

You built dashboards in Power BI and the posting says Tableau. You managed vendors and they call it supplier relationship management. You ran logistics for a hospital and the role is in retail. The work is the same. The words are not, and the person reading has thirty seconds.

The short answer: name what you actually used, then state the capability it demonstrates in their terms. "Built the reporting layer in Power BI" followed by evidence of what that involved does more than writing "Tableau" ever could — because the first is defensible and the second collapses in the first interview.

## The Three Kinds of Adjacency

They look similar on paper and they behave completely differently.

### Different tool, same job

Power BI instead of Tableau. HubSpot instead of Salesforce. GitLab CI instead of Jenkins. Redshift instead of Snowflake.

This is the strongest form. The underlying work is genuinely identical, the concepts transfer entirely, and any competent reader knows it. Most hiring managers will accept a tool substitution within the same category without hesitation.

### Different label, same work

You managed stakeholders; they call it partner management. You did quality assurance; they call it validation. You ran reporting; they call it business intelligence.

Also strong, and more common than people realise. The risk is the opposite of overclaiming — candidates frequently fail to translate at all, leaving the reader to work out that "kept department heads aligned on delivery" is stakeholder management.

### Different context, same capability

You did budget forecasting in a hospital and the role is in manufacturing. You led a team of six volunteers and the role wants people management.

This is the weakest form and needs the most support. The capability transfers but the context does not, so you have to supply the bridge rather than assume the reader builds it.

| Kind | Strength | What to lead with | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Different tool | Strong | Your tool, named plainly | Claiming their tool |
| Different label | Strong | The work, in their vocabulary | Failing to translate at all |
| Different context | Moderate | The capability and its scale | Assuming the bridge is obvious |

## The Practitioner Test

The line between legitimate adjacency and overclaiming is simple: would someone who does this work for a living accept the comparison?

Not a recruiter. Not a keyword matcher. A practitioner.

| Your experience | Their requirement | Practitioner verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Power BI | Tableau | Accepted — same category, concepts transfer |
| Redshift | Snowflake | Accepted — both columnar warehouses |
| HubSpot | Salesforce | Accepted — same job, different vendor |
| React | Vue | Accepted with a caveat — same paradigm |
| Docker | Kubernetes | **Rejected** — containers are not orchestration |
| Excel modelling | SQL | **Rejected** — different discipline entirely |
| Managing two juniors | Managing a team of fifteen | **Rejected** — scope is the skill |
| Volunteer coordination | Programme management | Depends — scale and formality decide |

The rejected rows share a property: the gap is not vocabulary, it is substance. Docker teaches you containers; Kubernetes is about scheduling, scaling and failure across a cluster. Claiming one for the other is not translation, it is a different claim — which is precisely [the line between framing and fabrication](/blog/tailoring-without-lying).

## How to Write It

The structure that works, in all three cases:

> [What you actually used or did] — [the scope] — [the outcome].

The capability becomes evident from the description. You do not need to assert it.

### Different tool

> **Weak:** Experience with BI and data visualisation tools.
>
> **Weak in the other direction:** Tableau, Power BI, Looker. *(listing tools you have not used)*
>
> **Strong:** Built and maintained the weekly executive dashboard in Power BI — 14 reports, automated refresh from three source systems, used by the leadership team for quarterly planning.

The reader needing Tableau can evaluate that directly. They know what building a refresh pipeline involves, and they know it is the same work in their tool.

### Different label

> **Weak:** Responsible for relationships with external suppliers.
>
> **Strong:** Owned vendor management across four agency partners — contract renewals, quarterly performance reviews, and escalation handling for a £400k annual spend.

Their word, your work, evidence attached. This is a rename, not a claim, and it is the most underused move in tailoring.

### Different context

> **Weak:** Managed budgets in a healthcare setting.
>
> **Strong:** Forecast and managed a £2.1m departmental budget across 40 cost centres, including monthly variance reporting to the finance director and a reforecast cycle that cut overspend from 7% to under 2%.

Nothing about healthcare appears. The capability, the scale and the mechanism are all legible to a manufacturing reader, and the numbers do the bridging work.

## Where Adjacency Belongs

Placement decides whether an adjacent skill reads as honest or evasive.

| Location | Use adjacency? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bullet under a role | Yes — best place | Room to describe the actual work |
| Summary | Yes, once | Sets the frame before details |
| Skills list | Only your real tool | A skills entry reads as a direct claim |
| Cover letter | Yes, for context gaps | Space to build the bridge explicitly |
| Job title line | Never | Titles must be factual |

The skills list is where people go wrong. A bullet has room for nuance; a skills line is a bare assertion, and adding "Tableau" there when you have used Power BI is a claim you will be asked about. [Where keywords sit](/blog/where-to-place-keywords-resume) changes what they are taken to mean.

## A Worked Example: Two Degrees Out

A candidate moving from agency account management into a client-side product marketing role. The posting asks for product positioning, go-to-market planning, and cross-functional leadership. Her title has never included any of those words.

What she actually did: ran campaigns for four B2B software clients, wrote the messaging for two product launches, and coordinated between client stakeholders, creative teams and media buyers.

| Their requirement | Her actual experience | Adjacency kind | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product positioning | Wrote launch messaging for two SaaS products | Different label | Strong |
| Go-to-market planning | Planned and ran the launch campaigns | Different label | Strong |
| Cross-functional leadership | Coordinated client, creative and media teams | Different context | Moderate |
| Product analytics | Campaign performance reporting | Different context | Weak — do not push |

Her rewritten bullet:

> Led go-to-market execution for two B2B SaaS launches — developed the positioning and messaging, built the campaign plan, and coordinated delivery across client stakeholders, creative and media teams to hit both launch dates.

Every element is true. Not one word claims a title she did not hold. A product marketing reader sees someone who has done three of the four things they need. She left product analytics alone because pushing it would have weakened the three that were strong.

## When Adjacency Stops Working

**Beyond two degrees.** Power BI to Tableau is one degree. Excel to Tableau is two and getting thin. Excel to a data engineering role is a career change, and needs [a career-change structure](/blog/resume-matching-for-career-changers) rather than a translation.

**When scope is the skill.** Managing two people does not become managing twenty through better wording. Some requirements are quantitative and no framing changes the number.

**When the tool is the point.** If a posting names a specific platform in the job title, the employer is hiring for that platform. Adjacency helps at the margin and will not carry the application.

**When you are stacking several.** One adjacent claim is credible. Four in a row reads as someone who has done none of it. Pick your strongest and let the rest go — the same discipline as [knowing when to stop optimising](/blog/good-ats-score-when-to-stop).

**Regulated or credentialed work.** A licence is binary. No amount of comparable experience substitutes for it.

## Common Mistakes

### Leading with their word instead of yours

"Salesforce administration (HubSpot)" inverts the honest order and reads as a workaround. Put your actual tool first and let the capability follow.

### Translating into jargon nobody uses

Over-translating produces "stakeholder-facing delivery orchestration" from "kept the client informed." Use their vocabulary where it is genuinely theirs, not invented corporate register.

### Claiming adjacency in the skills line

Bears repeating because it is the most common version. Bullets describe; skills lines assert. Keep adjacency in the describing layer.

### Forgetting to translate at all

The opposite failure and nearly as common. If your bullet says "liaised with department heads" and the posting says "stakeholder management," a keyword matcher sees nothing and a busy human may not translate for you.

### Using adjacency to cover a real gap

Adjacency surfaces work you actually did. It does not manufacture work you did not. When the skill is genuinely absent, [there are four honest moves](/blog/missing-required-skill-response) and this is only one of them.

## How Readers Actually Evaluate Adjacency

Understanding what happens on the other side explains why the bullet-level approach works and the skills-line approach does not.

### The recruiter pass

Thirty seconds, scanning for a shape rather than reading. At this stage adjacency mostly fails or succeeds on vocabulary — if their word appears somewhere legible, you survive; if it does not, you may not. This is the only stage where keyword placement genuinely decides something, which is why the translation has to be real rather than implied.

### The hiring manager pass

Two to four minutes, reading bullets properly. This is where adjacency actually pays. A manager who needs Snowflake and reads a detailed Redshift bullet does the comparison instantly and accepts it, because they know the work. Nothing you could have written in a skills line would have been more persuasive than the description.

### The screening call

Ten to twenty minutes, testing whether the document was accurate. Adjacency stated honestly survives this easily — "I used Redshift, here's how the modelling worked" is a good answer. Adjacency overclaimed dies here, and takes the rest of your credibility with it.

### The technical stage

Where scope claims get tested. If you described managing two people and the role needs fifteen, this is where it surfaces. No amount of framing changes it, which is why scope adjacency is the one type worth being conservative about.

The pattern across all four: the earlier stages reward vocabulary and the later stages reward accuracy. Since you have to pass all of them, the only strategy that works end to end is using their words for work you genuinely did. It is the same reason [a good score can still produce no interviews](/blog/high-ats-score-no-interviews) — optimising for the first gate alone just relocates the failure.

## What This Looks Like at Different Levels

| Level | Strongest adjacency type | What readers accept |
|---|---|---|
| Graduate | Context — coursework, projects, part-time work | Most things, if the capability is evidenced |
| Early career | Tool — one substitution is routine | Tool swaps within a category |
| Mid-level | Label — translating your actual work | Vocabulary differences, not capability gaps |
| Senior | Context — domain and industry moves | Scope and judgement transfer; tools matter less |
| Career changer | All three, with an explicit bridge | Depends entirely on how the bridge is argued |

Senior readers are the most receptive to context adjacency and the least interested in tool adjacency, because at that level the tools are assumed and the judgement is what is being bought. Graduates get the most latitude of all, since nobody expects an exact match from someone with two years of history.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if two tools are genuinely comparable?

Ask whether a practitioner would need retraining or just reorientation. Power BI to Tableau is reorientation — a week. Docker to Kubernetes is retraining — months. If you are unsure, you are probably looking at retraining.

### Should I mention the substitution explicitly?

No. Write your actual experience clearly and let the reader make the comparison. Explicitly flagging "similar to Tableau" draws attention to the gap rather than to the work.

### Does an ATS give credit for adjacent skills?

Older keyword matchers, no — they match strings. Newer systems using semantic matching often do. Since you cannot tell which is reading you, write for the human and use their vocabulary where it is honestly yours. [Exact match versus synonym](/blog/ats-exact-match-vs-synonym) covers the mechanics.

### What if my whole resume is adjacent?

Then this is a career change, not a tailoring problem. The document needs a different structure — a summary that states the transition explicitly, and evidence organised by capability rather than chronology.

### Can I use adjacency for years of experience?

Partially. Related-field experience often counts toward a years requirement in a reader's mind even when it does not match exactly. [When a JD asks for years you do not have](/blog/jd-asks-for-years-you-dont-have) goes into where the thresholds actually bite.

### How many adjacent claims can one resume carry?

Two, comfortably. Three starts to strain. Beyond that the reader stops seeing a match and starts seeing a stretch.

### Does industry adjacency matter as much as tool adjacency?

Less than people fear, in most functions. Finance, legal and regulated healthcare care about domain; marketing, engineering and operations care much less.

### Should the cover letter repeat the adjacency?

Only for context gaps, where the bridge genuinely needs explaining. Tool and label adjacency should be self-evident from the bullets.

### What if the recruiter filters on the exact tool?

Then you may not pass that filter, and that is a real cost of honesty. It is still the right trade — passing a filter into an interview you cannot survive is worse than not passing it.

### Is adjacency different for contract work?

Yes, and stricter. Contract hiring assumes no ramp-up, so tool adjacency that a permanent employer would accept is often rejected.

### Should I reorder bullets so the adjacent one comes first?

Usually yes. [Reordering by relevance](/blog/reordering-bullets-by-relevance) costs nothing and puts the transferable work where the recruiter's thirty seconds actually land.

### How do I describe adjacent work with no numbers attached?

Scope and mechanism substitute for metrics. How many systems, how many stakeholders, how often, what broke and what you did. [Quantifying without real numbers](/blog/how-to-quantify-achievements-without-numbers) covers the alternatives.

### Does a non-standard job title create the same problem?

It creates the mirror problem — your title obscures work you genuinely did. [Fixing a non-standard internal title](/blog/nonstandard-job-title-fix) is the same translation exercise applied to the title line.

### How much tailoring does an adjacency-heavy application need?

More than a direct match and less than a rewrite. The bullets carrying adjacent work need real attention; everything else can stay. [How much tailoring is too much](/blog/how-much-tailoring-is-too-much) draws the boundary.

## Describe the Work, Not the Keyword

Almost every adjacency problem resolves the same way: stop trying to match the word and start describing what you actually did, with enough specificity that the match becomes obvious.

A reader who needs Tableau and sees a detailed account of building a refresh pipeline in Power BI does the translation themselves, and trusts it more than they would have trusted the word.
