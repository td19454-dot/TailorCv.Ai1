## Key Takeaways

- Negotiation is more constrained in Japan than in the US, particularly at traditional companies with fixed grade-based pay tables.
- Foreign-owned companies and modern startups negotiate much like international employers; traditional firms often genuinely cannot move.
- Think in **nenshu** (annual income) rather than monthly salary, because bonus is frequently a large share of the total.
- Recruitment agents are the normal channel for salary discussion and will negotiate on your behalf.
- A single evidence-based request lands well; repeated pushing reads as a poor fit.

Japanese business culture is often described as one where negotiating pay is inappropriate. That is an overstatement, but it points at something real: the register is different, the timing is later, and at some employers the structure genuinely does not bend.

The practical answer depends almost entirely on which kind of company you are joining. A foreign-owned firm in Tokyo will negotiate much as its head office would. A traditional Japanese company with a grade-based pay table may have no discretion at all, and pushing will damage the relationship without changing the number.

This guide covers how to tell which you are dealing with, how to research, and how to ask.

## Understand Nenshu First

Japanese compensation is discussed as **年収 (nenshu)** - total annual income - rather than monthly salary, and this catches foreign applicants out badly.

A typical structure:

- **Monthly base salary**, paid twelve times
- **Bonus**, often paid twice a year in summer and winter, frequently expressed as a number of months' salary
- **Allowances** - commuting, housing, family, or overtime allowances are common
- **Overtime pay**, which for non-management roles is generally regulated and can be significant

**Why this matters:** a monthly figure can understate annual income substantially. Two offers with identical monthly salaries can differ meaningfully once bonus months are counted.

**Always ask:**

- How many months of bonus are typical?
- Is the bonus guaranteed, performance-based, or company-performance-based?
- What has it actually paid in recent years?
- Which allowances apply, and are they included in the quoted nenshu?

That last question matters because quoted nenshu sometimes includes assumed overtime or allowances, which makes the headline figure look better than the guaranteed portion.

## Which Kind of Employer Are You Dealing With?

### Foreign-owned companies

Negotiation is normal and expected, much as in the parent company's home market. Ranges are wider, individual assessment matters more, and a well-reasoned counter is unremarkable.

### Modern Japanese startups and tech companies

Generally negotiable, particularly for skills in short supply. Equity may form part of the package. More flexible than traditional firms but usually less so than foreign-owned ones.

### Traditional Japanese companies

Frequently operate grade-based pay tables where your salary follows from your grade, which follows from experience and role. There may be genuinely no discretion for the hiring manager.

At these employers, the productive question is not "can you pay more" but "which grade am I being placed at" - because the grade determines the number, and grade placement is sometimes negotiable where salary is not.

### SES and staffing firms

Compensation is constrained by the client billing arrangement. Understand the structure before accepting - see [IT jobs in Japan for foreigners](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/it-jobs-in-japan-foreigner).

## Research the Range

**Recruitment agents are the best source.** Bilingual agencies working the foreign professional market know actual ranges by role, level, and company type, and they will tell you. Ask directly.

**Job postings frequently state a nenshu range**, which is more common in Japan than in some markets. Read twenty for your role and level.

**Salary surveys** published by recruitment firms give sector benchmarks.

**Account for company type.** The same role can differ substantially between a foreign-owned firm, a domestic product company, and an SI firm.

**Account for location**, though the spread is narrower than in the US - Tokyo pays more, but not by the margin that separates San Francisco from the American average.

## The Expectations Question

You will be asked your **希望年収** - desired annual income - usually by the recruiter or agent rather than the employer directly.

**If an agent is involved**, this is easier than it sounds. Tell them your target honestly and let them handle the conversation. Agents negotiate on your behalf as a matter of routine, they know what the client will accept, and using them insulates you from appearing pushy. This is one of the genuine advantages of the Japanese hiring process.

**If asked directly**, give a range with your target near the bottom, and frame it around market rate rather than personal need:

> Based on my experience and what I understand of the market for this level, I am hoping for around ¥X million. I would like to understand the full package including bonus before finalising.

**On current salary:** you will usually be asked, and unlike in parts of the US there is generally no prohibition on the question. Answer honestly - it will typically be verified against documentation at offer stage in Japan, which is a real difference from other markets and makes inflation a serious risk.

## Making the Ask

**Timing:** at the offer stage, not during earlier interviews. Raising money early reads as the wrong priority, more strongly than in Western markets.

**Register:** indirect, evidence-led, and unfailingly polite. Frame it as seeking alignment rather than pushing for more.

> Thank you very much for the offer - I am very interested in the role. Based on my experience with [specific skill] and the market range I have seen for this level, I was hoping for closer to ¥X million. Would there be any flexibility?

**Give one clear reason** grounded in what you bring or in market rate. Personal circumstances are not arguments here.

**Ask once.** One well-reasoned request is entirely acceptable. Repeated pushing reads as a poor cultural fit and can genuinely damage the relationship you are about to begin.

**Accept a firm no gracefully.** If the answer is that the grade determines it, that is often literally true. Move to the other elements or accept.

## What Else Is Negotiable

Where base salary is fixed, other things sometimes are not.

**Grade or title placement.** The most valuable thing to discuss at traditional companies, because grade determines pay for your whole tenure.

**Start date.**

**Relocation support**, if you are moving to Japan - flights, initial accommodation, and shipping. Foreign employers offer this more readily.

**Housing allowance**, which is a common component in Japan and sometimes adjustable.

**Visa and immigration support.** Confirm who handles and pays for the Certificate of Eligibility process - see [the Japan work visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-work-visa-guide).

**Japanese language study support.** Frequently available and genuinely valuable, since language ability widens your options and adds points toward Highly Skilled Professional status.

**Remote or flexible working arrangements**, more available at modern companies.

**Paid leave.** Statutory minimums apply, and some employers offer more.

## Common Mistakes

**Comparing monthly salary instead of nenshu.** Bonus is often several months of pay, so two offers with identical monthly figures can differ substantially in annual terms.

**Negotiating too early.** Raising compensation during technical or early interviews reads as the wrong priority more strongly in Japan than in Western markets, and it colours the rest of the process.

**Pushing repeatedly.** One reasoned request is acceptable and expected; continuing after a clear answer reads as a cultural mismatch and can damage a relationship you have not yet started.

**Inflating your current salary.** It is commonly verified against documentation at offer stage in Japan, which makes this far riskier here than in markets where the question is simply asked and taken on trust.

**Not asking about bonus specifics.** Whether it is guaranteed or performance-based, and what it has actually paid recently, changes the value of the offer considerably.

**Ignoring assumed overtime in the quoted figure.** Some nenshu quotes include expected overtime or allowances, which makes the guaranteed portion smaller than the headline suggests.

**Not using your recruitment agent.** They negotiate as a matter of routine, know what the client will accept, and insulate you from appearing pushy - declining to use them is discarding a real advantage.

**Focusing on salary when grade is the real lever.** At traditional companies the grade determines the number, so discussing placement is productive where discussing salary is not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you negotiate salary in Japan?

Yes, though more narrowly than in the US and with a different register. Foreign-owned companies and modern startups negotiate readily; traditional firms with grade-based pay tables may have genuinely no discretion.

### What is nenshu?

Total annual income, including base salary, bonus, and often allowances. Japanese compensation is discussed in these terms rather than as a monthly figure, and the bonus portion can be substantial.

### Will they ask my current salary?

Usually yes, and there is generally no prohibition on the question. Answer honestly - it is commonly verified against documentation at offer stage, which makes inflating it a serious risk.

### How many months of bonus are normal?

It varies widely by company and by year, and some bonuses are performance-linked rather than guaranteed. Always ask what has actually been paid recently rather than accepting a stated maximum.

### Should I negotiate through a recruitment agent?

Yes, if one is involved. Agents negotiate routinely, know the client's range, and handle the conversation on your behalf - which is both more effective and culturally smoother than doing it directly.

### Is it rude to negotiate?

Not if done once, politely, at offer stage, with a reason grounded in market rate or your experience. What reads badly is raising it early, pushing repeatedly, or framing it around personal need.

### What if the salary is fixed by grade?

Discuss the grade rather than the salary, since grade determines pay and its placement is sometimes negotiable. Then look at housing allowance, relocation support, language study support, and start date.

Work out the nenshu before you compare anything. Then identify what kind of employer you are dealing with, because it determines whether negotiation is possible at all. Foreign-owned companies and startups negotiate readily; at traditional firms with grade-based pay, ask about grade placement rather than salary, since that is where the actual flexibility sits.

Finally, ask once, late, and through your agent if you have one. Make a single evidence-based request at offer stage framed around market rate rather than personal need, accept a firm no gracefully, and move to housing allowance, relocation support, language study support, and start date.
