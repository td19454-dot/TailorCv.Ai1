## Key Takeaways

- Evaluators score content, clarity, and group behaviour - not who spoke most or loudest.
- Two or three substantive contributions beat continuous talking, and dominating actively loses marks.
- The first sixty seconds are the hardest to enter; having a prepared opening structure solves most of it.
- Bringing a silent participant into the discussion scores highly and almost nobody does it.
- Complete silence is an automatic fail, so a modest contribution always beats none.

Group discussions are the round students find most unpredictable, because the criteria are invisible. You sit with ten strangers, everyone talks over everyone, and afterwards some people are selected for reasons nobody explains.

The criteria are not actually mysterious. Evaluators are watching for a specific set of behaviours, most of which have little to do with talking the most. Once you know what is being scored, the round becomes preparable - and a large share of your competition will not have prepared at all.

This guide covers what is scored, how to enter, what to say, and the behaviours that quietly lose marks.

## What Evaluators Are Actually Scoring

Typically a panel sits outside the circle with a scoring sheet. The dimensions are broadly consistent across companies.

**Content quality.** Do your points add something? A well-reasoned argument with a concrete example scores far above a restatement of what someone already said.

**Clarity of expression.** Can you make a point cleanly, in a sentence or two? Rambling costs marks even when the underlying idea is good.

**Listening.** Do you respond to what others said, or deliver a prepared monologue regardless of context? Building on someone's point - "picking up on what Rahul said about cost, the counterexample is…" - scores well and demonstrates you were listening.

**Group behaviour.** Do you help the discussion function? Bringing in a quiet participant, summarising when the group is stuck, or steering back on topic all score highly. Interrupting, dismissing others, and dominating all cost.

**Leadership without domination.** Initiating and structuring scores well. Controlling and talking over people does not.

**Body language.** Posture, eye contact around the group rather than only at the panel, and not fidgeting.

The critical point: **quantity is not the metric.** Students believe speaking most wins. Evaluators are looking for substance and group contribution, and a candidate who spoke four times with substance regularly outscores one who spoke fifteen times with noise.

## The Format

Typically eight to twelve participants seated in a circle, a topic announced, sometimes a minute or two of preparation time, then ten to twenty minutes of discussion. Some companies ask for a concluding summary.

**Topic categories:**

**Abstract topics** - "Is failure necessary for success", "Colour blue". Testing creativity and structured thinking rather than knowledge.

**Current affairs** - social, economic, or technology issues. Testing awareness and reasoning.

**Business or case-based** - a scenario with a decision to make. More common at consulting and product companies.

**Opinion-based** - "Should social media be regulated". Testing balanced argument.

**Preparation that helps:** read a newspaper daily for a month before placement season. Not to memorise facts, but so that no topic leaves you blank.

## Entering the Discussion

The hardest practical problem is the first sixty seconds, when ten people are trying to speak at once.

### Speaking first

**The advantage:** you set the direction, and you are guaranteed to be heard.

**The risk:** if you have nothing substantive, you have used your strongest moment badly.

**Do it if** you have a clear framing to offer. A good opening defines the scope: "Before we discuss whether social media should be regulated, it's worth separating two things - content moderation and data privacy. They need different answers."

That is a strong opening because it structures the discussion for everyone. The group frequently follows your framing for the rest of the round, and evaluators notice who provided it.

### If you cannot speak first

**Wait for a natural pause,** which comes after the initial rush of two or three speakers.

**Enter by building on someone.** "I'd like to add to what Priya said…" is easier to insert than a cold start, and it demonstrates listening.

**Use a slightly raised voice and start with a short phrase** to claim the floor, then continue at normal volume.

**Do not wait for perfect silence.** It will not arrive. Aim to enter within the first three minutes, because a candidate who has not spoken by the halfway point is in real difficulty.

## What to Say

**Structure each contribution.** Point, reason, example. Twenty to forty seconds. That is enough for a complete thought and short enough that you are not cut off mid-sentence.

**Use examples.** Concrete beats abstract every time. A specific case, a number, or a real situation lifts an ordinary point considerably.

**Take a position, then acknowledge the other side.** "I think regulation is necessary, though the obvious risk is that it becomes a tool for suppressing criticism" reads as balanced thinking rather than fence-sitting.

**Do not repeat.** If your point has been made, either add a new dimension or wait. Restating what someone said five minutes ago is visible and it costs marks.

**Summarise if the group stalls.** "We seem to agree on X but we're stuck on Y - maybe we should look at Y from the cost side" is a high-scoring intervention that few candidates make.

**Bring in a silent participant.** "Ananya, you had a point earlier?" This scores well on group behaviour and almost nobody does it. It costs you nothing and demonstrates exactly the awareness evaluators are looking for.

## What Loses Marks

**Dominating.** Talking continuously, cutting others off, refusing to yield. This is the most common mistake and it is scored negatively, not positively.

**Silence.** Usually an automatic fail regardless of how good your notes were. If you are struggling, make one short, safe contribution early just to be on the board.

**Aggression.** Raising your voice, dismissing someone's point, or arguing personally rather than substantively.

**Repetition.** Restating without adding.

**Speaking without content.** Long sentences that say nothing are more visible than students realise.

**Only addressing the panel.** Look at the group. It is a discussion, not a presentation.

**Fabricating facts.** Invented statistics get challenged and the damage is worse than not having the number.

**Rigidity.** Refusing to acknowledge any counterpoint reads as inability to reason rather than conviction.

**Getting personal.** Attacking a participant rather than their argument ends your candidacy.

## If You Are Not a Confident Speaker

This round is genuinely harder for some students, and pretending otherwise does not help. Practical adjustments:

**Prepare a structural opening you can use on any topic.** Defining scope or separating two aspects of the question works almost universally, and having it ready removes the blank-mind problem.

**Aim for three good contributions**, not for volume. That is enough to be scored properly.

**Practise in groups before the season.** With classmates, on random topics, timed. The discomfort reduces quickly with repetition and almost nothing else does.

**Work on the underlying skill separately** - see [campus placement communication skills](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/campus-placement-communication-skills).

**Speak early, even briefly.** The longer you wait, the harder entry becomes psychologically.

**Remember multiple candidates are selected.** It is not one winner from twelve. Being clearly competent is usually enough.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should I speak in a group discussion?

Three to five substantive contributions in a fifteen-minute discussion is generally sufficient. Evaluators score content and group behaviour rather than airtime, and dominating is penalised.

### Is it better to speak first?

Only if you have a clear framing to offer. Speaking first guarantees you are heard and lets you shape the discussion, but a weak opening wastes your best moment. If you are unsure, enter second or third by building on someone else's point.

### What if I cannot get a chance to speak?

Enter by building on a previous point rather than starting cold, use a slightly raised voice on your opening words, and aim to speak within the first three minutes. Not speaking at all is usually an automatic elimination.

### Should I agree or disagree with the majority?

Take the position you can argue best with examples. A well-reasoned minority view scores better than weakly agreeing with the room, provided you acknowledge the other side rather than dismissing it.

### What topics come up most often?

Current affairs, social issues, technology and its effects, and abstract topics. Reading a newspaper daily for a month before the season is the most efficient preparation.

### Do I need to conclude the discussion?

If asked, a good summary scores well - state the main positions, note where the group agreed, and close. If not asked, offering a brief summary when the discussion stalls is still a strong contribution.

### What should I do if someone is being aggressive?

Stay calm and do not match it. Addressing the argument politely while ignoring the tone scores well, and the aggressive participant is usually damaging their own evaluation without help from you.
