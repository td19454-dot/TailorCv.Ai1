## Key Takeaways

- Most rescinded graduate offers are business decisions — hiring freezes and reorganisations — not judgements about you.
- Ask immediately whether it is a deferral, a redeployment or a cancellation. The three have very different responses.
- Ask what support is available. Deferral payments, extended start dates and referrals are sometimes offered and rarely volunteered.
- International students face an authorisation problem as well as an income problem, and that clock needs addressing first.
- Restarting quickly matters more than restarting perfectly.

## Why it happens

Offers are pulled far more often for reasons that have nothing to do with the candidate:

- **Hiring freezes.** Conditions change between the offer in October and the start in July. Graduate classes are frequently the first thing cut, because nobody has started yet.
- **Reorganisation.** The team you were hired into no longer exists in that form.
- **Background or reference issues.** Discrepancies in dates, education or previous employment. Rarer, but this category is on you.
- **Failure to graduate or meet a stated condition.** Most graduate offers are conditional on completing the degree.
- **The requisition never existed properly.** Budget approval falls through after a verbal offer.

The first two account for most rescinded new-grad offers, and neither is a reflection on your ability.

## The first conversation

Get answers to specific questions rather than accepting the message as final. Ask by phone if you can.

**Is this a cancellation, a deferral, or a redeployment?** These are genuinely different. Some employers delay start dates rather than cancelling; some can move you to another team with headcount. Neither is always offered unprompted.

**Is any support available?** Some companies offer a payment to graduates whose offers are pulled, particularly where the person turned down alternatives. Some offer to keep you in the pipeline for the next cycle. Almost none of this is volunteered — you have to ask.

**Will you refer me?** A recruiter who just pulled your offer often will make introductions, and they know who else is hiring. This is the most valuable thing they can give you and it costs them nothing.

**Can I have it in writing?** For your own records, and useful when explaining the situation to other employers later.

Stay professional in this conversation, however unfair it feels. The person delivering the news usually did not make the decision, and they are the one who can offer a referral.

## If you signed something

Read what you signed. Most US employment is at-will, and offer letters typically say so explicitly, which means an offer can generally be withdrawn before you start.

Where you may have a claim is narrower: if you took specific, costly actions in reliance on the offer — resigned a job, relocated, declined a written alternative — some jurisdictions recognise that. This varies by state and is fact-specific.

If you incurred real costs, it is worth asking the company to cover them. Relocation expenses and non-refundable deposits are sometimes reimbursed simply because you asked. If the sums are large, a short consultation with an employment lawyer in the relevant state is proportionate.

## For international students

There is a second problem running alongside the income one, and it is more urgent.

If your work authorisation depends on employment, a rescinded offer can start or accelerate a clock. On post-completion OPT, the [90-day unemployment allowance](/blog/usa-opt-cpt-explained-students) accrues whether or not you had an offer in hand. If sponsorship was involved, a withdrawn petition has its own consequences.

Do two things immediately:

**Speak to your DSO.** Before anything else. They deal with this situation regularly and can tell you exactly where you stand.

**Recalculate your dates.** How many days you have, what happens at the end, and whether a shorter-term or part-time role would preserve status while you search.

This is also the moment where employers who are [E-Verify enrolled](/blog/usa-stem-opt-employer-requirements) and practised at hiring international graduates matter more, because you have less time to spend on employers who will not work out.

## Restarting the search

**Start within days, not weeks.** The instinct is to process it first. The market does not wait, and the [autumn cycle](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline) may already have closed, which changes where the roles are.

**Go where hiring is on demand.** If structured programmes have filled, mid-sized firms, regional companies and startups hire year-round. Off-cycle roles appear constantly at large employers as people decline and leave.

**Use your network first.** People who interviewed you elsewhere, alumni, former managers, and the recruiter who pulled the offer. A [warm introduction](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students) is worth far more than a cold application when you are compressing months into weeks.

**Tell your university.** Career centres sometimes have employer relationships specifically for this, and some maintain lists of employers with late openings.

**Check the resume before firing it out.** If it has been sitting since last autumn it needs updating, and it needs to [survive parsing](/ats-analysis) at employers with harsher screens than the one that hired you.

## Explaining it in interviews

Say it plainly and briefly:

> "I had an offer from X, and they froze graduate hiring in March. I started looking again straight away."

That is a complete answer. It is a common situation, interviewers recognise it, and it needs no defensiveness. What reads badly is bitterness or a long explanation — both invite a follow-up you do not want.

If the rescission was because of a background or reference discrepancy, that is different and needs honest handling, because it will surface again in the next check.

## Common Mistakes

- **Accepting the first message as final.** Deferral and redeployment are sometimes available if asked for.
- **Not asking for a referral.** The cheapest and most valuable thing they can offer.
- **Burning the relationship.** The recruiter usually did not decide, and they know who is hiring.
- **Waiting to restart.** Weeks of processing cost you roles that were open the day it happened.
- **International students not contacting their DSO immediately.** The authorisation clock is more urgent than the job search.
- **Over-explaining in later interviews.** One sentence is enough; more invites scrutiny.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a company legally rescind an offer?

Generally yes in at-will employment, and offer letters usually say so. Narrow exceptions exist where you took costly action in reliance on the offer, and that varies by state.

### Should I ask for compensation?

Yes, if you incurred real costs — relocation, deposits, a declined alternative. Companies sometimes cover these, and almost never offer without being asked.

### Will this hurt my next application?

No. Hiring freezes are common and well understood. Explained in one sentence, it is a non-event to most interviewers.

### Can I ask to be reconsidered later?

Yes, and it is worth asking to stay in the pipeline for the next cycle. Some employers keep a list.

### What if I already declined other offers?

Contact them. If the role has not been filled, some will reopen the conversation — an awkward call that occasionally works, and costs only pride.

### How fast should I restart?

Within days. The market position changes with the calendar, and momentum matters more than a polished restart.
