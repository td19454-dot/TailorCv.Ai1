- Foreign professionals frequently enter at a grade below their experience, and grade determines pay more than negotiation does.

The Japanese seniority system — *nenko joretsu* — is one of those institutions frequently declared dead and still visibly operating. Companies have introduced performance elements, some have abolished automatic progression entirely, and yet years of service continue to influence pay and promotion at a great many domestic employers.

For a mid-career foreign hire this has a specific consequence: you may be placed at a grade that reflects your years at the company rather than your years in the profession.

This guide covers how it works, how to read it, and what to ask.

## What nenko actually is

**Pay and promotion linked to length of service.** In the traditional model, an employee joins as a new graduate, progresses through defined grades on a broadly predictable schedule, and reaches senior positions after decades.

**The logic behind it:** companies invested heavily in training generalists over many years, rotating them through functions. Tying reward to tenure protected that investment and discouraged departure.

**It came bundled with other norms** — lifetime employment, extensive internal training, rotation across departments, and promotion from within.

**Why it is weakening:** an ageing workforce made the cost of automatic progression unsustainable, global competition pushed companies toward performance measurement, and the mid-career market matured — see [the Japan job change tenshoku guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-job-change-tenshoku-guide).

## What it means for you

**Grade determines pay.** At most domestic companies, your compensation follows from your assigned grade, which follows from a combination of role, experience and tenure. Individual salary negotiation has far less room than in Western markets — see [the Japan salary negotiation guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-salary-negotiation-guide).

**Mid-career hires are placed into that structure.** Ten years of experience elsewhere does not necessarily map to the grade someone with ten years at that company holds. Companies vary widely in how generously they credit external experience.

**Which makes grade placement the real negotiation.** Asking "can you pay more" frequently gets a truthful no. Asking "what grade am I being placed at, and what would justify a higher one" addresses the mechanism that actually sets your pay — and it affects every subsequent year, not just the first.

**Progression may be slower** than you are used to, particularly in the first years.

**Bonus is grade-linked too**, and bonus is a substantial share of Japanese annual income, so grade compounds through the whole package.

## Reading a company's position

Ask directly. These are reasonable questions and the answers are revealing.

- **How are mid-career hires placed in the grade structure?**
- **What determines promotion — is there a defined performance component?**
- **How long do people typically spend at this grade?**
- **Are there foreign employees at senior grades?** A company with none may have a ceiling nobody will describe to you.
- **How is bonus calculated, and how many months has it paid recently?**

**Signals a company has moved away from strict nenko:** published performance criteria, mid-career hires in senior roles, foreign managers, a role-based rather than person-based grade system.

**Signals it has not:** everyone senior joined as a new graduate, promotion timelines quoted in years rather than achievements, vagueness about how external experience is credited.

## Where nenko does not apply

**Foreign-owned companies (gaishikei)** largely operate on their parent company's model — performance-based, faster progression, more negotiable — see [gaishikei vs domestic](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-gaishikei-vs-domestic).

**Modern startups** typically have no seniority system at all — see [the Japan startup jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-startup-jobs-guide).

**Some large domestic companies** have genuinely reformed, particularly in technology and among firms competing internationally for talent.

**Specialist and technical tracks** at some companies sit outside the general management progression, with their own criteria.

## The upside worth acknowledging

Nenko is usually presented as pure disadvantage, which is incomplete.

**It comes with investment in you.** Companies operating this way frequently train extensively, rotate you through functions, and develop people over years rather than hiring for immediate output — see [the Japan new grad vs mid-career guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/japan-new-grad-vs-mid-career).

**It is predictable.** You can see the path, and progression does not depend on politics or a manager's discretion to the same degree.

**Job security is higher**, structurally and culturally.

**For a long career at one company**, the model eventually works in your favour rather than against it. The disadvantage falls on people who join mid-career and leave again.

## Common Mistakes

**Negotiating salary instead of grade.** Pay follows from grade at most domestic employers, so the salary conversation frequently has no room while the grade conversation does.

**Assuming external experience maps directly.** Companies vary widely in how generously they credit years earned elsewhere, and many credit them partially.

**Not asking how mid-career hires are placed.** It is a reasonable question and the answer tells you what your first five years will look like.

**Ignoring the bonus link.** Bonus is a large share of Japanese annual income and is grade-linked, so grade compounds through the whole package.

**Assuming nenko is gone because articles say so.** It is weakening at very different speeds across companies, and plenty still operate it substantially.

**Not checking whether foreign employees hold senior grades.** Their absence may indicate a ceiling that nobody will describe to you directly.

**Treating it as purely negative.** It comes with training investment, predictability and security that performance-based systems frequently do not.

**Applying the same expectations at gaishikei.** Foreign-owned firms and startups largely operate outside this system entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is nenko joretsu?

The seniority-based system linking pay and promotion partly to years of service, traditionally paired with lifetime employment and extensive internal training.

### Is it still used?

Yes, though weakening at very different rates between companies. Many domestic employers retain substantial seniority elements alongside performance components.

### How does it affect mid-career hires?

You are placed into a grade structure, and companies vary in how much external experience they credit. Progression may be slower than you expect in the early years.

### Can I negotiate salary at a Japanese company?

Less than in Western markets, because pay follows from grade. The productive conversation is about grade placement rather than salary itself.

### Does it apply at foreign companies?

Largely not. Gaishikei generally follow their parent company's performance-based model, as do most modern startups.

### How do I tell whether a company still operates it?

Ask how mid-career hires are placed, whether promotion has defined performance criteria, and whether foreign employees hold senior grades. The answers are revealing.

### Is there any advantage to it?

Yes — extensive training investment, predictable progression, and higher job security. The disadvantage concentrates on people who join mid-career.

## Make This Practical

Negotiate the grade, not the salary. At most Japanese domestic employers pay follows from grade placement, so asking what grade you are being placed at and what would justify a higher one addresses the mechanism that sets your compensation for years — while a direct salary request frequently meets a genuine no.

Then read the company before you join. Ask how mid-career hires are placed, whether promotion has defined performance criteria, how long people spend at each grade, and whether foreign employees hold senior positions. That last answer reveals ceilings nobody will state outright.

Finally, match your expectations to the segment. Gaishikei and startups largely operate outside this system with faster, performance-based progression; established domestic firms offer slower advancement alongside genuine training investment and security. Both are legitimate — just know which one you are joining.
