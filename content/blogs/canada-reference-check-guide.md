## Key Takeaways

- Canadian reference checks are usually more conversational than the factual-only approach common in the UK.
- Offers are conditional until references clear, so do not resign before written confirmation.
- Overseas referees create real friction — time zones, language, and companies that no longer exist.
- A Canadian reference, even from volunteering or a contract, is worth disproportionately more than a foreign one.
- Ask referees first, brief them on the role, and warn them a call is coming.

Reference checks in Canada are frequently more substantive than in markets where liability concerns have pushed employers toward confirming dates only. A Canadian hiring manager may actually ask what you were like to work with — which is an opportunity if your referees are prepared and a problem if they are not.

For newcomers there is an additional difficulty: your referees are in another country, possibly another language, and possibly at a company that has since been acquired or closed.

This guide covers what is asked, who to choose, and how to handle the international problem.

## What actually gets asked

Typically a short call with your former manager, covering:

- **Confirmation of role, dates and reporting line**
- **What you were responsible for**
- **Strengths, and areas for development**
- **How you worked with others**
- **Attendance and reliability**
- **Whether they would rehire you** — the question that carries the most weight

**Some employers use written forms** or third-party screening services instead, which are closer to a factual verification.

**Regulated sectors** may have additional requirements — see [the Canada regulated professions guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-regulated-professions-guide).

## Choosing referees

**Ask first, every time.** A referee who has been asked has time to prepare and will not be caught cold. Naming someone without asking is both discourteous and risky.

**Direct managers beat senior titles.** Someone who saw your work daily gives a far more useful reference than a director who barely knew you.

**Two or three is standard**, usually including your most recent manager.

**If you cannot use your current employer**, say so. "Available after an offer, as my current employer is not aware I am searching" is normal and universally understood.

**Brief them.** Send the job description and a short note on what the role involves. A referee who knows what is being assessed gives a much stronger reference than one answering blind.

**Warn them a call is coming**, with the company name and rough timing.

## The international reference problem

This is where newcomers lose time and offers.

**The practical difficulties:**

**Time zones.** A Canadian hiring manager calling India or Nigeria has to plan around a twelve-hour difference, and many simply will not persist.

**Language.** A referee who is not comfortable in English or French may give a weaker impression than your work deserves.

**Companies that no longer exist.** Acquisitions, closures and restructures make verification hard.

**Cultural differences in how references are given.** Some markets are considerably more reserved, and understatement can read as lukewarm to a Canadian ear.

**What to do about it:**

**Warn your referees properly.** Tell them a Canadian employer may call, at what time of day, and what the role is. Ask them to reply to email if a call is difficult.

**Offer email as an alternative.** Many Canadian employers accept a written reference where a call is impractical.

**Provide context to the employer.** "My former manager is in Bangalore and is happy to speak — he is available between 8 and 10am your time" removes friction rather than leaving it to be discovered.

**Keep documentation.** Old offer letters, contracts, payslips and performance reviews help where a company has closed.

**Prioritise getting one Canadian reference.** This is the single most valuable thing you can do — from a contract role, a co-op placement, a bridging program, or professional volunteering. A Canadian referee removes every problem above at once, and it addresses the wider difficulty described in [the Canadian experience problem](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canadian-experience-problem).

## Timing

References are usually taken **after a conditional offer**, and frequently as the final step before it becomes unconditional.

**Do not resign until you have written confirmation** that all conditions are satisfied. A conditional offer is exactly that, and this is where people end up with nothing — the same discipline as [notice period negotiation](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/notice-period-negotiation-guide).

**Allow time.** International references take longer, and a delay is not a rejection.

## Common Mistakes

**Naming referees without asking.** A surprised referee gives a weaker reference, and it is avoidable.

**Not briefing referees on the role.** Someone answering blind gives a generic reference; someone who knows what is being assessed gives a useful one.

**Not warning overseas referees about time zones.** A Canadian manager calling at 3am gets voicemail, and many will not try twice.

**Choosing titles over familiarity.** A direct manager who saw your work daily is worth more than a senior name who did not.

**Resigning against a conditional offer.** References are frequently the last condition, and this is where people lose both roles.

**Not offering email as an alternative.** Many Canadian employers accept written references where a call is impractical across time zones.

**Not keeping old employment documents.** Offer letters, contracts and payslips resolve verification when a former employer no longer exists.

**Relying entirely on overseas references.** One Canadian reference — from contract work, co-op, or professional volunteering — removes most of these problems at once.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do Canadian employers ask referees?

Usually role, dates, responsibilities, strengths and development areas, how you worked with others, and whether they would rehire you. It is frequently more conversational than a factual-only check.

### Can I use overseas referees?

Yes, and many newcomers must. Warn them about timing, offer email as an alternative, and give the employer practical detail about when your referee is reachable.

### How many referees do I need?

Two or three, usually including your most recent manager.

### What if my former company no longer exists?

Keep old offer letters, contracts, payslips and reviews. Employers generally accept documentary evidence where verification is impossible.

### Can I refuse to give my current employer?

Yes. Explaining that your search is confidential and offering them after an offer is normal and well understood.

### When are references checked?

Usually after a conditional offer, frequently as the final condition before it becomes unconditional. Do not resign before written confirmation.

### How valuable is a Canadian reference?

Disproportionately. Even one from contract work, a co-op placement or professional volunteering removes the time zone, language and verification problems at once.

## Make This Practical

Prioritise obtaining one Canadian reference above almost everything else if you are a newcomer. A contract role, a co-op placement, a bridging program or professional volunteering all produce one, and a single Canadian referee eliminates the time zone, language and verification problems that overseas references create.

Then prepare the referees you do have. Ask before naming them, send the job description, tell them which company will call and roughly when, and give overseas referees the specific hours that work in Canadian business time.

Finally, protect the transition. Give the employer practical detail about reaching an international referee rather than leaving them to discover the difficulty, offer email as an alternative where a call is impractical, keep old employment documents for companies that no longer exist, and never resign until the offer is confirmed unconditionally in writing.
