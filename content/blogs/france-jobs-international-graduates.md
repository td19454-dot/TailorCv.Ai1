## Key Takeaways

- **CDI ve[rsu](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-job-offer-email)s CDD** is the central distinction in French employment — permanent versus fixed-term — and it affects everything from [housing](/blog/canada-major-city-job-markets) to borrowing.
- The **Talent Passport (Passeport Talent)** is a multi-year [residence permit](/blog/canada-work-permit-types-guide) covering qualified employees, researchers and founders, and it is far better than the standard route.
- **Grandes écoles carry disproportionate weight** in French hiring, and the school on your [CV](/blog/resume-optimization-guide) follows you for years.
- **French is required for most of the market**, though technology, research and international companies in Paris run substantially in English.
- **Stage and alternance** — internships and apprenticeship contracts — are the mainstream entry route, more so than direct graduate hiring.

## CDI, CDD and why it dominates every conversation

If you understand one thing about French employment, make it this.

**CDI (contrat à durée indéterminée)** is a permanent contract with no end date. It carries strong protection, a structured dismissal process, and — crucially — social legitimacy. Landlords want to see one. Banks want to see one before lending. It is the marker of professional stability in France in a way no Anglo-American equivalent quite matches.

**CDD (contrat à durée déterminée)** is fixed-term, used for defined needs, with legal limits on duration and renewal. It is normal, especially early on, but it is a different status.

**The practical consequence for a graduate:** a CDI offer is worth more than its salary suggests, and a CDD is not a failure but is a step toward one. When comparing offers, the contract type belongs in the comparison alongside the money.

**Période d'essai** — the probation period — is longer in France than in many countries and either side can end the contract easily during it. A CDI with a long trial period is still a CDI, but understand what the [first months](/blog/first-job-abroad-practical-checklist) mean.

## The Talent Passport

The single most useful thing for a qualified non-EU graduate to know about France.

**What it is.** The *Passeport Talent* is a multi-year residence permit, typically issued for up to four years, covering several categories including qualified employees with a master's-level degree, employees of innovative companies, researchers, and founders of [startups](/blog/japan-startup-jobs-guide).

**Why it is better than the standard route.** It is multi-year rather than annual, it does not require the labour market test that the ordinary salaried route can involve, and it grants an accompanying *passeport talent famille* permit to your family with full working rights.

**The qualified employee category** generally requires a master's-level qualification and a salary above a defined threshold tied to the French [reference](/blog/resume-references-guide) wage. Check the current threshold with the French administration rather than a secondary source.

**Recherche d'emploi ou création d'entreprise (RECE)** is the post-study permit — graduates of French institutions at master's level and above can obtain a period to look for work or start a business, and can then transition to a Talent Passport.

**The strategic read:** a French master's degree is a genuinely effective route into France, because it unlocks both the job-search permit and the qualification threshold for the Talent Passport.

## Grandes écoles, and why they matter

French hiring places unusual weight on educational pedigree, and it is worth understanding rather than resenting.

**The grandes écoles** — Polytechnique, Centrale, Mines, HEC, ESSEC, ESCP, Sciences Po and others — sit outside the university system and recruit through competitive entrance examinations. Their [alumni networks](/blog/international-student-networking-guide) are powerful and long-lasting.

**The school follows you.** Where an American CV foregrounds companies and an Australian one foregrounds outcomes, a French CV's education line does real work for a decade or more.

**If you did not attend one**, this is not a barrier — it is a reason to lean harder on demonstrable outcomes, international experience and specific technical capability, which are exactly the things that differentiate an international candidate anyway.

**International qualifications** are read with less automatic calibration, so state your institution's standing plainly where it helps, and describe the level of your qualification in terms a French reader recognises.

## Where the jobs are, beyond Paris

Paris dominates by volume and the regional cities are genuinely under-applied.

**Paris and Île-de-France** hold the corporate headquarters, [finance](/blog/resume-matching-insurance-jobs), [consulting](/blog/case-interview-preparation-guide-2026), media and the largest technology scene, centred on Station F and the wider startup ecosystem.

**Toulouse** is Europe's aerospace capital — Airbus, ATR, Thales and a deep supply chain. For an aerospace or systems engineer it is one of the best places in Europe.

**Grenoble** is a serious microelectronics and research centre, with CEA-Leti, STMicroelectronics and a substantial physics and materials cluster.

**Lyon** is pharmaceuticals, biotechnology and chemicals, with Sanofi and a strong life-sciences base.

**Sophia Antipolis**, near Nice, is a long-established technology park with a genuinely international workforce.

**Nantes, Bordeaux and Lille** all have growing technology scenes with dramatically lower housing costs than Paris.

**The trade is straightforward:** Paris has more roles and more applicants; the regional cities have fewer roles, far fewer applicants, and lives that cost half as much.

## Salary, contributions and what arrives

France's gross-to-net gap is large and worth understanding before you evaluate an offer.

**Salaries are quoted as gross annual (brut annuel)**, sometimes over 12 payments and sometimes 13 or more. Ask which.

**Social contributions are substantial**, funding health insurance, [pension](/blog/first-90-days-new-job), unemployment and family benefits. The gap between brut and net is much larger than in the UK or US.

**Income [tax](/blog/canada-first-job-taxes-and-benefits) is now withheld at source** (prélèvement à la source), which changed relatively recently — older advice describing an annual payment is out of date.

**Mutuelle** is supplementary health insurance, and employers are required to offer and part-fund it. It covers what the state system does not, and it is a genuine benefit worth checking.

**Tickets restaurant** — subsidised meal vouchers — are a widespread and surprisingly valuable benefit.

**RTT days.** The 35-hour week means employees working longer standard weeks accrue additional days off. Combined with statutory paid leave, French holiday entitlement is generous, and it is genuinely taken.

**Ask for the net figure.** A French employer will quote brut; asking what that means net monthly is a normal question and it is the number you will live on.

## Getting set up in France

French administration has a reputation and some of it is deserved. The sequence matters.

**Numéro de sécurité sociale.** Your social security number, which unlocks healthcare reimbursement and much else. Apply as soon as you start work; it takes time.

**Carte Vitale.** The physical health card that follows the number, used at every pharmacy and doctor.

**A French bank account** with a RIB — the account identifier used for salary, rent and direct debits. Almost nothing works without one.

**[Attestation](/blog/canada-credential-assessment-guide) d'hébergement or a lease** for proof of address, which several other steps require.

**Housing is the hard part in Paris.** Landlords typically want a French guarantor (*garant*), proof of income at several times the rent, and a CDI. Schemes like Visale exist to provide a state-backed guarantee for people without a French guarantor, and knowing about them changes the search substantially.

**CAF** provides housing assistance for which many residents including students and low earners are eligible — worth checking rather than assuming it is not for you.

**Titre de séjour.** If you need a residence permit, the process runs through the prefecture and appointments can be difficult to obtain. Start early and keep every piece of paper.

## Working culture in France

Worth calibrating, because the stereotypes are half right and misleading in the other half.

**[Hierarchy](/blog/japan-nenkou-seniority-explained) is real.** French organisations are more formal and more vertical than Nordic or Dutch ones. The *vouvoiement* form of address is standard with people you do not know well and with seniors, and switching to *tu* is something that happens, not something you initiate on day one.

**Meetings are for discussion and debate.** Disagreement is intellectually respectable and directness about ideas is not read as personal. Debating a proposal robustly is engagement, not obstruction.

**The lunch break is genuinely protected**, and eating at your desk is faintly sad rather than impressive.

**Hours are long in some sectors** — finance, consulting and law in Paris are demanding — and protected in others. The 35-hour week is a legal reference point, not a description of every job.

**Holiday is taken seriously and taken.** Statutory paid leave is generous, RTT days add to it, and August genuinely empties out.

**Written communication is formal.** French professional emails have conventions — proper openings, formal closings — and a breezy Anglo-American email register reads as careless rather than friendly.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You hold a master's degree and a good offer

Check Talent Passport eligibility immediately. It is multi-year, avoids the labour market test, and gives your family full working rights — a materially better position than the standard salaried permit.

### Scenario 2: Your French is limited

Focus on Paris technology and research, international companies, and the English-speaking research institutions. It is a real market but a fraction of the whole, and French study should start now.

### Scenario 3: You are offered a CDD rather than a CDI

Take it if the work is right, and understand what it means practically — renting and borrowing are harder. Many CDIs begin as CDDs, and a year of French experience makes the next conversation very different.

> **Tip:** If you have a master's degree, check the Talent Passport before accepting any other permit route. It is multi-year, skips the labour market test, and gives your partner full working rights — and a surprising number of eligible graduates are put onto the standard permit by employers who never looked.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Check Talent Passport eligibility first
- Understand the CDI/CDD distinction properly
- Consider stage and alternance as entry routes
- Look beyond Paris to Lyon, Toulouse and Grenoble
- State your qualification level in French terms
- Start French study seriously

### Don't

- Treat a CDD as equivalent to a CDI for housing and credit
- Assume the standard salaried permit is your only [opt](/blog/opt-cpt-resume-guide)ion
- Ignore the weight French employers give to education
- Expect the whole market to run in English
- Overlook the RECE post-study permit
- Underestimate the length of the période d'essai

## Common Mistakes

- **Missing the Talent Passport.** Many eligible graduates are placed on the standard permit by default.
- **Underrating the contract type.** A CDI changes your access to housing and credit, not just your job security.
- **Ignoring stage and alternance.** They are the mainstream entry route, not a consolation prize.
- **Assuming English is enough.** It works in a slice of Paris and not much else.
- **Paris tunnel vision.** Toulouse, Grenoble, Lyon and Nantes have real technical employment.
- **Understating your education.** French readers use it as a calibration point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between CDI and CDD?

CDI is a permanent contract with strong protection and social legitimacy — landlords and banks look for it. CDD is fixed-term, normal early in a career, and a common step toward a CDI.

### What is the Talent Passport?

A multi-year French residence permit for qualified employees, researchers and founders. It avoids the labour market test and grants family members full working rights.

### Do I need to speak French?

For most of the market, yes. Technology, research and international companies in Paris run substantially in English, but that is a fraction of the whole.

### Do grandes écoles really matter that much?

They carry unusual weight and their alumni networks are powerful. If you did not attend one, lean on demonstrable outcomes and specific technical capability instead.

### What is the RECE permit?

A post-study permit letting graduates of French institutions at master's level and above remain to look for work or start a business, with a route into the Talent Passport afterwards.

### What is alternance?

An apprenticeship-style contract combining study and paid work. Along with the stage (internship), it is the mainstream entry route into French professional employment.
### Why is my French net pay so much lower than gross?

Social contributions funding health, pension, unemployment and family benefits are substantial. Ask for the net monthly figure when comparing offers — the brut number is not what you live on.

### What are RTT days?

Additional days off accrued by employees working beyond the 35-hour standard week. Combined with statutory leave, French holiday entitlement is generous and genuinely used.

### Is Paris the only option?

No. Toulouse for aerospace, Grenoble for microelectronics, Lyon for life sciences, and Nantes, Bordeaux and Lille for growing technology scenes at half the housing cost.
### What do I need to rent an apartment in France?

Typically proof of income at several times the rent, and often a French guarantor plus a CDI. State-backed guarantee schemes such as Visale exist for people without a French guarantor.

### What is a Carte Vitale?

The French health card linked to your social security number, used at pharmacies and doctors for reimbursement. Apply for the number as soon as you start work.
