## Key Takeaways

- Assessment centres score you against defined competencies, not against the other candidates in the room.
- The group exercise is not a debate to win. Assessors are watching how you handle other people.
- You are being observed during breaks and informal moments, not only in the scored tasks.
- Most tasks reward visible structure — saying what you are doing and why — over reaching the perfect answer.
- Preparing for the format matters as much as preparing the content, because the format is unfamiliar to most candidates.

## What an assessment centre is

The final stage of most UK graduate schemes. Half a day to a full day, at an employer's office or online, with several tasks and multiple assessors.

A typical day:

- **Group exercise** — a problem to solve as a team, 30 to 45 minutes
- **Case study** — individual analysis, often written, sometimes presented
- **Presentation** — prepared in advance or on the day
- **Competency interview** — one or two, structured
- **In-tray or situational exercise** — prioritising a set of realistic work items
- Informal elements — lunch, a talk from current graduates, time with recruiters

Not every employer runs all of these. The [graduate scheme process](/blog/uk-graduate-scheme-guide) usually specifies the format in advance, and asking the recruiter directly is normal.

## The scoring model

This is the thing most candidates misunderstand, and it changes how you should behave.

Assessors work from a **competency framework**. Typically five to eight named competencies — commercial awareness, teamwork, analytical thinking, communication, resilience, leadership — each with behavioural indicators and a scale.

During a task, an assessor is watching one or two candidates and writing evidence against those competencies. Afterwards, assessors pool their notes and agree scores.

The consequences:

**It is not a competition against the room.** You are scored against a standard. In principle everyone can pass, or nobody can. Undermining another candidate does not raise your score and frequently lowers it, because collaboration is usually one of the measured competencies.

**Evidence must be visible.** An assessor cannot score a thought you did not express. Excellent silent reasoning produces an empty page.

**Each task maps to specific competencies.** The group exercise is rarely scored on whether the group reached the right answer.

## The group exercise

Five to eight candidates, a scenario, a time limit, sometimes conflicting information distributed among you.

What assessors are looking for:

- **Do you contribute substance?** Not volume — substance. One well-judged intervention beats constant talking.
- **Do you bring others in?** "We haven't heard from you — what do you think?" is one of the highest-scoring behaviours available and costs nothing.
- **Do you manage the time?** Someone noticing there are ten minutes left and steering toward a conclusion scores well.
- **Do you build on others' points?** Explicitly referencing and developing someone else's idea shows listening.
- **Do you handle disagreement well?** Disagreeing with the point rather than the person.

What loses marks:

- **Dominating.** The candidate who talks most usually does not score highest.
- **Silence.** Nothing to write down means nothing to score.
- **Ignoring the clock.** Groups that run out of time without a conclusion all score poorly.
- **Point-scoring off others.** Visible, and it reads as poor teamwork.

The counterintuitive part: **helping the group succeed is how you succeed.** A group that produces a coherent answer gives every member evidence of good collaboration.

## The case study

An individual exercise: a pack of information, a question, a time limit, usually a written recommendation or a short presentation.

What works:

**Read the question first, then the pack.** Reading everything before knowing what you are looking for wastes the time you needed for analysis.

**Structure before you write.** Two minutes of planning produces a document an assessor can follow.

**Answer the question asked.** A brilliant analysis of a different question scores badly. If asked whether to proceed, say whether to proceed.

**Commit to a recommendation.** "It depends" without a decision is the most common weak ending. State a position, give the main reason, name the biggest risk.

**Use the numbers.** If the pack contains figures, they are there deliberately. An answer that ignores them is incomplete.

## Presentations

Sometimes prepared beforehand, sometimes built during the day.

Keep it simple: a clear structure, a small number of points, and a conclusion that answers the brief. Assessors are scoring clarity and structure, not visual design.

Time yourself. Overrunning is scored badly and is entirely avoidable. Expect questions afterwards and prepare for the obvious ones — the questions are often where the real assessment happens.

## The interview

Usually competency-based: "tell me about a time when..." with heavy probing.

UK competency interviews go deeper than a single answer. Expect three or four follow-ups on the same story — what was your specific role, why that approach, what would you do differently, how did you know it worked. A story that holds up for ninety seconds and collapses on the second probe is a common failure.

Prepare six to eight solid stories rather than one per question. The [approach to building a story bank](/blog/usa-behavioral-interview-star-campus) applies directly, and [UK interview conventions](/blog/uk-interview-tips) cover tone.

## The parts nobody tells you are scored

**Lunch and breaks.** Assessors and current graduates are present. You are not being formally scored, but impressions are shared. The candidate who is charming to assessors and dismissive to the catering staff is noticed.

**How you treat other candidates.** Repeatedly observed and frequently discussed in the wrap-up.

**Your questions.** Having none reads as disinterest. Two specific questions per assessor is plenty.

## Preparing properly

**Ask what the format is.** Recruiters routinely tell you the tasks and sometimes the competencies. It is free information.

**Learn the employer's competency framework.** Many publish their values or graduate competencies. Preparing evidence against each is the highest-return preparation available.

**Practise a group exercise at least once.** The format is genuinely unfamiliar, and the first time should not be the real one. University careers services usually run them.

**Do a timed case study.** Time pressure is the difficulty, and practising under it is different from reading about it.

**Bring the basics.** Copies of your CV, a pen and paper, your ID.

Everything here assumes you reached this stage, which means your application survived screening — [UK CV format](/blog/uk-cv-format-guide-2026) and passing an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) are what get you to the assessment centre in the first place.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the group exercise as a debate to win.** Collaboration is usually a scored competency.
- **Talking constantly.** Volume is mistaken for contribution and scores badly.
- **Staying silent.** No evidence means no score.
- **Ignoring the time limit.** Whole groups fail this way.
- **No recommendation in the case study.** Analysis without a decision reads as indecision.
- **Relaxing at lunch.** Impressions formed informally are shared formally.
- **Not asking what the format is.** The information is available for the price of an email.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are candidates ranked against each other?

Not usually. Scoring is against a competency framework, so in principle all or none could pass. This is why undermining others is counterproductive.

### What if the group exercise has a dominant person?

Manage it rather than compete with it. "That's a good point — can we check we've heard from everyone before we decide?" scores well precisely because it demonstrates the competency being tested.

### Should I lead the group?

Only if it is genuinely needed and you can do it without steamrolling. Effective contribution scores as well as formal leadership, and forced leadership is transparent.

### How long is an assessment centre?

Half a day to a full day. Virtual ones are usually shorter but denser.

### What if I do badly in one task?

They are scored separately and pooled. One weak task rarely eliminates you if the others are strong, so recover rather than dwelling on it during the day.

### Can I take notes during the group exercise?

Yes, and it usually helps. Noting others' points lets you reference them, which is a scored behaviour.
