Students and freshers face a specific version of the job-search problem: you are applying to *more* roles than an experienced candidate, with a *thinner* resume, and often with no idea why your applications vanish. The right browser extension helps disproportionately here — more than it does for a mid-career applicant — precisely because your situation demands more tailoring over less material. This guide covers what actually helps a student job hunt, and what to skip.

## Why Extensions Help Students Most

The math is lopsided in your favour. An experienced candidate applies to a handful of targeted roles with a resume full of relevant experience. A student applies broadly — many roles, many companies, many sectors — because you are still figuring out what fits, and your resume is short, so every posting needs more adaptation to look like a match.

That means more tailoring, more often, over a resume where each change matters more. And tailoring by hand is exactly what a busy student — juggling classes, projects, maybe a part-time job — does not have time for. So the friction that stops everyone from tailoring stops students hardest, which is why an on-page tool that makes tailoring [take one click](/blog/one-click-resume-tailoring-how-it-works) instead of ten minutes helps you more than almost anyone. The [ATS filter](/blog/ats-resume-checker-how-it-works) does not go easy on freshers; if anything, it is where your applications are quietly dying.

## What a Student Actually Needs

Not every job-search tool matters equally when you are starting out. In priority order:

**1. A free ATS score.** Before anything, you need to know whether your resume is even the problem. A [free, unlimited match score](/blog/how-to-check-ats-score-free) tells you — and "free and unlimited" matters enormously on a student budget, because a tool that charges or limits the diagnosis is one you cannot actually use across a real search. Your [ATS score as a fresher](/blog/ats-score-for-freshers) works a little differently, leaning on projects and coursework.

**2. On-page tailoring.** The tool that closes the gap the score reveals, in one click, on the posting. This is the highest-leverage thing for a student because you tailor so often.

**3. A cover letter generator.** Freshers benefit from cover letters more than experienced candidates, because a [letter with no work experience](/blog/cover-letter-no-work-experience) is where you sell projects, enthusiasm and potential — the things a thin resume cannot fully show.

**4. A portfolio.** Not strictly an extension, but for students it is often the single biggest differentiator — a [portfolio site](/blog/how-to-build-professional-portfolio) shows real work when your resume is still short, and [linking it](/blog/portfolio-website-vs-resume) gives recruiters something to actually look at.

What you do *not* need yet: heavy autofill (student applications are often shorter forms), or LinkedIn-influencer tooling. Keep your toolbar lean.

## Why Free Matters (and What "Free" Should Mean)

On a student budget, "free" is not a nice-to-have — it is the whole question. But be careful what free means. Some tools let you do the work free and then charge to download the result. Others give you five checks and then a paywall right when your search heats up. Neither is genuinely usable for a real student search.

The honest version: the *diagnosis* should be free and unlimited, because you should never pay to find out whether you have a problem. ## The Mistake Most Students Make

There is one error that sinks more student job searches than any other, and it is worth naming because it feels like the opposite of a mistake: applying to as many jobs as humanly possible with one resume.

It feels productive — a hundred applications must beat ten, surely. But a hundred generic applications to a [thin resume that does not match](/blog/tailored-vs-generic-resume) is a hundred near-certain rejections, and it burns the time and morale you needed for the applications that could have worked. Students fall into this harder than anyone because the pressure is real — placement season, graduation looming, peers getting offers — and volume feels like control. It is not. It is a busy way of getting filtered out a hundred times.

The students who do well invert this. They apply to fewer roles, [tailor each one](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) (fast, on the page), and put real effort into the [cover letter](/blog/cover-letter-guide-2026) and [interview prep](/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-job-interview-in-2026-complete-guide-for-every-industry). Ten matched applications with tailored resumes beat a hundred generic ones, and they cost less total effort once tailoring is one click. The lesson every early-career job seeker learns eventually — usually the hard way — is that a job search is won on [match quality, not application count](/blog/how-many-jobs-should-you-apply-to-per-day). Learn it early and you save yourself months.

The second most common mistake is treating projects as an afterthought. For a student, [projects are your experience](/blog/fresher-resume-projects-that-get-interviews) — they are what a thin resume leans on, and a [portfolio site showing real work](/blog/portfolio-website-vs-resume) often does more than another line of coursework. Build one, link it, and let it do the talking a short resume cannot.
