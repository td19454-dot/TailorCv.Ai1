## Key Takeaways

- **The summary has replaced the objective** in almost all cases, because it serves the reader rather than the writer.
- **An objective states what you want**; a summary states what you offer.
- **A few situations still suit an objective**, mainly genuine career changes and first roles.
- **Three lines is the working length**, and it must be rewritten per role type.
- **A generic summary is worse than none**, because it wastes the most valuable space on the page.

## Why the objective faded

The reasoning behind the shift.

**It addresses the wrong reader.** An objective describes your goals, while the person reading is assessing whether you solve their problem.

**It was almost always generic.** Seeking a challenging role in a dynamic organisation described no one and excluded nothing.

**It occupied the best space on the page** with content that helped nobody decide anything.

**The role you are applying to already states the objective.** You want this job; that is established by the fact of the application.

**And it dated the document.** A resume opening with an objective signals a template that has not been revisited in a long time.

**The summary inverts all of this** by using the same space to state what you bring, which is the question the reader actually has.

## What a summary contains

Three lines, doing three jobs.

| Line | Content |
|---|---|
| **First** | Who you are professionally and at what level |
| **Second** | The most relevant experience or capability for this role |
| **Third** | A concrete proof point or the specific value you bring |

**Name the role you are applying for**, using [the posting](/blog/how-to-read-a-job-description-properly)'s title, so the match is immediate.

**Include your years or level** where it helps rather than where it dates you.

**Put one specific thing in it** — a number, a named system, a domain — because a summary of pure adjectives is indistinguishable from every other summary.

**Avoid the standard filler.** Results-driven, detail-oriented and passionate appear on so many resumes that they carry no information at all.

**And rewrite it per role type**, not per application. One version per kind of job you apply for is usually enough, adjusted lightly each time.

## Writing the three lines

A method that produces a usable summary quickly.

**Start from the posting.** Identify the role's core requirement, because your summary's job is to answer it in the first line.

**Write the first line as an identity statement** — what you are and at what level — using the title the posting uses rather than your internal one.

**Write the second line as your closest relevant capability**, choosing the single strongest match rather than listing several.

**Write the third line as evidence.** A number, a named system, a scale or a domain, which is what separates a summary from a self-description.

**Then cut every adjective** that survives from the first draft, since almost none of them are earning their space.

**Read it against the posting one final time** and check that someone comparing the two would see the match immediately.

**The whole process takes about ten minutes** once you have a version per role type, and it is the highest-return ten minutes in the document.

## Summary examples by situation

How the shape changes with circumstance.

**Experienced and staying in field.** Lead with the level and the specialism, then the strongest capability, then a proof point. This is the simplest case and the most common.

**[Career changer](/blog/career-change-to-tech-guide).** Lead with the transferable capability described in the target field's language, then the bridging experience, then evidence that the transition is already underway.

**Recent graduate.** Lead with the field and the qualification, then the most relevant project or placement, then a concrete outcome from it.

**Returning after a break.** Lead with the professional identity and level, then the relevant capability, then something current that demonstrates the skills are live.

**[Contractor](/blog/resume-for-contract-to-full-time) moving to permanent.** Lead with the breadth of the contract work, then the specialism, then the reason permanent work suits the next stage.

**Internal applicant.** Lead with your current role and tenure, then the capability the new role needs, then a result from within the organisation that the reader can verify easily.

**In every case the structure holds** — identity, relevance, evidence — and only the emphasis changes.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You are changing careers

This is the strongest remaining case for objective-like language, but write it as a summary. State the transferable capability first and the direction second, so it opens with what you offer rather than what you want.

### Scenario 2: You are applying for your first role

Use a summary built from education, projects and any relevant experience. A graduate with no employment history still has capabilities to state, and an objective adds nothing.

### Scenario 3: You are applying within your existing field at your current level

A straightforward summary works. Name the role, the experience and one proof point, then let the rest of the resume carry the detail.

> **Tip:** Write your summary last, after the rest of the resume is tailored. It is a compression of what follows, and writing it first means compressing something that does not exist yet.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Write a summary rather than an objective
- Name the target role explicitly
- Include one concrete specific
- Keep it to three lines
- Rewrite it per role type
- Write it after tailoring the rest

### Don't

- Open with what you are seeking
- Use filler adjectives
- Keep one summary for a year
- Write five lines of prose
- Repeat your entire resume in miniature
- Leave the space empty

## Common Mistakes

- **Using an objective.** It answers a question nobody asked.
- **Generic adjectives.** They occupy the best space on the page with nothing.
- **A permanent summary.** The clearest marker of an untailored resume.
- **Excessive length.** Beyond three lines it stops being read.
- **No specifics.** A summary without one concrete detail is decoration.
- **Writing it first.** It is a compression of the tailored document.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I use a summary or an objective?

A summary, in almost every case. An objective states what you want, while a summary states what you offer, which is the question the reader actually has.

### When is an objective still appropriate?

Rarely, and even for career changes it is better written as a summary that leads with transferable capability rather than with your intention.

### How long should a summary be?

Three lines or fewer. Beyond that it stops being read, which defeats the purpose of occupying the most valuable space on the page.

### What should the summary contain?

Who you are professionally, the most relevant capability for this role, and one concrete proof point. Name the target role using the posting's own title.

### How often should I rewrite it?

Per role type rather than per application. Maintain one version for each kind of job you apply to and adjust it lightly for individual postings.

### Is no summary better than a generic one?

Close to it. A generic summary wastes the best space on the page, though a specific one is considerably better than leaving that space empty.
### How do I write a resume summary quickly?

Start from the posting's core requirement. Write an identity line using their title, a capability line naming your closest match, and an evidence line with something concrete. Then cut every adjective.

### Does the summary change for a career change or a graduate?

The structure stays identity, relevance, evidence. A career changer leads with transferable capability in the target field's language; a graduate leads with the field and qualification, then the strongest project.
