## Key Takeaways

- Four things change per application: your summary, your top bullets' order, your skills emphasis, and the specific vocabulary for two or three central requirements.
- Five things stay fixed: your employment history, dates, titles, education, and the factual content of your achievements.
- Tailoring is mostly reordering and renaming, not rewriting - which is why it should take minutes, not hours.
- If you are rewriting bullets from scratch for every application, you are doing more work than the return justifies.
- The test for any change: would it still be true if the employer called your last manager?

Most people either do not tailor at all, or they tailor by rewriting the whole document and give up after four applications. Both fail for the same reason: no clear line between what moves and what stays.

Here is the line.

## The Four Things That Change

### 1. Your summary

The most valuable three lines on the page, and the cheapest to adapt. Your summary should name the role you are applying for and the two capabilities that posting cares about most.

> **Generic:** Experienced operations professional with a track record of delivering results in fast-paced environments.
>
> **For a process-improvement role:** Operations coordinator with four years in process improvement and stakeholder management across finance and supply chain.
>
> **For a project-delivery role:** Operations coordinator who has planned and delivered cross-functional projects to deadline, including a 120-person relocation on a £180k budget.

Same person, same facts, different emphasis. This alone does much of the work of tailoring.

### 2. The order of your top bullets

Not their content - their order. Whatever this posting cares about most should be the first bullet under your most recent role.

This is the highest-return edit available and it takes fifteen seconds. A recruiter reads the first two bullets under your current job. If the relevant one is fourth, it is effectively invisible.

### 3. Which skills you lead with

Keep your skills section largely stable, but reorder it so the posting's central terms appear first, and drop items that are irrelevant to this application.

A posting asking for SQL and dbt should not have to read past Excel and PowerPoint to find them.

### 4. The vocabulary for two or three central requirements

Where the posting names something you have done under a different name, use their name. This is translation, not invention.

> **Yours:** Worked with suppliers on contracts and delivery schedules.
>
> **Theirs:** vendor management, SLA management
>
> **Tailored:** Owned vendor management for six suppliers, holding delivery to agreed SLAs.

Two or three of these per application is enough. Do not attempt the whole missing-keyword list.

## The Five Things That Stay

**Your employment history.** Every role, in order. Dropping a job to make the story tidier creates a gap you will have to explain, and background checks surface the omission.

**Your dates.** Never adjusted to close a gap or extend tenure. This is the most verifiable data on your resume.

**Your job titles.** Clarify an unclear internal title in brackets if you like. Do not substitute one.

**Your education.** Fixed facts. The only variation is placement - higher when you are early-career, lower once experience carries more weight.

**The factual content of your achievements.** The numbers, the scale, the outcomes. You can reword and reorder them. You cannot inflate them for a posting that wants bigger ones.

## The Ten-Minute Routine

**1. Read the posting once (2 min).** Identify the two or three requirements that repeat across the title, opening paragraph and bullet list. Those are the real ones.

**2. Rewrite your summary (3 min).** Name the role. Name those two or three capabilities. Keep it three lines.

**3. Reorder your top bullets (1 min).** Most relevant first, under your most recent role.

**4. Adjust your skills line (1 min).** Lead with the posting's terms that you genuinely have. Drop the irrelevant ones.

**5. Rename two or three things (3 min).** Where you did the work under a different name, use theirs.

Ten minutes. Everything else on the document stays exactly as it was.

## When Something Bigger Is Warranted

Occasionally a deeper rewrite is worth it - a career change, a role a level above yours, or a target you are genuinely invested in. In those cases you may reorder entire roles, expand a relevant project into its own section, or rework several bullets.

That is a different exercise with a different budget, and it should be rare. If every application feels like it needs one, the problem is usually targeting: you are applying across too wide a range for one base resume to serve.

## The Honesty Test

For any change you are considering, ask: **would this still be true if the employer called my last manager?**

Reordering bullets - yes. Renaming client management as stakeholder management - yes, if that is what it was. Adding a tool you have not used, inflating a team size, extending your tenure by four months - no.

That single question covers almost every judgment call in tailoring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should tailoring take per application?**
Ten to fifteen minutes once you have a solid base resume. If it takes an hour, you are rewriting rather than tailoring.

**Should I keep a separate file for every application?**
Keep two or three base versions for genuinely different role types, then tailor per application from the closest one. A file per application becomes unmanageable quickly.

**Is it worth tailoring for a role I am unlikely to get?**
If it is a stretch, tailoring matters more, not less - your relevance is less obvious and needs surfacing. If it is a genuine mismatch, skip the application entirely.

**Does tailoring the summary really make a difference?**
It is the first thing read after your title. Three lines that name the role and the right capabilities change the reader's frame for everything below.

**What if the posting is vague and I cannot find the central requirements?**
Use the job title and the first paragraph. If those are vague too, tailor lightly and spend the saved time on a clearer posting.

## Check What Actually Needs Changing

The fastest way to find the two or three things worth renaming is to see which of the posting's central terms are missing from your resume.

[Scan your resume against the posting](/ats-analysis) and use the missing list as your tailoring worklist - it is usually shorter than you expect, and most of it is vocabulary rather than substance.
