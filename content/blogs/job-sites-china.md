## Key Takeaways

- The Chinese job market runs on domestic platforms, not company career pages - applying the Western way reaches a small fraction of the listings.
- BOSS Zhipin is built around messaging the hiring manager directly, which changes what your first message needs to do.
- LinkedIn wound down its local China operation, so it now mainly reaches multinationals rather than domestic employers.
- Most platforms build a structured profile from your uploaded resume, so how your file parses determines how you appear in recruiter searches.
- English-language boards carry far fewer listings but concentrate the roles genuinely open to foreign applicants.

Job hunting in China fails for a lot of foreign applicants before it starts, and usually for the same reason: they apply the way they would at home. They find companies they like, visit the careers page, and submit through a form.

That reaches a fraction of the market. The bulk of Chinese hiring flows through a handful of domestic platforms with their own conventions, their own screening logic, and in one prominent case a chat-first model that has no real Western equivalent.

This guide covers which platforms matter, what each is good for, and how to set yourself up so recruiters can actually find you.

## The Major Domestic Platforms

### BOSS Zhipin (BOSS直聘)

The most distinctive of the group. Instead of submitting an application into a queue, you message the hiring side directly and the conversation starts immediately. It is heavily mobile-first and very widely used, particularly for technology and startup roles.

**What this changes for you:** your opening message does the work a cover letter would do elsewhere, in about three lines. Lead with the role, one concrete piece of evidence, and your practical situation. Response rates depend on being specific fast.

**Best for:** tech, startups, mid-level roles, anyone who wants speed.

### 51job (前程无忧)

One of the long-established general platforms, covering a broad range of industries and seniority levels. More traditional in flow - you apply, and the employer reviews.

**Best for:** broad coverage, corporate roles, non-tech industries.

### Zhaopin (智联招聘)

The other major general-purpose platform, comparable in scale and coverage to 51job. Many candidates maintain profiles on both, since employer usage is split.

**Best for:** general coverage, particularly outside the tech sector.

### Liepin (猎聘)

Positioned toward mid-to-senior and executive hiring, with heavy headhunter involvement. Less useful early career, genuinely useful once you have a decade behind you.

**Best for:** senior roles, management, specialist positions.

### Lagou (拉勾)

Focused on internet and technology roles. Smaller than the giants but concentrated in the sector, which makes browsing more efficient if that is your field.

**Best for:** internet, software, product roles.

## English-Language and Foreigner-Focused Sites

These carry far fewer listings, but the listings they carry are pre-filtered for the thing you need most: employers willing and able to hire foreign nationals.

**eChinacities** and **HiredChina** are among the better-known options aimed at foreign job seekers, with a heavy weighting toward teaching, plus some professional and technical roles.

**Teaching-specific boards** cover the largest single category of foreign employment in China. Vet employers carefully here - the range in quality is wide, and the sector changed significantly after the 2021 regulatory reforms. See [teaching English in China](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/teaching-english-in-china-resume) for how to evaluate roles and build the application.

**LinkedIn** is worth keeping current, but understand its position: after winding down its localised China service it functions mainly as a route into multinationals and foreign-invested companies, and for recruiters based outside China hiring into it. Keep the profile strong using the [LinkedIn profile optimization guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/linkedin-profile-optimization-guide), but do not treat it as your primary channel.

## WeChat Is a Job Channel

This is the part missing from most guides written from outside China.

A meaningful share of hiring circulates through WeChat - industry groups, city-based expat groups, company official accounts posting openings, and recruiters who move a conversation there within two messages of contact.

Practical steps:

- Join groups for your city and industry, and read before posting
- Set your WeChat profile so it does not undercut a professional first impression
- Put your WeChat ID on your resume - covered in the [Chinese resume format guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/chinese-resume-format-guide-2026)
- Expect recruiters to move the conversation to WeChat quickly, and to reply there faster than by email

The underlying approach is ordinary networking, just on a different surface - [networking tips for job search](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/networking-tips-for-job-search) transfers directly.

## Recruiters and Agencies

Recruitment agencies play a larger role in China than in many Western markets, particularly for foreign hires, senior placements, and teaching.

Worth using, with judgement. Signals of a legitimate agency: they are clear about the employer's identity, they can explain the work permit process accurately, and they do not ask you for fees. Signals to walk away from: pressure to accept quickly, vagueness about the sponsoring entity, or any suggestion that you can start work before the permit is issued. That last one is not a shortcut - it is a legal risk, as the [China work visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/china-work-visa-z-visa-guide) explains.

## How These Platforms Screen You

Whichever site you use, one mechanic is consistent: the platform builds a structured profile from what you upload, and recruiters search that structure.

This has direct consequences for your file:

**Your resume must parse.** Text in images, unusual layouts, and content trapped in headers or footers gets lost in extraction. The failure modes are in [ATS resume formatting mistakes](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ats-resume-formatting-mistakes).

**Job titles are search fields.** A creative internal title returns nothing when a recruiter searches the standard term. Use the recognisable title, and put the internal one in brackets if it matters.

**Keywords matter, honestly used.** Recruiters search by skill and tool. If you have the skill, name it explicitly - see [the best resume keywords for China](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/best-resume-keywords-china) and the general method in [best resume keywords to beat ATS](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/best-resume-keywords-to-beat-ats).

**Profile completeness affects visibility.** Most platforms surface complete profiles above sparse ones. Fill everything in, including the fields that feel redundant.

**Your stated city matters.** Search is heavily location-filtered. Set it deliberately, and if you are willing to relocate, say so in the field rather than only in your resume.

## Common Mistakes

**Only applying through company career pages.** Reaches a small slice of the market.

**Ignoring the Chinese-language platforms because of the interface.** Translation tools handle them adequately, and this is where the listings are.

**One resume for every platform.** Tailor per posting - [how to tailor your resume for every job](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) is the method.

**Treating BOSS Zhipin like a job board.** It is a conversation. A blank application with no message performs poorly.

**Leaving WeChat off your resume.** Recruiters will try it first.

**Not stating visa status anywhere.** Employers screening foreign applicants need to know immediately whether they must sponsor you.

**Relying on LinkedIn alone.** It reaches multinationals, not the domestic market.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which job site is best for foreigners in China?

It depends on the role. For multinationals, LinkedIn and the English-language boards. For teaching, the foreigner-focused and teaching-specific sites. For domestic professional roles, you need the Chinese platforms - BOSS Zhipin for tech and startups, 51job and Zhaopin for broad coverage, Liepin for senior positions.

### Do I need Chinese to use these platforms?

The interfaces are in Chinese, but browser translation handles them well enough to navigate and apply. The bigger question is the roles themselves - most domestic listings expect working Mandarin. Options without it are covered in [working in China without Mandarin](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/working-in-china-without-mandarin).

### Is LinkedIn still useful for China?

For multinationals and foreign-invested companies, yes. For domestic employers, much less so since the local service wound down. Keep it current, but do not make it your only channel.

### Should I pay for premium features?

Generally not at the start. Free tiers are enough to test whether your profile attracts responses. If you are getting no traction, the problem is usually the resume or the targeting rather than the tier.

### How do I avoid scams?

Be wary of anyone asking you for fees, being vague about which company is actually hiring, pressuring a fast decision, or suggesting you start work before the permit is issued. Verify the employer independently and confirm they are licensed to sponsor foreign staff.
