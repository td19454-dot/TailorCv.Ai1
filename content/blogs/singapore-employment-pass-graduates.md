## Key Takeaways

- The Employment Pass is the main route for graduates in professional roles; the S Pass covers mid-skilled work and is subject to employer quotas.
- Qualifying salary is not a single number — it rises with age, so a fresh graduate faces a lower threshold than an experienced hire.
- Passes are applied for by the employer, not by you. You cannot hold one independently of a job.
- A points-based assessment now sits alongside the salary threshold, scoring things you do not control, such as employer diversity and local workforce ratios.
- Thresholds and rules are reviewed periodically. Verify current figures with the Ministry of Manpower before relying on them.

## The passes that matter for graduates

**Employment Pass (EP)** — for foreign professionals, managers and executives. This is the pass most graduates in professional roles are hired on. It requires a job offer, a qualifying salary, and acceptable qualifications.

**S Pass** — for mid-skilled staff. Lower salary threshold than EP, but subject to a quota: an employer can only hold so many S Pass holders as a proportion of their local workforce, and pays a levy for each. That quota makes S Pass hiring a business decision as much as a hiring one.

**Work Permit** — for semi-skilled work in specific sectors. Not the relevant route for most graduates.

There are also passes for entrepreneurs and for holders of exceptional credentials, but for a fresh graduate the practical question is almost always EP, sometimes S Pass.

## Qualifying salary rises with age

This is the mechanic most people miss, and it works in a graduate's favour.

The EP qualifying salary is not one figure. It increases with age, on the reasoning that an older candidate should command more. A fresh graduate in their early twenties faces the lowest threshold on the scale; a forty-five-year-old faces a considerably higher one.

The financial services sector has its own, higher thresholds.

Two consequences:

**A graduate is cheaper to hire on an EP than an experienced foreigner**

in the same role, because the salary floor is lower. This is a genuine advantage in your first job.

**Thresholds are raised periodically.** They have moved several times in recent years. Check the current figure with the Ministry of Manpower rather than relying on a number in an article, including this one.

## The points-based assessment

Alongside meeting the salary threshold, EP applications are assessed under a points framework. Understanding it explains hiring behaviour that otherwise looks arbitrary.

Points are awarded across several dimensions, including:

- **Salary relative to local benchmarks** for the role and sector
- **Qualifications**, with recognised institutions scoring higher
- **Diversity of the employer's workforce** — how concentrated it is by nationality
- **Support for local employment** — the employer's ratio of local to foreign staff
- Bonus points for skills on shortage lists and other specific criteria

The important insight: **several of these are properties of the employer, not of you.** A strong candidate at a firm with a heavily concentrated foreign workforce can score worse than an average candidate at a firm with a balanced one.

That is why two companies respond so differently to the same application. It is frequently not about your profile at all.

Some exemptions exist, typically for very high earners and certain shortage occupations.

## What this means for your job search

**Ask whether the employer sponsors, early.** Not "do you sponsor" in the abstract, but "have you obtained EPs for graduate hires recently?" A firm that does it routinely has a process; one that has never done it is being asked to learn.

**Understand that small firms often cannot.** The levy, the quota for S Pass, and the points framework all bear more heavily on smaller employers. This is not prejudice — it is arithmetic.

**Local hiring requirements come first.** Employers generally must advertise locally and demonstrate fair consideration of local candidates before hiring a foreigner. This lengthens timelines, which is why applying early matters more for you than for a local classmate — the same structural point that applies to the [Singapore graduate recruiting calendar](/blog/singapore-campus-recruiting-guide).

**A local degree helps.** Not as a formal points item in every case, but in familiarity: employers know the institutions, the transition is routine, and you are already in the country.

**Do not lead with pass status in interviews.** Answer clearly when asked. Unlike the US — where [visa status does not belong on the resume at all](/blog/usa-resume-format-for-students) — Singapore convention does accept stating your status on the document, because pass eligibility is genuinely part of the hiring decision. Keep it to one line.

## If you are graduating from a Singapore university

Your position is materially stronger than an overseas applicant's:

- You are already in-country, so there is no relocation uncertainty
- Employers recognise your institution without needing to assess it
- Campus recruiting channels are open to you
- Internships done locally are directly relevant experience with local references

There are also schemes allowing recent graduates a period to remain and seek work. The details change, so confirm the current position with MOM or your university's career office rather than relying on what a senior told you two years ago.

## If you are applying from overseas

Harder, but far from impossible. What works:

**Target multinationals with regional headquarters in Singapore.**

They hire internationally as a matter of course and have practised immigration functions.

**Target shortage skills.** Where demand genuinely exceeds local supply — some technical and specialised finance roles — employers are much more willing to absorb the process.

**Build a referral.** A referral from someone inside the company changes an unsolicited overseas application into a considered one, and it is the most reliable way past the initial screen.

**Make the resume unambiguous.** An overseas application is being read quickly by someone deciding whether you are worth the administrative effort. Evidence, outcomes and clean formatting matter more than they do for a local candidate. Run it through an [ATS check](/ats-analysis) — an overseas application that parses badly gets no second look.

## What this means for how you search

The pass framework is not a detail to sort out after an offer. It determines which employers were ever realistic, and using it as a filter early saves an entire cycle.

**Ask whether the employer sponsors, in the first conversation.**

"Does this role consider candidates who would require an Employment Pass?" is a normal, factual question. Recruiters at employers who hire internationally hear it constantly, and the answer takes ten seconds. Asking at offer stage instead is how candidates lose months.

**Understand why small firms often cannot.** This is arithmetic rather than attitude. The framework weighs employer-side factors — the local-to-foreign ratio in the workforce, the firm's standing within its sector — and a ten-person company simply scores differently from a multinational with a large local workforce. A startup that genuinely wants you may still be unable to make the numbers work at graduate salary levels.

**Weight your list toward employers who hire internationally as routine.**

Regional headquarters of multinationals, the larger banks, and organisations with established immigration processes. The [technology employer groups](/blog/singapore-tech-jobs-graduates) differ sharply on this and filtering early is the single most valuable planning step.

**Target scarce skills.** Where a skill is genuinely in short supply locally, the business case for hiring a foreigner is easier to make and the employer is more willing to go through the process. This is one of the few areas where what you studied changes the answer materially.

**Build a referral.** A referred candidate at an employer who does sponsor gets read, and the internal advocate frequently knows the answer to the pass question before you have to ask it.

**Make the resume unambiguous.** State your availability date and your current status plainly. Unlike [US convention](/blog/usa-resume-format-for-students), this is expected here, and ambiguity produces a recruiter guessing rather than progressing you.

**Do not lead with pass status in interviews.** State it once, plainly, when asked or at a natural point, then move on. The same principle as [answering the sponsorship question](/blog/usa-visa-sponsorship-question-interview) applies: brevity signals routine, elaboration signals a problem.

## Local graduate or overseas applicant

Your situation changes the strategy substantially, and the two cases are worth separating.

**If you are graduating from a Singapore university:**

**A local degree helps**, both because employers are more comfortable and because it opens the campus recruiting channels — fairs, on-campus interviews, university portals — that are effectively closed to overseas candidates.

**Local hiring requirements come first.** Employers have obligations to consider local candidates, and this shapes intake composition at structured graduate programmes. It is not a bar, and it does mean the general management associate intakes are harder to enter than technical and specialist roles.

**Apply to technical and specialist positions preferentially** if you need a pass. The business case is easier and the competition is thinner.

**Use the internship route.** A local internship resolves both of the employer's doubts — capability and paperwork — before the full-time decision, and the [internship guide](/blog/singapore-internship-guide) covers the authorisation rules for students.

**If you are applying from overseas:**

**Apply to the Singapore entity directly**, not through a global portal that may route you elsewhere.

**Referrals do most of the work**, since the campus channel is unavailable to you. Alumni from your institution now based in Singapore are the highest-yield group.

**Have a specific answer to "why Singapore".** Asked of every overseas applicant, and a vague one reads poorly.

**Be precise about your position.** "I'd need an Employment Pass, and as a fresh graduate I'd fall under the lower qualifying threshold" is a candidate who has done the homework. Vagueness here creates work for the recruiter, and recruiters with two hundred applications do not do work.

**Expect a longer process** and plan your other options accordingly.

## The passes that matter, and how they differ

Three routes come up for graduates, and confusing them wastes applications.

**Employment Pass.** The main route for graduates entering professional, managerial and executive roles. Applied for by the employer. Subject to a qualifying salary that varies by age and sector, and to a points-based assessment. There is no quota on Employment Passes in the way there is for other categories, but the assessment includes employer-side factors that effectively constrain smaller firms.

**S Pass.** Covers mid-skilled roles at a lower salary threshold, and — importantly — is subject to employer quotas and a levy. Because the quota is a share of the employer's local workforce, a firm can be entirely willing and still have no room. Relevant to some technical and associate-level graduate positions.

**Work Permit.** Covers specific sectors and occupations and is not generally the route for degree-holding graduates in professional roles.

**Two structural points worth internalising:**

**Qualifying salary rises with age.** This is the piece of good news for graduates. A fresh graduate faces the lowest threshold in the schedule, and the figure climbs with each year of age thereafter. It means the pass question is easier for you now than it will ever be again, which is an argument for entering the market earlier rather than after several years elsewhere.

**You cannot hold a pass independently of a job.** There is no pass you apply for yourself and then use to look for work. The employer applies, for a specific role, at a specific salary. Which is why the whole question is really about employer selection rather than personal eligibility, and why filtering your target list early is worth more than any amount of individual preparation.

## What to do when the answer is no

It happens, and how you respond determines whether it costs you one application or a season.

**Ask whether it is the role or the organisation.** Some employers sponsor in technology and specialist functions and not in general graduate intakes. "Are there other functions where the position is different?" is a fair question and the answer is sometimes yes.

**Ask whether an internship is possible instead.** Attachment and internship arrangements sit under different rules from full employment, and an employer who cannot hire you full-time may be able to host you — which then changes the conversation entirely twelve months later. The [internship guide](/blog/singapore-internship-guide) covers the distinction.

**Do not press.** The constraint is usually structural — a ratio, a quota, an assessment score they cannot move — rather than a decision about you. Pressing does not change arithmetic and does change how you are remembered.

**Re-weight the list.** Two or three no's from a similar type of employer is information about the segment, not about you. Move toward larger organisations, regional headquarters, and functions where scarce skills make the case easier.

**Consider the timeline.** Qualifying salary rises with age, which means the threshold you face now is the lowest it will ever be. If Singapore is a genuine goal, entering the market earlier is materially easier than arriving after several years elsewhere — one of the few places where "apply now rather than later" is structurally rather than motivationally true.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming one salary threshold applies to everyone.** It rises with age, and finance has its own.
- **Believing you can hold a pass independently.** The employer applies; the pass is tied to the job.
- **Ignoring the points framework.** It explains why identical candidates get different outcomes at different firms.
- **Treating small employers as equally likely to sponsor.** Quotas and levies fall harder on them.
- **Relying on figures from articles.** Thresholds change; MOM is the authority.
- **Leaving pass questions to offer stage.** By then both sides have spent weeks on a process that may not be viable.

## Verify before you rely on any of this

The most important instruction on this page, and the one most often ignored.

**Thresholds are raised periodically.** Qualifying salary figures have been revised upward several times in recent years, sometimes with sector-specific variations. A number quoted in an article — including this one — may be out of date by the time you read it.

**The points-based assessment has changed the picture.** Salary alone is no longer sufficient; a candidate must also pass a points assessment that scores factors including qualifications, the employer's local workforce ratio, and the employer's sector diversity. Several of these are entirely outside your control, which is precisely why employer choice matters more than personal qualification.

**Rules for specific occupations and sectors vary.** Shortage lists and sector-specific arrangements exist and change.

**Where to check.** The Ministry of Manpower publishes current thresholds, the assessment framework and eligibility criteria. That is the authority, it is public, and it takes ten minutes to read the relevant page.

**What not to rely on.** Forum posts, an article without a date, or a friend's experience from two years ago. The rules genuinely have changed in that period, and confident out-of-date advice is the most common source of wasted applications.

**Your employer applies, not you.** This is worth restating because candidates sometimes try to establish eligibility independently. You cannot hold a pass without a job, the application is made by the employer, and the assessment includes factors about them as well as about you. Which means the practical question is never "do I qualify" in the abstract — it is "does this employer, hiring me for this role at this salary, qualify."

## The points-based assessment, and why it changes your strategy

The addition that most candidates have not caught up with, and it shifts where your effort belongs.

**What it is.** Alongside meeting the salary threshold, an application must pass a points-based assessment. Points come from a mix of factors — some about the individual, some about the employer, some about the role.

**Individual factors** include salary relative to local benchmarks for the occupation, and qualifications. These are partly within your influence.

**Employer factors** include the proportion of local employees in the firm's professional workforce relative to its sector, and the firm's contribution to broader workforce objectives. **These are entirely outside your control**, and they are frequently decisive.

**Role factors** include whether the occupation appears on shortage lists, which change over time.

**The strategic consequence is large.** Under a pure salary threshold, a strong candidate could reason "I meet the number, so I am eligible." Under a points system that scores the employer, the same candidate can be unhirable at one firm and straightforward at another for the identical role and salary.

**Which means:**

**Employer selection is now the dominant variable.** A large multinational with a substantial local workforce scores differently from a twenty-person startup, regardless of how much either wants you.

**Asking early is more important than ever.** "Does this role consider Employment Pass candidates?" is a question the employer can answer against their own position; you cannot work it out from outside.

**Shortage-list skills are worth knowing about.** Where your field appears, the calculation eases materially.

**And personal over-qualification does not rescue a weak employer position.**

Candidates sometimes respond to this by adding credentials. It is the wrong lever — the constraint is usually on the other side of the table.

**Verify the current framework** with the Ministry of Manpower before making decisions on it. The factors, weightings and thresholds are reviewed, and this is precisely the kind of detail that goes stale in articles.

## A short checklist

Half an hour, before you send a single application, and it changes where the whole season goes.

- [ ] **Read the current Ministry of Manpower guidance** on qualifying salary and the points assessment. Not a summary — the source.
- [ ] **Write down your own position in one sentence**, with the pass type and your understanding of the threshold that applies to a fresh graduate.
- [ ] **Build the target list with a sponsorship column.** Large multinationals, regional headquarters, banks, and organisations that hire internationally as routine.
- [ ] **Mark the small firms and startups separately.** Not excluded — just understood as lower-probability, so you spend applications there deliberately rather than by accident.
- [ ] **Check whether your field appears on any shortage list**, since it materially changes the calculation.
- [ ] **Draft the one-sentence answer** for the recruiter call and say it until it is boring.
- [ ] **Note your exact availability date**, because it gets asked in the first conversation.

The purpose of all of it is a single thing: to stop you spending an autumn applying to employers who were never going to be able to hire you, which is how most candidates in this position lose a cycle.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I apply for an Employment Pass myself?

No. The employer applies on your behalf, and the pass is tied to that employment. Changing employer requires a new application.

### What is the qualifying salary for a fresh graduate?

It is the lowest point on an age-linked scale, and the figure is revised periodically. Check the Ministry of Manpower's current published threshold rather than a secondary source — including this one.

### Is the S Pass easier to get than an EP?

The salary threshold is lower, but it is quota-limited and carries a levy, so many employers avoid it. Easier on paper, often harder in practice.

### Does the points assessment mean I can be rejected despite meeting the salary threshold?

Yes. Meeting the salary floor makes you eligible; the points assessment is a separate hurdle, and several of its criteria relate to the employer rather than to you.

### Do I need a job offer before moving to Singapore?

For a pass, yes — it is employer-sponsored. Some graduates of local institutions have a window to remain and search after completing study; confirm the current rules for your situation.

### How long does a pass application take?

Weeks rather than days in the ordinary case, longer if documents need verification. Build it into your expected start date rather than assuming you can begin immediately after accepting.
