## Key Takeaways

- Employers ask two separate questions: are you authorised to work now, and will you need sponsorship in future. They have different answers.
- Answer both precisely with dates and durations. Vagueness reads as risk far more than a "yes, eventually" does.
- Never misrepresent your status. It is verified at offer stage and discovering it late ends the process.
- Do not raise sponsorship in the first thirty seconds of an interview. Answer when asked, clearly and briefly, then return to the role.
- Knowing which employers sponsor before you apply saves more time than any answer technique.

## The two questions, and why the distinction matters

Application forms almost always ask two things:

**"Are you legally authorised to work in the United States?"**

If you are on OPT or CPT with valid authorisation, the answer is **yes**. Students frequently answer no here because they think it means permanently, and screen themselves out of jobs they were eligible for.

**"Will you now or in the future require sponsorship for employment visa status?"**

If your authorisation expires and you would need H-1B afterwards, the answer is **yes**. Answering no because you have two years of OPT left is a misrepresentation, and it surfaces later.

These are separate questions with separate answers. The common failure is treating them as one and getting both wrong — "no" to the first out of caution, or "no" to the second out of hope.

## What a strong verbal answer sounds like

When it comes up in conversation, the goal is to sound like someone who has read their own paperwork.

> "Yes — I'm authorised to work now on OPT, which runs through August 2028 with the STEM extension. After that I'd need sponsorship."

That is three facts in one sentence: authorised now, for how long, and what happens after. It takes eight seconds and it closes the topic.

Compare with what students often say:

> "Um, I think I have OPT, and then maybe there's an extension, so possibly three years? I'd have to check."

Identical legal position. Completely different impression. The first sounds like someone who manages their own affairs; the second creates work for the recruiter and a suspicion that the paperwork might go wrong.

**Know your dates before the interview.** Your authorisation start and end, whether your degree is STEM-designated, and what the extension would give you. Write it on one line and rehearse it.

## When to raise it yourself

The default is: **answer when asked, do not volunteer it early.**

Two exceptions where raising it yourself is right:

**Late in the process, if nobody has mentioned it.** Before a final round or an offer conversation, confirming that sponsorship is understood protects you from a withdrawn offer. "I want to make sure we're aligned — I'll need sponsorship after August 2028. Is that something the team supports?"

**When the recruiter asks what questions you have, in a first screen.** If sponsorship is a dealbreaker for them, finding out in week one is better than week six.

What not to do is open with it. Leading an interview with your visa status frames you as an immigration case rather than a candidate. Let them see the work first.

## What not to say

**Do not say you will not need sponsorship if you will.** This is the one genuinely damaging mistake. It is checked at offer stage, and being caught turns a manageable constraint into a trust problem.

**Do not say "I have three years so it's not an issue."** It is an issue eventually and the employer knows it. Minimising it reads as either naive or evasive. Better: acknowledge it and show you understand the mechanics.

**Do not over-explain.** A two-minute account of visa categories signals anxiety. State the facts and stop.

**Do not apologise.** Sponsorship is a normal administrative process that thousands of companies complete every year.

## When the answer is no

Some employers do not sponsor, and some cannot for a specific role — federal contracts and clearance-required positions have genuine legal constraints.

If you learn this early, thank them and move on. Arguing does not work; the decision usually sits with legal or finance, not the interviewer.

The better strategy is filtering before you apply. Many companies state their position on careers pages. For STEM OPT specifically, the employer must be enrolled in E-Verify, which quietly excludes many small firms — worth checking before you invest in an application. The [mechanics of OPT and the STEM extension](/blog/usa-opt-cpt-explained-students) determine which employers are viable for you at all.

## How this fits the wider search

Sponsorship makes you a slower hire, so being early matters more for you than for domestic classmates. The [autumn recruiting window](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline) is where structured roles are decided, and being in the pipeline in September rather than November is worth more than any interview technique.

It also means volume with targeting. Your viable employer pool is smaller, so you need more applications in flight — but a generic resume sent widely produces rejections at scale rather than interviews. Keeping documents [tailored to each posting](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) while raising volume is the balance that works.

Your resume should carry none of this. Visa status is not a screening credential and does not belong on a document being machine-read; it takes space from evidence. Keep the [US resume format](/blog/usa-resume-format-for-students) clean and let the question come up in conversation.

For roles requiring H-1B eventually, understanding [how the lottery and cap-exempt employers work](/blog/usa-h1b-lottery-new-grads) lets you have a substantive conversation rather than a nervous one — and cap-exempt employers remove the lottery from the equation entirely.

## Common Mistakes

- **Answering "no" to "are you authorised to work now"** when you hold valid OPT. This screens you out unnecessarily.
- **Answering "no" to the future sponsorship question** because your authorisation has time left. It is a misrepresentation.
- **Not knowing your own dates.** The most avoidable weakness in the entire conversation.
- **Raising it in the first minute.** It reframes you as a paperwork problem before they have seen your work.
- **Applying to employers who never sponsor.** Wasted applications that could have gone to viable ones.
- **Assuming a large company automatically sponsors.** Many do not for every role, particularly government-adjacent work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I mention sponsorship on my resume?

No. It is not a credential and the resume is being screened by software against the posting. Answer the question on the application form, and in conversation when asked.

### What if the application form only asks one question?

Answer what is asked, precisely. If the only question is about future sponsorship and you will need it, answer yes. Some forms combine them badly — if it is genuinely ambiguous, answer in the way that is truthful about your future need.

### Can I ask whether a company sponsors before applying?

Yes, and it saves time on both sides. A short message to a recruiter asking whether the role supports sponsorship is a normal question and is usually answered directly.

### Does saying I need sponsorship end my chances?

At companies that do not sponsor, yes — but that outcome was fixed before you answered. At companies that do, it is an administrative fact they have handled before. The answer changes nothing except how competent you appear.

### What if my status changes during the process?

Tell them. An extension approved, or a delay, is normal and easily absorbed if communicated. Discovered late without warning, it damages trust.

### Is it different for internships?

Usually simpler, because CPT covers the internship without employer sponsorship. The complication arrives at conversion to full-time, which is when the future-sponsorship question matters — worth raising before you accept, not after.
