## Key Takeaways

- A posting is usually written by three or four people - hiring manager, HR, a reused template, sometimes legal - and each contributes different weight.
- The title and first paragraph are the hiring manager's; the long bullet list is often accumulated and unedited.
- Requirements framed as "must have" with specific detail are real; vague categories near the bottom rarely are.
- Clustered similar tools almost always mean "one of these," not all of them.
- Five minutes of reading this way saves an hour of mis-targeted tailoring.

Job postings read like unified documents written by one person. They almost never are — and once you can see the seams, you can tell which requirements decide the shortlist and which are decoration.

## Who Writes Which Part

**The hiring manager writes the title and the opening paragraph.** This is the part closest to what they actually need, because it is the part they care about and usually draft themselves. It is the highest-signal text in the posting.

**HR or a recruiter writes the structure and the boilerplate.** Section headings, the culture paragraph, benefits, the equal-opportunity statement. Necessary, and irrelevant to whether you are qualified.

**The bullet list is usually inherited and extended.** Most requisitions start from a similar prior role. Each contributor adds items; almost nobody removes them. This is why lists reach twenty items that no single person expects.

**Legal or compliance adds the constraints.** Work authorisation, licensing, background-check requirements. These are genuinely non-negotiable, unlike much of the bullet list.

## What This Tells You

**Weight the top of the posting far more than the bottom.** A skill in the title and opening paragraph is central. The same skill appearing only as bullet fourteen is likely inherited.

**Repetition is the strongest signal.** If something appears in the title, the summary and the bullets, three different contributors all thought it mattered. That is the job.

**Specificity beats generality.** "Snowflake" is a real, checkable requirement someone chose deliberately. "Familiarity with modern data tooling" is a category hedge covering whatever the candidate happens to know.

**Length signals process, not expectation.** A twenty-item list is evidence of an unedited requisition, not evidence that they expect twenty matches.

## The Patterns Worth Recognising

**The cluster.** Several similar tools listed together — "Tableau, Power BI, or Looker," "AWS, Azure, GCP." This almost always means one is sufficient. Listing all three because you saw them flagged is transparent padding.

**The wish list tail.** The last four or five bullets are frequently "nice to haves" that migrated into the main list. They read as requirements and function as preferences.

**The duplicated soft skill.** "Communication," "stakeholder management," "cross-functional collaboration" and "presentation skills" as four separate bullets is one requirement written four times by different contributors.

**The impossible combination.** Requirements that rarely co-occur in one person — deep ML research plus production infrastructure plus stakeholder-facing consulting. Usually two roles merged, or an aspirational composite. Apply if you have a strong subset.

**The copied template.** Bullets referencing tools or processes that do not match the rest of the posting, or a seniority level inconsistent with the title. Leftovers from the role the requisition was cloned from.

## Working Example

> **Senior Analytics Engineer — Reporting Platform**
>
> We're looking for a senior analytics engineer to own our dbt models and reporting layer as we scale from 40 to 200 tables...
>
> Requirements: dbt, SQL, Snowflake, Python, Looker, Tableau, Power BI, Airflow, Git, data modelling, stakeholder management, communication, cross-functional collaboration, Agile, Jira, bachelor's degree, fast-paced environment, attention to detail.

Reading it by source:

- **Title and opening paragraph:** dbt, SQL, reporting layer, scale. This is the job.
- **Cluster:** Looker / Tableau / Power BI — one of these is fine.
- **Duplicated soft skill:** stakeholder management, communication, cross-functional collaboration — one requirement, three phrasings.
- **Process tail:** Agile, Jira, attention to detail, fast-paced — noise.
- **Real constraint:** bachelor's degree, if stated firmly.

Eighteen items reduce to four that matter: **dbt, SQL, Snowflake, and one BI tool.** Tailor to those and you have addressed the posting properly.

## What to Do With the Reading

**If you match the top-signal items,** apply and tailor to them specifically. Your summary and first bullets should speak to the title and opening paragraph, not the bullet list.

**If you match the bullet list but not the opening paragraph,** reconsider. You match the accumulated preferences and miss the actual job.

**If you miss one top-signal item,** decide whether it is adjacent to something you have. If yes, apply and make the adjacency explicit. If it is genuinely the whole role, move on.

**If the posting is entirely vague,** there is little to tailor to. Apply lightly and spend your effort on clearer postings — vagueness often signals an unclear internal brief, which tends to produce a difficult process.

Some postings are full of internal tool names and team jargon; [a JD written for the person already doing the job](/blog/jd-written-for-an-insider) shows how to decode them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I still apply if I miss several listed requirements?**
Depends which. Missing several tail-end items is normal. Missing what is in the title and opening paragraph is not.

**How do I tell a real "must have" from a wish?**
Position, repetition and specificity. Something in the title, repeated, and named precisely is real. Something appearing once in the bullet tail usually is not.

**Do recruiters expect candidates to match everything?**
No. Recruiters know their own postings are inflated, and shortlists routinely include people missing several listed items.

**What about postings that list years of experience?**
Treat it as a band rather than a threshold. One or two years short with strong relevant evidence is often fine; five years short rarely is.

**Is a very long requirements list a red flag?**
Not usually about the company — more often it reflects an unedited requisition process. It is a signal about how to read the list, not whether to apply.
