## Key Takeaways

- Licences are issued by individual states, not nationally, so moving states can mean re-applying rather than transferring.
- "Reciprocity" usually means a faster route, not an automatic one — you still apply, pay, and wait.
- Start the licensure process before the job search, because it is routinely the longest item on the path.
- Foreign-trained professionals face an extra credential-evaluation step that can add months.
- Put your licence number and status on your resume — it is a screening field, not a detail.

If you work in a regulated profession, licensure is usually the real constraint on your US job search — not your resume, not interviews, and not the visa. A nurse or engineer with the right experience can still be unable to start for six months because the state board has not issued a licence.

The mistake that costs most time is treating it as paperwork to sort out after landing a job. It runs the other way: employers generally need you licensed, or clearly licensable on a known timeline, before they will make an offer.

This guide covers how the system works and how to plan around it.

## Why it is state by state

The US regulates professions at state level. There is no national nursing licence or national engineering licence. Each state has its own board, its own requirements, its own fees and its own processing times.

Practically this means:

- Being licensed in Texas does not let you work in California
- Requirements genuinely differ — supervised hours, exams, continuing education
- Moving states mid-career means a new application
- Some states are fast; some take many months

Some professions have compacts or model agreements that ease movement, but even those have conditions.

## Common licensed professions

**Nursing.** The Nurse Licensure Compact allows nurses whose primary residence is in a participating state to practise in other participating states. Not all states participate, and the residency condition matters — the compact follows where you live, not where you were licensed.

**Engineering.** The PE (Professional Engineer) licence, required for work involving public safety and for signing off drawings. Route is typically an accredited degree, the FE exam, supervised experience, then the PE exam. Comity between states exists but is an application, not a transfer.

**Accounting.** The CPA is state-issued. Education requirements — commonly around 150 semester hours — plus the Uniform CPA Exam plus experience. Mobility provisions let CPAs practise across state lines in many circumstances, with conditions.

**Teaching.** State certification, with interstate agreements that reduce but rarely eliminate additional requirements. Frequently the slowest of the group.

**Law.** Bar admission is state by state and one of the most restrictive. Some states allow admission on motion for experienced attorneys; others require the bar exam regardless.

**Healthcare more broadly** — physicians, pharmacists, physical therapists, social workers, counsellors — all state-licensed with their own boards.

## What reciprocity actually means

The word causes more confusion than any other in this area.

**Reciprocity, endorsement, or comity** generally means: your existing licence and experience may substitute for some requirements, so the state will consider you without making you start over.

**It does not mean automatic.** You still submit an application, pay the fee, provide verification from your current board, and wait. Some states add jurisprudence exams on their own law, or additional coursework.

**Check the specific state board.** Not a forum, not a recruiter, not a general article. The board publishes its requirements and they are authoritative.

## Foreign-trained professionals

An extra layer, and the timeline grows.

**Credential evaluation.** A recognised agency assesses whether your foreign qualification is equivalent to the US requirement. This takes weeks to months and requires transcripts, sometimes translated and sometimes sent directly by your institution.

**English proficiency.** Frequently required, even for fluent speakers, as a formal test.

**Additional exams.** Nurses typically need NCLEX; physicians face a longer route including USMLE and residency requirements.

**Supervised practice.** Some professions require a period of US-based supervised work.

**Immigration runs in parallel.** Licensure and visa are separate processes with separate timelines, and one does not wait for the other — see [how to get a job in the USA as an international candidate](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-get-a-job-in-usa-international).

**Start early.** For foreign-trained professionals, licensure is almost always the longest item, frequently longer than the visa.

## Putting it on your resume

Licensure is a screening field. Recruiters filter on it and hiring managers check it.

**If licensed in the target state:**

> RN, California Board of Registered Nursing — Licence #123456, active through 06/2028

**If licensed elsewhere and moving:**

> RN, Texas (active) — California endorsement application submitted 03/2026, expected 08/2026

That second version is the important one. It converts "not licensed here" into "licensed, with a date" and lets an employer plan around you rather than screen you out.

**If in progress:** say so with the stage. "FE passed 2024; PE exam scheduled October 2026" is far better than silence.

## Planning a move

**Work backwards from a start date.** Find the board's stated processing time, add a margin, and begin that far ahead.

**Apply before you have the job** where the state allows it. Many do.

**Ask employers about support.** Some cover licensure fees, some help with the paperwork, and healthcare systems in particular often have staff dedicated to it. It is a fair question at offer stage, alongside the items in [the US relocation package guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/us-relocation-package-guide).

**Keep your existing licence active** while you transition. Letting it lapse can complicate the endorsement route.

**Track continuing education.** Most licences require CE hours to renew, and requirements differ by state.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating licensure as post-offer paperwork.** Employers generally need you licensed or credibly licensable before offering, so leaving it until afterwards stalls the start date by months.

**Assuming reciprocity is automatic.** It is a faster application route, not a transfer — you still apply, pay, and wait, and some states add jurisprudence exams.

**Relying on forums instead of the board.** Requirements change and vary; the state board's own published requirements are the only authoritative source.

**Letting the current licence lapse during a move.** An active licence is usually what makes the endorsement route available, and reinstating a lapsed one is slower.

**Omitting licence status from the resume.** It is a screening field, and silence gets read as "not licensed" rather than "in progress".

**Not stating an expected date when in progress.** "Application submitted, expected August" lets an employer plan around you; leaving it vague does not.

**Starting credential evaluation late.** For foreign-trained professionals this is frequently the single longest step, often longer than the visa process.

**Forgetting continuing education requirements.** Renewal depends on CE hours that differ by state, and discovering a shortfall at renewal is expensive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I work in another state with my current licence?

Generally not without action. Some professions have compacts or mobility provisions with conditions, but the default is that you apply to the new state's board.

### What does reciprocity actually mean?

That your existing licence and experience can substitute for some requirements. You still submit an application, pay a fee, and wait — and some states add their own exam.

### How long does licensure take?

Weeks to many months depending on the profession and state. Check the specific board's published processing times and add margin.

### Do employers help with licensure?

Some do, particularly healthcare systems, which often have dedicated staff and cover fees. It is a reasonable question to ask at offer stage.

### What if I trained outside the US?

Expect a credential evaluation, possibly an English test, and possibly additional exams or supervised practice. Start this early — it is usually the longest part of the path.

### Should licensure be on my resume?

Yes, with the number, the state, and the status. If it is in progress, say which stage and give an expected date.

### Can I apply for jobs before I am licensed?

Often yes, if you can show a credible timeline. Employers plan around known dates far more readily than around uncertainty.

## Make This Practical

Start the licence application before the job search, not after. Find your target state's board, read its published requirements and processing times directly rather than relying on forums, and work backwards from the start date you want with a margin added.

Then make your status visible. Put the licence number, state, and expiry on your resume if you hold one — and if you are mid-process, state the stage and an expected date so employers can plan around you instead of screening you out.

Finally, if you trained outside the US, begin credential evaluation immediately, since it is routinely longer than the visa process and the two run in parallel rather than in sequence. Keep your existing licence active throughout, and ask at offer stage whether the employer supports licensure costs.
