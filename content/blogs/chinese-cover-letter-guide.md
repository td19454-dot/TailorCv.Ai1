## Key Takeaways

- A Chinese cover letter (求职信, *qiúzhíxìn*) is more formal and more deferential in tone than a Western one - confident is fine, self-promotional is not.
- Foreign applicants must answer three questions early: your visa situation, your Mandarin level, and whether you are already in China.
- Send it in the language of the job posting, and in the email body rather than only as an attachment.
- Machine-translating an English cover letter produces phrasing that undercuts otherwise strong content.
- Keep it to one page - roughly 250 to 350 words - with specifics rather than adjectives.

Cover letters have declined in importance across much of the Western hiring world. In China they still carry weight, and for a foreign applicant they carry more weight than the resume in one specific respect: they are where you answer the questions an employer would otherwise have to guess at.

A Chinese employer looking at a foreign resume is thinking about sponsorship, language, and whether you will still be there in two years. If your cover letter does not address those, the reader has to assume, and assumptions tend not to run in your favour.

This guide covers structure, tone, the two language versions, and what to say when you are applying from abroad.

## How the Tone Differs

This is the part that trips up Western applicants most, and it is subtle rather than dramatic.

**Western cover letters sell.** They open with a hook, lead with achievement, and project confidence. "I transformed the department's performance" is normal phrasing.

**Chinese cover letters are more measured.** They open with respect, present qualifications factually, and express interest in contributing rather than in transforming. Confidence comes through evidence rather than adjectives.

The practical translation: keep your achievements and numbers exactly as they are, but change the framing around them. Instead of "I am the ideal candidate for this role," write "My three years in supply chain analytics align closely with what this role requires." Same claim, different register.

Two more conventions worth knowing:

**Formality is higher throughout.** Address the reader properly. Use the company's full name. Avoid contractions and casual phrasing in the Chinese version particularly.

**Expressing genuine interest in the company and in China matters.** Employers weigh whether a foreign hire will stay. Specific, informed interest is read as a stability signal, not as flattery.

## The Structure

### Opening

State the role you are applying for and where you saw it. If you have a referral, name them here - referrals carry substantial weight, and the general approach in [how to ask for a referral](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/networking-messages-for-referrals) applies with WeChat as the channel.

### Why you fit

Two or three sentences with concrete evidence. Pick the specific requirements from the posting and match each with something real. This is the same evidence discipline as [how to tailor your resume for every job](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-every-job) - the letter should not repeat the resume, it should explain it.

### Your practical situation (foreign applicants)

Three facts, stated plainly:

- **Visa status** - whether you need sponsorship, already hold a valid permit, or are transferring one
- **Mandarin level** - HSK level if you have one, or a functional description
- **Location** - whether you are in China now, and if not, when you could relocate

One short paragraph. Do not bury it, and do not leave it out hoping it will not come up.

### Why this company, and why China

Two sentences of specific interest. Reference something real about the company. Generic enthusiasm is transparent.

### Close

Thank them, state your availability for interview, and confirm your contact details - including your WeChat ID.

## English or Chinese?

**Send Chinese** for domestic companies, state-owned enterprises, and any posting written in Chinese.

**Send English** for multinationals, foreign-invested companies, and English-language postings.

**If you genuinely cannot tell,** send both - Chinese first, English below, in the same email body.

### A warning about translation

Do not machine-translate your English letter and send the output. Direct translation of Western cover letter phrasing produces exactly the self-promotional register that reads wrong, and the errors cluster in job titles and achievement claims - the parts that matter most.

If your Mandarin is not strong enough to write it, one of two approaches works: write a shorter, simpler Chinese letter you can actually control, or write in English and be straightforward about your language level. A short, correct Chinese letter is better received than a long, awkward one.

## Template: English Version

> Dear Hiring Manager,
>
> I am writing to apply for the Supply Chain Analyst position at [Company], which I saw advertised on [platform].
>
> I have four years of experience in supply chain analytics, most recently at [Company], where I managed procurement data across six regional suppliers and reduced order lead time by 22% through a revised forecasting model. Your posting emphasises supplier performance reporting, which was the core of my last two years.
>
> Regarding my situation: I currently hold a valid work permit sponsored by my present employer in Shanghai and would require a transfer. My Mandarin is at HSK 4 - sufficient for daily work communication, and improving. I am based in Shanghai and available at short notice.
>
> I have followed [Company]'s expansion into Southeast Asian sourcing with interest, and the combination of scale and regional complexity is exactly the environment I want to work in next.
>
> Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss the role. I can be reached at [email], [phone], or on WeChat at [ID].
>
> Yours sincerely,
> [Name]

Notice what it does: names the role and source, gives one quantified proof matched to the posting, resolves visa and language in three sentences, shows specific company knowledge, and closes with WeChat included.

## Template: Structure for the Chinese Version

If you are writing in Chinese, follow this shape:

1. **尊敬的 [公司名] 人力资源部:** - formal salutation
2. **Opening line** stating the position and where you saw it
3. **Background paragraph** - education and experience, factually presented
4. **Evidence paragraph** - specific achievements with numbers
5. **Practical paragraph** - visa status, HSK level, current location
6. **Interest paragraph** - why this company, why China
7. **Closing** - 此致敬礼 and your contact details

Keep sentences shorter than you would in English. Chinese business writing rewards clarity over elaboration.

## Common Mistakes

**Repeating the resume.** The letter explains and connects; it does not list.

**Skipping the visa question.** The employer will ask anyway. Answering first saves a round trip and reads as organised.

**Overstating Mandarin.** It will be tested in the first call. "Conversational" that turns out to mean "some phrases" damages trust immediately.

**Generic company praise.** "A leader in the industry" applies to everyone and signals you did not look.

**Wrong character set.** Simplified for the mainland; traditional is for Hong Kong and Taiwan.

**Attachment only.** Many recruiters read on mobile in a messaging app. Put the letter in the email body, and attach the resume as PDF.

**Excessive length.** One page. If it runs to two, you are explaining your resume rather than supplementing it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do Chinese employers actually read cover letters?

More often than employers in many Western markets, and especially for foreign applicants, where the letter answers questions the resume cannot. For domestic candidates applying through platforms it matters less, since much of the initial screening happens on structured profile fields.

### Should I write it in Chinese if my Chinese is weak?

A short, simple, correct Chinese letter is usually better received than a long awkward one, and better than English for a domestic employer. Do not machine-translate. If you cannot produce something clean, write in English and state your level honestly.

### How long should it be?

One page, roughly 250 to 350 words. Shorter than most Western guidance suggests.

### Do I need to include a photo in the cover letter?

No. The photo goes on the resume, as covered in the [Chinese resume format guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/chinese-resume-format-guide-2026).

### Should I mention salary expectations?

Only if the posting asks. If it does, give a range and note it is negotiable. Bear in mind that for foreign hires the salary figure also interacts with work permit eligibility, as explained in the [China work visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/china-work-visa-z-visa-guide).

### What if I am applying from outside China?

Say so, and give a realistic relocation timeline. Being upfront about needing sponsorship is better than a surprise later. Your case is stronger if you can show you understand the process - see [how to get a job in China as a foreigner](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-get-a-job-in-china-foreigner).

Write the practical paragraph first - visa, Mandarin level, location - because it is the part most applicants avoid and the part employers most want. Three sentences, no hedging.
