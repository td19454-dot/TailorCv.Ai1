## Key Takeaways

- A low score on a well-written resume almost always comes from one of three causes: the parser cannot read parts of it, your vocabulary differs from the posting's, or your evidence sits too far down the page.
- Design quality and score quality are unrelated - the most attractive resumes often parse worst.
- You can identify which cause applies in about two minutes using a copy-paste test and a vocabulary comparison.
- Fixing a parsing problem usually takes one edit; fixing a vocabulary problem takes three or four.
- A low score on a genuinely mismatched job is correct information, not a problem to fix.

A resume that reads well to you and scores badly in a scanner is not a contradiction. You and the scanner are evaluating different things. You are reading meaning; it is matching text. When those two diverge, the score drops while the document still looks fine on your screen.

There are only three common reasons for that divergence, and they need different fixes.

## Cause 1: The Parser Cannot Read Part of Your Resume

This is the most common cause and the easiest to miss, because nothing looks wrong. Text inside a text box, a table cell, a header or a footer may render perfectly in your PDF viewer and arrive as nothing on the other side.

The two-column layout is the usual culprit. A parser reading left-to-right across a two-column page can interleave the columns into nonsense - your skills list shuffled into your job titles - or drop the narrow column entirely.

**The copy-paste test.** Open your PDF, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor. What you see is roughly what the parser sees.

Look for:

- Sections that vanished entirely
- Your contact details missing (common when they sit in a header)
- Columns interleaved into scrambled lines
- Bullet characters becoming odd symbols
- Dates separated from the jobs they belong to

If your skills section is absent from that paste, no amount of keyword work will raise your score. The scanner never saw those words.

## Cause 2: You and the Posting Use Different Words for the Same Thing

Your resume says "client relationship management." The posting says "stakeholder management." You have done the work. The scanner does not credit it, because it is matching against the posting's vocabulary, not assessing your career.

This is not about adding skills you lack. It is about naming skills you have in the language the reader uses.

> **Your resume:** Managed relationships with 12 enterprise clients, resolving escalations and running quarterly reviews.
>
> **The posting asks for:** stakeholder management, account governance, QBRs
>
> **Rewritten:** Owned stakeholder management for 12 enterprise accounts, running quarterly business reviews (QBRs) and resolving escalations.

Same job, same truth, same achievements. The second version is legible to a system matching against that posting.

Two cautions. First, only do this where the claim is genuinely true - if you have never run a QBR, do not write QBR. Second, do not do it mechanically; a resume that reads like the job description pasted back at the employer will pass the scanner and lose the human.

## Cause 3: Your Evidence Exists but Sits Too Low

Some systems weight the top of the document more heavily, and every human reader does. If the skill the posting cares about most appears once, in the fourth bullet of your third job, it is technically present and practically invisible.

The fix is placement, not addition. Move the relevant evidence up: into your summary, into your skills section, into the first two bullets of your most recent role.

This is also the cheapest fix. You are not writing anything new - you are reordering what is already there for the specific job you are applying to.

## Working Out Which One You Have

In order, because the first is the most common and the cheapest to check:

**1. Run the copy-paste test.** If content is missing or scrambled, that is your problem. Stop here and fix the layout - switch to a single-column template, move contact details out of the header, replace tables with plain lines.

**2. Compare vocabulary.** Put the posting beside your resume. Take its five most-repeated terms and check whether those exact words appear on your resume. If you do the work but use different words, rewrite those lines.

**3. Check placement.** For the two or three things the posting cares about most, ask how far down the page the evidence sits. If it is past the first half, move it up.

If none of the three applies and the score is still low, the honest answer is usually that the match is genuinely weak. That is useful information - it tells you to spend your time on better-fitting postings rather than on this document.

## What This Is Not

It is not a reason to strip your resume of all formatting. A plain single-column resume in a standard font parses reliably and still looks professional. You are removing structural traps, not design.

It is not a reason to chase a perfect score. Past a reasonable threshold, additional keyword work produces diminishing returns and starts to damage readability for the human who reads next.

And it is not a reason to add skills you do not have. A scanner can be satisfied by a word. An interview cannot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**My resume was designed by a professional and still scores badly. Why?**
Design quality and parse quality are unrelated, and often inversely correlated. Designers optimise for visual impact, which means columns, text boxes and custom bullets - exactly the structures that parse worst.

**Does a low score mean a human never sees my resume?**
Not usually. Most systems rank rather than reject outright, so a low-scoring resume tends to sit further down a long list rather than being deleted. The practical effect is similar on a posting with hundreds of applicants.

**Should I use the exact words from the posting even if they sound clumsy?**
Use them where they are accurate and can be written naturally. If a phrase cannot be written without sounding forced, the cost to the human reader is usually greater than the gain with the scanner.

**Will converting my resume to .docx fix a low score?**
Only if your problem was a PDF that exported as an image or had unselectable text. If the copy-paste test returns clean text, the file format is not your issue.

**How much should my score move after fixing these?**
A parsing fix often moves it substantially, because whole sections become visible for the first time. A vocabulary fix moves it moderately. A placement fix moves it least with scanners but most with human readers.
