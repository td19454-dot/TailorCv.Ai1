## Key Takeaways

- A broken link or dead deployment is worse than having no portfolio, because you invited the check and it failed.
- Reviewers spend ninety seconds, so anything requiring exploration to understand is lost.
- Showing output without reasoning is the most common substantive failure.
- Too many projects is a worse problem than too few, because it dilutes your strongest work.
- Slow loading and broken mobile layouts eliminate you before content is assessed.

Portfolios rarely fail because of how they look. They fail because a link does not work, because a reviewer cannot tell what a project was for, or because there are twelve projects and the good one is fourth.

These are all fixable in an afternoon, and most people never audit for them.

This guide is the audit.

## Things that eliminate you before content

**A dead link.** The single worst failure. You put a URL on your resume, a reviewer clicked it, and it 404'd or timed out. That is worse than no link, because you invited the check.

**A sleeping deployment.** Free hosting tiers frequently spin down after inactivity, and a project taking thirty seconds to wake has already lost the reviewer. Check yours during the job search rather than assuming.

**Slow loading.** Large unoptimised images are the usual cause. Reviewers on mobile with poor connections leave.

**Broken on mobile.** A meaningful share of reviewers open links on a phone. A layout that breaks there eliminates you before anything is read.

**A project that errors on load.** Test everything you link, from a browser you are not logged into.

**Requiring login to view.** If a reviewer must create an account, they will not.

## Things that waste the reviewer's time

**No context on what a project is.** A screenshot with no explanation forces the reviewer to work out what they are looking at, and they will not — see [portfolio case study writing](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/portfolio-case-study-writing).

**Burying the outcome.** Put what happened near the top. Reviewers scan.

**Requiring navigation to understand.** If the homepage does not communicate what you do within a few seconds, the structure is wrong.

**A long personal statement first.** Nobody reads three paragraphs about your passion before seeing any work.

**Twelve projects.** More is worse. Reviewers look at the first two, so eight weak entries actively bury your strongest — the same curation principle as [side projects on your resume](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/side-projects-on-resume-2026).

## Substantive failures

**Output with no reasoning.** The most common real problem. Showing what you built proves it exists; explaining why you built it that way proves you made decisions, which is what is actually being assessed.

**No constraints mentioned.** Work with no budget, timeline or technical constraint reads as academic. Constraints are what make decisions interesting.

**Vague role on team projects.** "Worked on the redesign" tells a reviewer nothing. State what you personally owned — ambiguity is read unfavourably.

**No numbers.** An improvement without a measure is an assertion.

**Everything presented as flawless.** Naming a genuine misstep and what you changed is the strongest credibility signal available, and almost nobody includes it.

**Tutorial projects presented as original.** A to-do app or a well-known clone is recognisable immediately. Extend at least one beyond what the tutorial did — see [choosing a final year project for placements](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/final-year-project-for-placement).

## Presentation failures

**Design that fights the content.** Unusual navigation, animations that delay reading, low-contrast text. Creativity in the frame rather than the work is a common trap for designers particularly.

**Inconsistent quality.** One strong case study next to four thin ones drags the average down. Remove the weak ones.

**Typos.** In a document you controlled entirely, they read as carelessness.

**Stale content.** A portfolio last updated three years ago raises questions about what you have done since.

**No contact information**, or contact details that are hard to find.

**Numbers inconsistent with your resume.** A metric stated differently in two places undermines confidence in both — see [portfolio vs resume](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/portfolio-vs-resume-guide).

## The audit

Do this once and it catches almost everything.

**Open your portfolio on a phone**, on mobile data, in a browser where you are not logged in.

**Time how long the homepage takes to load.**

**Click every link**, including project links and any GitHub repository.

**Ask someone outside your field to look for two minutes**, then ask them what you do and which project was strongest. If they cannot answer, the structure is wrong rather than the work.

**Check every number** against your resume.

**Read it for typos** on a different day from when you wrote it.

**Confirm your contact details are correct** and easy to find.

## Common Mistakes

**Linking something that does not work.** A dead link or a sleeping deployment is worse than no link, because the reviewer checked at your invitation.

**Ignoring mobile.** A substantial share of reviewers open portfolio links on a phone, and a broken layout eliminates you before content is read.

**Showing output without reasoning.** It proves the work exists but not that you made decisions, which is what is being assessed.

**Including too many projects.** Reviewers look at the first two, so weak entries bury your strongest work rather than adding to it.

**Being vague about your role.** On team projects, unclear contribution is read as minimal contribution.

**Presenting everything as flawless.** A genuine misstep with what you changed afterwards is the strongest credibility signal you can offer.

**Letting it go stale.** A portfolio untouched for years raises questions about what you have done since.

**Numbers that disagree with your resume.** A metric stated two ways undermines confidence in every other claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the single worst portfolio mistake?

A broken link or a deployment that does not load. It is worse than having no portfolio, because you invited the reviewer to check and the check failed.

### How many projects should I include?

Two or three explained properly. Reviewers rarely go past the second, so additional weak entries bury your strongest work.

### Does mobile really matter?

Yes. A meaningful share of reviewers open links on a phone, and a layout that breaks there loses you before anything is assessed.

### Should I include work I am not proud of?

No. Inconsistent quality drags the average down, and curation is itself a signal.

### Do I need to show failures?

Including a genuine misstep and what you changed is the strongest credibility signal available, and almost nobody does it.

### How often should I update it?

At least when you change roles or complete something significant. A portfolio years out of date raises questions.

### What if my best work is confidential?

Generalise the client and use relative figures, or use older or personal work — see [portfolio for non-tech roles](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/portfolio-for-non-tech-roles) for handling this.

## Make This Practical

Audit the mechanics before touching the content. Open your portfolio on a phone, on mobile data, logged out — then click every single link including repositories, and time the homepage load. A dead link is the worst failure available to you and the easiest to miss.

Then cut rather than add. Remove everything except your two or three strongest pieces, because reviewers rarely go past the second and weak entries actively bury the good ones.

Finally, add the reasoning that most portfolios lack. For each remaining piece, state the constraint you worked within, what you considered, what you personally owned, the measured outcome, and one thing you would do differently — then check every number matches your resume exactly.
