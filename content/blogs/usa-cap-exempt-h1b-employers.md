## Key Takeaways

- Some employers can sponsor H-1B outside the annual numerical cap, which means no lottery, no March registration, and no waiting for October.
- The main categories are higher education institutions, non-profits affiliated with them, non-profit research organisations and government research organisations.
- Cap-exempt petitions can be filed at any time of year, and the person can start once approved rather than waiting for a fiscal year to begin.
- The exemption attaches to the employer, not to you — moving to a capped employer later usually means entering the lottery.
- Concurrent employment is possible, which is how some people combine a cap-exempt role with other work.

## Why this matters more than students realise

The standard US path for an international graduate runs through an [annual lottery](/blog/usa-h1b-lottery-new-grads). You work on [post-graduation training](/blog/usa-opt-cpt-explained-students), your employer registers you in March, and a random selection decides whether the petition can even be filed. Chances vary by year and by how many people register, and a strong career can end because a number did not come up.

Cap-exempt employment removes that step entirely. Not improves the odds — removes the step. There is no registration, no selection, no annual quota. If the employer qualifies and the petition is approvable, it can be filed and approved at any point in the year.

That is a structural difference, and it is startling how few graduates know the category exists. Students spend an autumn optimising [applications](/blog/usa-handshake-linkedin-job-search) to employers who will all have to enter the same lottery, while a whole class of organisations that never enters it goes unconsidered.

## Who qualifies

Four categories, and the boundaries matter because the exemption is a legal characteristic of the employer rather than a policy choice.

Institutions of higher education. Colleges and universities meeting the statutory definition. This is the largest and clearest category, and universities employ far more than faculty — research staff, data analysts, engineers, IT, clinicians, administrators, librarians, financial and grants professionals.

Non-profit entities related to or affiliated with an institution of higher education.

This is the category people miss. [Teaching](/blog/japan-jet-programme-and-teaching-jobs) hospitals, university-affiliated research institutes, foundations and similar organisations may qualify through their affiliation. The affiliation has to be genuine and documented, and where it exists the range of roles is broad.

Non-profit research organisations. Organisations primarily engaged in basic or applied research.

Government research organisations. Federal, and in some circumstances state, entities engaged in research.

What does not qualify: ordinary private companies, however prestigious; most non-profits without a qualifying research purpose or educational affiliation; and start-ups, regardless of what they do.

## The practical advantages

**No lottery.** The headline benefit and the reason to look at this at all.

**No filing season.** A capped petition is registered in March and, if selected, filed for an October start. A cap-exempt petition can be filed whenever the job exists, and the person can begin once it is approved. For someone whose training period expires in February, that difference is the whole ballgame.

**No cap on how many times you try.** The lottery gives you a defined number of attempts before your authorisation runs out. Cap-exempt employment has no equivalent constraint.

**Extension and portability work normally.** Once you hold H-1B status through a cap-exempt employer, the usual rules on extensions apply.

**It can preserve your position while you plan.** For someone who has exhausted lottery attempts, a cap-exempt role is a way to remain in status and in the workforce rather than leaving.

## The limitations, stated honestly

This is not a loophole and it carries real trade-offs.

The exemption attaches to the employer. If you later move to an ordinary private company, that employer generally has to go through the cap — meaning the lottery — unless an exemption applies to them too. Your cap-exempt years do not carry over as a personal entitlement.

Compensation is usually lower. University and non-profit salaries are generally below private-sector equivalents, particularly in technology and finance. This is the main reason people discount the route, and it is a real cost.

The hiring process is slower and more bureaucratic. Posting requirements, committee processes, and timelines measured in months. Plan accordingly.

Not every role at a qualifying employer is straightforward. The petition still has to establish that the position is a specialty occupation and that you are qualified for it.

Affiliation is a legal question. Whether a particular non-profit qualifies through its relationship with a university is a determination requiring evidence, and organisations differ in whether they have established it. Do not assume; ask.

## How to find these employers

**Start with universities directly.** Every university in a city you would consider, [career pages](/blog/usa-handshake-linkedin-job-search) searched for roles matching your field. Research staff, technical staff, data and IT roles, and administrative positions requiring a degree.

**Then their affiliated hospitals and research institutes.** Academic medical centres, university-affiliated laboratories, and research foundations. These employ engineers, statisticians, data scientists, project managers and clinical research staff in volume.

**Non-profit research organisations.** Policy institutes, scientific research organisations, public health bodies.

**National laboratories and federally funded research centres.**

Substantial technical employers, and a category almost invisible to graduating students — worth reading alongside the [national labs guide](/blog/usa-national-labs-and-research-jobs).

**Ask the question plainly.** "Is this position cap-exempt for H-1B purposes?" A university HR office answers this routinely. A smaller non-profit may not know, in which case ask whether they have sponsored H-1B before and how.

**Check public petition data.** Employers who have filed petitions are recorded in public disclosure data, searchable by organisation. An employer with a filing history has a process, which is the same signal that matters when [assessing any employer's ability to support you](/blog/usa-stem-opt-employer-requirements).

## Concurrent employment

A feature of the H-1B category that is worth knowing and is frequently misunderstood.

It is possible to hold H-1B status through more than one employer at the same time, each with its own petition. In practice this means a person can hold a cap-exempt position — often part-time — while also working for a capped employer that files a concurrent petition.

Two cautions. This is a genuinely technical area where the details matter and mistakes are consequential, so it is not something to arrange from an article. And the cap-exempt position must be real: an actual job, actually performed, with the hours and duties described. Arrangements that exist only on paper are precisely what draws scrutiny.

If this looks relevant to your situation, it is a conversation with an immigration attorney rather than a plan to execute yourself.

## Building a search around this

If you are an international graduate and you intend to stay, the practical move is to treat cap-exempt employers as a distinct segment of your target list rather than as a fallback.

**Build the list in parallel with your main list.** Every university and academic medical centre in the metropolitan areas you would consider, plus the research organisations in your field. Twenty to thirty organisations is realistic.

**Search their career pages weekly**, the same routine that works for [any target list](/blog/usa-handshake-linkedin-job-search). Note that university postings often stay open longer than corporate ones, which reduces the timing pressure.

**Expect the applications to look different.** Academic and non-profit applications frequently want a [cover letter](/blog/usa-cover-letter-for-employers) that is actually read, sometimes a statement of research interest, and references earlier in the process than a corporate application would.

**Network into them the same way.** Faculty, postdocs and research staff are approachable and are rarely contacted by students seeking staff roles. Your own professors are the obvious starting point and the most under-used contact you have.

**And weigh the whole package.** Lower salary, and frequently better benefits, more predictable hours, tuition support for further study, and — the thing that dominates everything else if you intend to stay — a route that does not run through a lottery.

## The roles people overlook

Because "university job" makes most graduates picture a lecturer, the actual breadth of the category is worth spelling out. Universities and academic medical centres are large, complex organisations and they employ almost every function a company does.

Research and technical staff. Research assistants, research associates, laboratory managers, bioinformaticians, statisticians and data analysts. These are the closest equivalent to a graduate technical role and they exist in volume at any research-heavy institution.

Software and IT. Universities run substantial technology operations — student systems, research computing, clinical systems, networks, security. These are real engineering jobs and they are almost invisible to students who only look at [technology companies](/blog/japan-tech-jobs-for-foreign-graduates).

Data and analytics. Institutional research, enrolment analytics, clinical data, public health data. Growing quickly, and frequently short of good candidates.

Clinical research. Coordinators, associates and managers at academic medical centres. Substantial hiring, structured progression, and a common entry point for life sciences graduates.

Engineering and facilities. Large campuses employ civil, mechanical and electrical engineers, plus project managers running capital work.

Finance, grants and administration. Research grant administration in particular is a specialised, well-paid function that most graduates have never heard of and that institutions consistently struggle to fill.

Library and information science, instructional design, communications, and international student services — all professional roles requiring degrees.

The practical instruction: search a university's career page by function rather than by the word "research", and search the affiliated hospital separately, because it is frequently a distinct employer with its own postings and its own — usually also exempt — status.

## Building the parallel list

Because this is a distinct segment rather than a fallback, it deserves its own list built at the same time as your main one.

**Start geographically.** Pick the metropolitan areas you would actually live in. For each, list every university, every academic medical centre, and every research institute. In a large city this is easily fifteen organisations.

**Add the national laboratories and federally funded research centres** relevant to your field, wherever they are — the [national labs route](/blog/usa-national-labs-and-research-jobs) is worth a separate look.

**Add non-profit research organisations** in your sector: policy institutes, public health bodies, scientific foundations.

**Record three columns per organisation:** whether they are cap-exempt, whether they have filed petitions before, and where their careers page lives.

**Check the pages weekly.** University postings stay open longer than corporate ones, which lowers the urgency, and they do not cluster into a recruiting season, which means there is no window to miss and no window to wait for either.

**Then treat it as a real channel.** The mistake is building this list, feeling reassured that a backstop exists, and never applying. It is not a backstop — for an international graduate intending to stay, it is frequently the highest-expected-value channel on the whole list, because every application to a capped employer is discounted by the lottery and these are not.

## Talking to a university about sponsorship

The conversation runs differently from a corporate one, and knowing the shape of it saves time.

Ask HR, not the hiring manager. At most universities, immigration is handled by a central office that does this constantly. A department head may genuinely not know whether a role is cap-exempt; the international employment office will answer in a sentence.

Ask early and plainly. "Is this position cap-exempt for H-1B purposes, and has the department sponsored before?" Both are ordinary questions in this sector and neither is awkward.

Expect a slower, more procedural answer. Universities have committees, posting requirements and defined processes. Timelines are longer than corporate hiring and the process is more predictable — you are unlikely to be ghosted, and you are unlikely to hear quickly.

Understand the salary constraint. The petition requires payment at or above the prevailing wage for the role and location. University salary bands are compressed, and occasionally a role's band sits below the required wage, which blocks the petition. It is rare and it happens, and asking about it is not rude.

Ask about the timeline relative to your authorisation. If your current status expires in five months and the university's process takes four, that is worth knowing in week one rather than week twelve.

And ask what happens after. Some institutions support permanent residence applications for staff and some do not. If you intend to stay long term, that is a materially important answer and it is rarely volunteered.

> **Tip:** Search universities, teaching hospitals and affiliated non-profits directly — they can file at any time without the lottery. This is the single largest structural advantage available to an international graduate.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not knowing the category exists.** By far the most common, and it costs people the whole option.
- **Assuming every non-profit qualifies.** Assuming every non-profit qualifies the exemption is specific, and most non-profits do not meet it.
- **Assuming a cap-exempt employer means permanent exemption.** Assuming a cap-exempt employer means permanent exemption the exemption is the employer's, and moving to a capped employer usually means the lottery.
- **Only looking at faculty roles.** Universities and academic medical centres employ engineers, analysts, IT staff and administrators in far larger numbers.
- **Applying with a corporate-style resume.** Academic and non-profit applications frequently expect more written material and earlier references.
- **Arranging concurrent employment without advice.** Arranging concurrent employment without advice a technical area where errors have real consequences.

## What the petition still has to establish

Cap-exempt removes the lottery. It does not remove the petition, and the requirements are the same ones any H-1B has to meet.

**The role must be a specialty occupation.** The position must normally require at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. A university role with a genuine degree requirement in a defined discipline satisfies this straightforwardly; a broadly-defined administrative role may be harder.

**You must be qualified for it.** Your degree must be in the relevant field, or you must have an equivalent combination of education and experience.

**The wage must meet the required level.** There is a prevailing wage determination for the role in that location, and the employer must pay at or above it. This occasionally causes friction at institutions with compressed salary bands.

**The employer must file properly**, including the labour condition application that precedes the petition.

**What this means for you practically.** An employer's cap-exempt status is necessary and not sufficient — the specific role still has to work. When you ask "is this position cap-exempt?", the useful follow-up is "and has the department sponsored this kind of role before?" An institution's central HR may be experienced while an individual department has never done it.

**And confirm the affiliation question directly for non-university employers.** A hospital or research institute's exemption rests on documented affiliation or on its research purpose. Organisations that qualify know they qualify and will say so; a vague answer means nobody has established it, and that is not something to assume on your own behalf.

## Why this route stays under-used

It is worth naming the reasons, because each of them is a bias rather than a real obstacle, and recognising them is most of what it takes to act.

Nobody mentions it. Career services talk about employers who come to campus. Peers talk about the companies everyone has heard of. The category simply does not come up, so students do not know to look.

The salary comparison is visible and the risk comparison is not. A lower number is concrete and immediate. A lottery you might not clear is abstract until it happens. People discount the second and over-weight the first, and then find themselves unselected in March with no plan.

"University job" sounds like academia. It reads as a step off the career track rather than as a technical role with a research group, which is what a great many of these positions are.

The applications look unfamiliar. Longer forms, cover letters that get read, references early. It is a different genre and it feels like more work.

And it feels like settling. This is the real one. Choosing an employer partly for its immigration characteristics feels like optimising for the wrong thing.

The honest counter: if your intention is to build a career in the US, the route that does not depend on a random draw is not settling — it is the one that lets everything else you do actually compound. Two years at a research institute that can sponsor beats two years of excellent work at an employer where a number decides whether you stay.

## A worked comparison

Two graduates, same degree, same ability, different channel. This is the arithmetic that makes the case.

**Candidate A** takes a software role at a well-known technology company. Salary is high. In March the employer registers them in the lottery. They are not selected. The employer registers them again the following March, using the second year of their STEM extension. Not selected. Third and final attempt the year after. Whether they build a life in the US comes down to three draws, and if none of them land, they leave a job they were good at.

**Candidate B** takes a research software role at a university-affiliated medical centre. Salary is perhaps a third lower. Their employer files a cap-exempt petition in September, it is approved in November, and they start on H-1B status without a lottery ever entering the picture. Three years later, with real experience and a stable status, they move to a private employer who files a petition — or they stay, because the work turned out to be interesting.

**The point is not that B made the better choice.** It depends on the person, the work and how much the salary difference matters to them. The point is that A is running an excellent career through a random filter, and a great many people in A's position never knew that B's option existed.

**The practical version:** you do not have to choose. Apply to both, and if the lottery does not come through, the cap-exempt applications you sent in parallel are the reason you still have options in April.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a PhD to work at a university?

No. Universities and academic medical centres employ large numbers of staff in technical, analytical, administrative and clinical support roles at bachelor's and master's level. The petition requires the position to be a specialty occupation, not a doctorate.

### Is there really no lottery?

For a genuinely cap-exempt employer, correct — no registration and no numerical cap. The petition still has to be approvable on its merits.

### Can I move to a private company later?

Yes, and that employer will generally need to go through the cap unless an exemption applies to them. Some people use a cap-exempt role to remain in status while continuing to try the lottery through a concurrent capped petition, which is a technical arrangement requiring proper advice.

### How do I confirm an employer is cap-exempt?

Ask their HR or immigration team directly — universities answer this routinely. For affiliated organisations the answer depends on documented affiliation, so ask whether they have filed cap-exempt petitions before.

### Are the salaries much lower?

Generally lower than private-sector equivalents, particularly in technology. Benefits, hours, stability and tuition support offset some of it, and the absence of lottery risk is worth a great deal if your intention is to stay.

### When should I start looking at these?

Early — ideally while you are still building your [autumn target list](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline), because university hiring timelines run longer and the roles do not cluster in a season the way corporate graduate hiring does.
