## Key Takeaways

- The conventional route is a **Non-Immigrant B visa followed by a work permit**, both employer-sponsored.
- **Employer conditions matter as much as your own** — registered capital and Thai employee ratios determine whether a company can hire you.
- The **SMART Visa** targets specialists, investors, executives and startups in defined industries with lighter conditions.
- The **Long-Term Resident visa** offers a multi-year route for high earners, specialists, remote workers and retirees.
- **Working on a tourist visa is unlawful** and enforcement is real.

## The conventional route

How most foreign employment in Thailand is authorised.

**Non-Immigrant B visa.** Obtained before entry, on the basis of employment documentation from your Thai employer. It permits entry for the purpose of work; it does not itself permit working.

**Work permit.** Applied for after arrival, through the Ministry of Labour, on the basis of the employer's documentation. Only this authorises you to work.

**The distinction matters.** Holding a Non-B visa without a work permit does not make working lawful.

**The permit is tied** to the employer, the position and frequently the work location.

**Extensions of stay** are then applied for through immigration, aligned to the employment.

**90-day reporting.** Foreigners staying long-term must report their address to immigration at defined intervals, which is an ongoing obligation people forget.

**Re-entry permits** are needed if you leave and return, otherwise your extension of stay lapses.

**And changing employer requires a new permit**, not a transfer.

## What the employer must satisfy

The conditions that frequently decide the outcome.

**Registered capital** at a specified minimum per foreign employee.

**Thai employee ratio** — a specified number of Thai staff per work permit issued.

**These are the reason** many small Thai companies simply cannot employ foreigners, whatever they might wish.

**BOI-promoted companies** face relaxed conditions, which is a substantial practical advantage and worth identifying when job hunting.

**Representative offices and regional offices** have their own arrangements.

**Which means** the single most useful question to ask a prospective Thai employer is whether they have employed foreigners before and whether they hold BOI promotion.

## SMART Visa and LTR

The newer routes that changed the picture for some people.

**SMART Visa** targets four groups — highly skilled talent, investors, executives and startup entrepreneurs — in targeted industries defined by government policy.

**Its advantages** include a longer permitted stay, exemption from the work permit requirement in defined circumstances, relaxed reporting obligations and provisions for family.

**Eligibility is industry-specific**, which is the main constraint — it applies to targeted sectors rather than to all employment.

**The Long-Term Resident visa** is broader, covering wealthy global citizens, wealthy pensioners, work-from-Thailand professionals and highly skilled professionals.

**Its advantages** include a multi-year stay, a digital work permit route, relaxed reporting and family inclusion.

**The work-from-Thailand category** is the significant one for remote workers, requiring employment with an established foreign company and meeting income and experience conditions.

**Both have specific criteria** including income thresholds, and both are revised — verify current requirements with the Thai authorities rather than relying on summaries.

## Documents and process detail

What the application actually needs.

**From you:** passport, degree certificates, professional qualifications, employment references, a medical certificate and passport photographs.

**Document legalisation** where required — practice varies by nationality and document type, so confirm what your case needs.

**From the employer:** company registration documents, financial statements, VAT registration, the employee list demonstrating the Thai staffing ratio, and a description of the position.

**The employer does most of the work**, which is why their experience with the process matters so much.

**Processing** is generally reasonably quick once the file is complete, which puts the emphasis on document preparation.

**The permit book** — historically a physical blue book — records your employer, position and location, and changes must be recorded in it.

**And keep it with you** or accessible, since you can be asked to produce it.

## Family and dependants

The arrangements that follow.

**Dependants** can generally obtain Non-Immigrant O visas on the basis of your employment, with extensions aligned to yours.

**A spouse's right to work is separate.** Accompanying status does not confer permission to work, and a spouse wanting employment needs their own permit through their own employer.

**International schooling** in Bangkok is extensive and expensive, and it is frequently the largest cost in a family relocation.

**Healthcare** is excellent privately, and international insurance makes it accessible.

**The LTR visa includes family provisions** that are more generous than the conventional route, which is a further argument for checking eligibility.

**And 90-day reporting applies to dependants too**, which is easy to overlook.

## Common problems and how to avoid them

Six that recur.

**Arriving without the Non-B.** The visa should generally be obtained before entry for the conventional route, and arriving as a tourist intending to convert is not reliable.

**Working before the permit is issued.** The visa alone does not authorise work, and the gap between arrival and permit issuance is a period in which you must not work.

**Employer unable to sponsor.** Capital and staffing ratio conditions are frequently the blocker, and discovering this after accepting an offer wastes months.

**Missing 90-day reports.** An ongoing obligation carrying penalties.

**Travelling without a re-entry permit**, which lapses your extension of stay and creates a genuine problem on return.

**And letting documents expire mid-process** — medical certificates and other supporting documents have validity periods.

**The common thread** is that Thailand's system is procedural rather than discretionary. Following the steps in order works; improvising does not.

## Long-term status and permanent residence

Where the routes lead.

**Permanent residence exists** and is available after a qualifying period of consecutive annual extensions of stay, subject to a quota by nationality and to conditions including income, tax payment history and Thai language ability.

**The quota is genuinely limiting** — a fixed number per nationality per year — which makes Thai permanent residence considerably harder to obtain than in most countries covered in this series.

**Citizenship** follows permanent residence after a further qualifying period, with language and other requirements.

**The LTR visa** offers a long-term status without leading to permanent residence in the same way, which is a different proposition — long stay without settlement.

**The Elite visa** similarly offers long stay through payment without work rights or a settlement path.

**Which means** Thailand is a country where long-term living is achievable through several routes and formal settlement is genuinely difficult, and it is worth being clear about which you are pursuing.

## Comparing the routes

A summary to help you choose.

**Non-B plus work permit.** For anyone employed by a Thai entity. Requires the employer to meet capital and staffing conditions. Ties you to that employer.

**BOI-promoted employer route.** Same structure with relaxed conditions on the employer's side, which is why identifying BOI companies matters.

**SMART Visa.** For specialists, executives, investors and startups in targeted industries. Longer stay, work permit exemption in defined circumstances, lighter reporting.

**LTR visa.** For high earners, specialists, remote workers and pensioners. Multi-year, digital work permit, annual rather than 90-day reporting, family inclusion.

**DTV.** Longer-stay multiple entry for remote workers and defined activities, with a lower bar and a different structure.

**Elite visa.** Long stay through paid membership, no work rights.

**The question to ask yourself:** do I have a Thai employer, a qualifying foreign employer, targeted-industry expertise, or none of these? The answer points directly at the right route.

## Costs and who pays

The financial side of the process.

**Visa fees** at the Thai embassy or consulate for the Non-B.

**Work permit fee**, varying with the permit duration.

**Extension of stay fee** through immigration.

**Re-entry permit fees**, single or multiple entry.

**Document legalisation and translation**, which varies by nationality and is frequently the largest cost.

**Medical certificate.**

**And agent fees**, where the employer uses a visa agent — which many do, since the process is administratively fiddly.

**Practice on who pays varies.** Many employers cover the whole process; some expect the employee to bear document costs. Clarify at offer stage, since it is a normal question and the amounts are not trivial.

**LTR fees are higher** but cover a multi-year period, which changes the annualised comparison considerably.

## A note on visa agents

Widely used and worth understanding.

**Most employers use an agent** for work permit and extension processing, because the paperwork is fiddly and the offices are bureaucratic.

**Good agents save real time** and know the current requirements at specific offices, which vary more than they should.

**Poor agents create problems**, including cutting corners in ways that put your status at risk.

**Some arrangements are outright improper** — agents offering work permits without genuine employment, or extensions based on documentation that does not reflect reality. These leave you personally exposed.

**Ask who the agent is** and whether the employer has used them repeatedly.

**And retain your own documents.** An agent holding your passport for extended periods is normal in the process and you should still know where it is and when you get it back.

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: You have a conventional job offer

Confirm the employer can legally sponsor you — capital and staffing ratio — before anything else, then obtain the Non-B before entering and the work permit after arriving. The order matters.

### Scenario 2: You are a specialist in a targeted industry

Check SMART Visa eligibility. Where it applies it removes the work permit requirement in defined circumstances and offers a longer stay with lighter obligations.

### Scenario 3: You work remotely for a foreign employer

The LTR work-from-Thailand category was built for this, with income and employer conditions. It is a considerably better position than trying to make a tourist or education visa work.

> **Tip:** Remember that the Non-B visa and the work permit are two separate things. Holding the visa does not make working lawful — only the work permit does, and it is applied for after you arrive.

## Do's and Don'ts

### Do

- Confirm the employer meets the sponsorship conditions
- Ask about BOI promotion
- Check SMART and LTR eligibility
- Obtain the Non-B before entering
- Apply for the work permit after arrival
- Keep up 90-day reporting

### Don't

- Work on a tourist visa
- Assume the visa alone permits work
- Assume a permit transfers to a new employer
- Leave the country without a re-entry permit
- Ignore the restricted occupation list
- Rely on outdated threshold figures

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing visa with work permit.** They are separate and only the permit authorises work.
- **Assuming any employer can sponsor.** Capital and ratio conditions frequently prevent it.
- **Missing SMART or LTR eligibility.** Both are considerably lighter where they apply.
- **Forgetting 90-day reporting.** An ongoing obligation that people overlook.
- **Travelling without a re-entry permit.** It lapses your extension of stay.
- **Working on a tourist visa.** Unlawful and genuinely enforced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between a Non-B visa and a work permit?

The Non-B permits entry for the purpose of work; the work permit authorises the work itself. Holding the visa alone does not make working lawful.

### Why can some Thai employers not hire foreigners?

Minimum registered capital per foreign employee and a required ratio of Thai staff per permit. Small companies frequently cannot meet these conditions.

### What is the SMART Visa?

A route for highly skilled talent, investors, executives and startup entrepreneurs in government-targeted industries, offering longer stay and work permit exemption in defined circumstances.

### What is the LTR visa?

A long-term resident route covering wealthy global citizens, pensioners, work-from-Thailand professionals and highly skilled professionals, with multi-year stay and lighter obligations.

### Do I have to report to immigration?

Yes. Long-term foreign residents must report their address at 90-day intervals, and it is an obligation people routinely forget.

### Can I change employer on my work permit?

Not by transfer. A new permit is required, which is a genuine constraint on job mobility in Thailand.
### What documents does a Thai work permit need?

From you: passport, degrees, qualifications, references and a medical certificate. From the employer: company registration, financial statements, VAT registration and the Thai employee list proving the staffing ratio.

### Can my spouse work in Thailand?

Not automatically. Accompanying dependant status does not confer permission to work; a spouse needs their own work permit through their own employer.
### Can I get permanent residence in Thailand?

It exists but is genuinely difficult — available after a qualifying period of consecutive annual extensions, subject to a fixed annual quota per nationality and conditions including income, tax history and Thai language ability.
### Which Thai visa route fits my situation?

A Thai employer means Non-B plus work permit; targeted-industry expertise points to the SMART Visa; a qualifying foreign employer with sufficient income points to the LTR; and the DTV is the lower-bar alternative.
### Who pays for a Thai work permit?

Practice varies — many employers cover the whole process while some expect you to bear document legalisation costs. Clarify at offer stage, as the amounts are not trivial.
