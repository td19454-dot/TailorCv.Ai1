## Key Takeaways

- Salary is negotiable in the UK more often than British candidates assume, but the register is more restrained than the American approach.
- Many UK adverts publish a salary band, which changes the negotiation from guessing to positioning within a stated range.
- Answer the expectations question with a researched range, and try to get them to state a figure first.
- Pension contribution, annual leave, and bonus structure carry real value and are frequently more flexible than base salary.
- If you need visa sponsorship, the salary must clear the relevant threshold - which limits how far down you can go.

British reticence about money is real, and it costs people money. A significant number of UK candidates accept the first figure offered without a conversation, on the assumption that asking would seem grasping.

It would not. Employers expect a discussion at offer stage, and most have room. What differs from the American approach is the tone: a single well-reasoned ask lands well, while aggressive back-and-forth does not.

This guide covers researching the range, handling the expectations question, and negotiating the full package.

## Research the Range First

Negotiating without a number is guessing, and guessing low is the default failure.

**Published bands.** A large share of UK adverts state a salary range, which is a significant advantage. If the advert says £45,000-£55,000, the negotiation is about where you sit in that band, not whether the band is right.

**Salary surveys.** Recruitment agencies publish annual salary guides by sector and region, and they are among the more reliable free sources for UK market data.

**Job boards.** Search the same title in the same region and read twenty adverts. The pattern is more informative than any single figure.

**Recruitment consultants.** If an agency is involved, ask directly what the client's budget is. Good consultants will tell you - it is in their interest for the placement to happen.

**Regional variation is large.** London salaries carry a substantial premium over the rest of the UK, and it is partly offset by housing costs. A £55,000 London offer and a £45,000 Manchester offer may be broadly equivalent in practice. ## The Expectations Question

It usually arrives in the first recruiter call, sometimes before you know much about the role.

**Try to defer once, politely:**

> I'd rather understand the role properly first - do you have a band in mind for this position?

This works more often than people expect, particularly with agency consultants, and whoever names a figure first anchors the discussion.

**If pressed, give a researched range:**

> Based on what I've seen for similar roles in this region, I'd be looking at somewhere in the £48,000 to £55,000 range, though it depends on the wider package.

Two rules for the range:

**Put your target near the bottom of it.** Employers hear the lower number. If you want £52,000, say £52,000 to £58,000 - not £45,000 to £55,000.

**Do not lowball to seem affordable.** It anchors everything that follows and quietly signals you do not know your market.

Fuller framing is in [the salary expectation answer guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/salary-expectation-answer-guide).

**On current salary:** you are not obliged to disclose it, and if you are underpaid, disclosing it caps you. Redirecting to your expectations for the new role is entirely normal and rarely challenged.

## Negotiating the Offer

Once you have an offer, you have leverage you did not have before - they have chosen you and would rather not restart.

**Be pleased first.** Thank them, confirm your enthusiasm, then raise the number. Negotiating from an obviously positive position works better than an adversarial one.

**Make one clear ask, with a reason:**

> Thank you - I'm really pleased, and I'd like to accept. Before I do, could we discuss the base? Based on the market rate for this role and the CRM migration experience I'd be bringing, I was hoping for £56,000. Is there flexibility there?

**Justify with value, not need.** Rent increases and personal circumstances are not arguments. Market rate and what you bring are.

**Ask once, properly.** In UK culture, one well-reasoned ask is normal and expected. A third round of haggling starts to damage the relationship you are about to begin.

**Get it in writing** before resigning anything.

**Be prepared for no.** If the answer is a firm no on base, that is when you move to the rest of the package.

## Negotiate the Whole Package

Base salary is frequently the least flexible element, particularly in banded structures and the public sector. These often are not:

**Pension contribution.** The most undervalued item in UK negotiation. Employers must contribute a minimum under auto-enrolment, but many offer considerably more, and matched contributions differ enormously between employers. An extra 3% employer contribution is real money that compounds.

**Annual leave.** The statutory minimum is 28 days including bank holidays, but many employers offer more, and extra days are often easier to grant than salary. Some run buy-and-sell schemes.

**Bonus.** Ask what it is actually based on, what it has paid out historically, and whether it is discretionary or contractual. A "up to 20% bonus" that has never paid above 5% is worth knowing about.

**Flexible and hybrid working.** Frequently negotiable, and worth real money in commuting cost and time.

**Start date.** If you need time between roles, ask.

**Professional fees and training.** Chartered body membership, study support for ACCA or CIMA, and conference budgets are commonly granted.

**Private medical, life assurance, cycle to work, season ticket loan.** Smaller, but they add up.

The general principles are in [how to negotiate a salary offer](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary-offer).

## If You Need Visa Sponsorship

This changes the negotiation in one specific way: the salary must meet the applicable thresholds for the Skilled Worker route, and those were raised substantially in 2024.

Practical consequences:

**There is a floor you cannot go below.** An employer cannot sponsor you at a salary beneath the relevant threshold even if you would accept it.

**That floor can work in your favour.** It removes the option of a lowball offer entirely.

**Do not offer to take less to make sponsorship easier.** It does not work that way, and it signals you do not understand the system.

**Ask who pays the visa costs.** The Immigration Skills Charge falls on the employer, but the application fee and health surcharge often fall on you unless negotiated. It is a legitimate question at offer stage, and some employers cover it.

## Common Mistakes

**Accepting immediately.** Even a day to consider is normal and costs nothing.

**Naming a figure first when you could avoid it.** Whoever anchors, wins ground.

**Building the range around your minimum.** They hear the bottom number.

**Justifying with personal need.** Market rate and value are the arguments.

**Negotiating too early.** Not in the first interview. Wait for the offer.

**Ignoring pension and leave.** Often more flexible than base, and worth real money.

**Haggling repeatedly.** One clear ask is the UK norm.

**Resigning before the offer is in writing.** Never do this.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal to negotiate salary in the UK?

Yes, at offer stage, and employers expect it. What differs from the US is the register - one well-reasoned request rather than extended back-and-forth.

### Do I have to tell them my current salary?

No. You can decline politely and redirect to your expectations for the new role. If you are currently underpaid, disclosing it anchors you low.

### How much should I ask for above the offer?

There is no fixed rule, and it depends entirely on where the offer sits against market rate. Research the range first and make your ask consistent with it - a request grounded in market data lands far better than a percentage plucked from advice articles.

### What if the salary band is fixed?

Common in the public sector, the NHS, and banded corporate structures. Base may genuinely be immovable, but starting point within the band, annual leave, flexible working, and training budget often are not.

### Can I negotiate a graduate scheme salary?

Rarely - graduate scheme salaries are usually standardised across the intake and genuinely fixed. See [the UK graduate scheme guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-graduate-scheme-guide).

### When should I bring up salary?

Not in a first interview unless they raise it. The recruiter screening call is where expectations usually come up, and the offer stage is where the real negotiation happens - as covered in [UK interview tips](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-interview-tips).

## Make This Practical

Research before anyone asks you a number. Read twenty adverts for your title in your region, check an agency salary guide, and settle on a target figure you can justify - so the expectations question meets a prepared answer rather than an improvised one.

Then handle the anchoring. Try once to get them to state a band, and if pressed, give a range with your target near its bottom. Never volunteer your current salary.

Finally, negotiate the whole package once the offer arrives. Make one clear ask on base with a market-rate justification, and if that is fixed, move to pension contribution, annual leave, bonus structure, and flexible working - which are frequently more negotiable and genuinely valuable. Get everything in writing before you resign anything, and if you need sponsorship, confirm the salary clears the threshold in the [UK Skilled Worker visa guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/uk-skilled-worker-visa-guide).
