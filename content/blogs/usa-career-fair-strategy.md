## Key Takeaways

- Apply online before the fair. Most large employers use the fair to meet candidates already in their system, not to collect new ones.
- Recruiters take brief notes and move on. Your goal is to be memorable in one specific way, not to be liked in general.
- Target ten to fifteen employers deliberately rather than walking every aisle collecting merchandise.
- The follow-up within 24 hours is the step that converts a conversation into an interview, and most students skip it.
- Fairs matter far less than the online application for some employers and far more for others. Ask which.

## Before: the work that decides the outcome

**Apply online first.** This is the single highest-value preparation step. Large employers open applications in August, screen continuously, and use the fair to put faces to resumes already in the pipeline. Arriving without having applied means the recruiter has nothing to look up afterwards.

**Get the employer list and cut it down.** Most fairs publish attendees in advance. Ten to fifteen deliberate targets beats forty drive-bys. Sort them into three groups: must-speak, worth-speaking, and skip.

**Check who sponsors, if that applies to you.** International students waste a lot of fair time on employers who will not sponsor. Filtering in advance is more useful than any conversation technique — the [visa sponsorship question](/blog/usa-visa-sponsorship-question-interview) is better resolved before you queue for twenty minutes.

**Research three specifics per must-speak employer.** Not the mission statement — something concrete. A product they shipped, a team you would want to join, a technology in their stack. Three facts is enough to ask a question nobody else at the fair is asking.

**Print resumes, but expect them to be scanned.** Bring more than you think. Many recruiters scan a QR code or ask you to apply online instead; the printed copy is a prop that structures the conversation as much as a document they keep. Make sure the version you bring is the one that [passes an ATS check](/ats-analysis), because that is the copy that gets uploaded.

## During: what recruiters are actually doing

Understand the recruiter's day. They will speak to somewhere between fifty and two hundred students, standing, for six hours. They remember almost none of it without notes.

What they write down is short: name, school, major, graduation date, and one line about you. Your entire objective is to control what that one line says.

**Lead with something specific.** "Hi, I'm Priya, I'm a junior in computer science — I applied for your data engineering internship last week and I wanted to ask about the team that works on the ingestion pipeline." That gives them a name, a role, and a reason to remember you.

Compare with "Hi, can you tell me about your company?" That produces the recruiting pitch and a line in their notes that reads: *asked what we do*.

**Ask a question that shows preparation.** The best questions are ones the recruiter cannot answer from the brochure. "Do interns on the platform team work on the same codebase as full-time engineers, or on separate projects?" gets a real answer and marks you as someone who has thought about the work.

**Keep it to three to five minutes.** Queues form. Recruiters notice who monopolises time and who is considerate of it. Leaving on a high note is better than being asked to wrap up.

**Get a name and a contact.** Ask for a business card or the recruiter's name and email. Without this, follow-up is guesswork.

**Ask what happens next.** "What's the best next step from here?" produces actionable information: apply online, watch for an assessment, contact a specific person. It also tells you how much the fair actually matters at that company.

## What to wear, briefly

Business casual is the safe default at most US university fairs — trousers or a skirt, a collared shirt or blouse, closed shoes. Banking and consulting skew formal; startups and many tech companies do not care at all.

Nobody has ever been rejected for being slightly overdressed. Being visibly underdressed at a finance table does get noticed. If the fair is industry-mixed, dress for the most formal employer on your must-speak list.

## After: the step that converts

**Follow up within 24 hours.** This is where most students lose the value of the fair entirely. A short email while the recruiter still remembers the conversation is worth more than the conversation was.

What it should contain:

- Where you met and roughly when
- One specific detail from the conversation, so they can place you
- What you have done since — usually "I submitted my application for the X role"
- A short, clear ask

> "Hi Marcus — we spoke at the Georgia Tech fair yesterday about the ingestion pipeline team. I've now applied for the data engineering internship (req 4471). If it's useful, I'd be glad to send the project I mentioned where I processed the transit dataset. Thanks for the time."

Four sentences. Specific, actionable, no pressure.

**Track everything.** By November you will not remember which of thirty conversations was which. Name, company, role, date, what was discussed, what you promised to send. A spreadsheet is fine.

**Do what you said you would.** If you offered to send something, send it that day. This is a surprisingly effective differentiator, because most people do not.

## When fairs matter and when they do not

Ask recruiters directly: *"Is the fair part of your screening process, or should I focus on the online application?"*

Answers vary honestly and usefully. At some employers, fair conversations feed directly into interview selection. At others, the fair is branding and every candidate goes through the same online funnel regardless.

Where fairs matter less, your effort belongs in the [autumn application window](/blog/usa-campus-recruiting-timeline) and in referrals. Where they matter more, prepare properly for a handful of employers rather than spreading thin.

Smaller employers at fairs are systematically underrated. The queue at the famous company is ninety minutes; the regional firm with real openings and no queue will have a ten-minute conversation with you and remember it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not applying online beforehand.** Leaves the recruiter with no record to attach you to.
- **Asking what the company does.** Signals zero preparation and produces a forgettable note.
- **Collecting merchandise instead of contacts.** A tote bag is not a lead.
- **Talking for fifteen minutes.** Recruiters remember who ignored the queue.
- **No follow-up.** The most common and most costly omission.
- **Leaving without a name.** Without a contact, follow-up goes to a generic inbox.
- **Only visiting famous companies.** Long queues, low conversion, and the accessible employers go unspoken to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to bring printed resumes?

Bring them. Many recruiters will direct you to apply online instead, but the printed copy structures the conversation and some still collect them. Ten to fifteen is usually enough.

### What if I have not decided what I want to do?

Say so honestly but narrow it to a direction. "I'm deciding between data and backend work" is fine; "I'm open to anything" gives the recruiter nothing to write down.

### Should I go to fairs in my first year?

There is little downside and some value in seeing how they run before it matters. Do not expect roles — most fair recruiting targets penultimate-year students.

### How do I follow up without an email address?

Ask at the table. Failing that, LinkedIn works — a connection request with a short note referencing the conversation is acceptable and often answered.

### Is a virtual career fair worth attending?

Usually yes, and often less competitive because attendance is lower. The mechanics are the same: apply first, prepare specifics, follow up. Queue dynamics are gentler.

### What if I am nervous talking to recruiters?

Prepare a two-sentence opening and rehearse it aloud. Nerves fall away after the third conversation, so start with an employer from your worth-speaking list rather than your top choice.
