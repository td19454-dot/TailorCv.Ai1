## Key Takeaways

- Before tailoring a resume someone else wrote, audit it. You will be interviewed on every line as if you wrote it.
- Check three things first: facts (are they accurate?), claims (can you defend each one for two minutes?), and voice (does it sound like you?).
- Professionally written and AI-written resumes often share the same problems: inflated verbs, generic phrases, and results nobody can explain.
- Fix structural problems - layout, parsing, length - before tailoring, or every tailored copy inherits them.
- Once audited, tailor the normal way: title, summary, bullet order, skills. The difference is you now know every line is yours.
- Keep what the writer did well. Good structure and strong phrasing are worth keeping; only replace what you cannot own.

Your resume was written by someone else - a paid service, your college's placement cell, a parent, a friend, a template, or an AI tool - and now you need to tailor it for a specific job. Start with an audit, not the tailoring. Every line will be questioned in interviews as if you wrote it, so check that the facts are accurate, the claims are defensible and the voice sounds like you. Then tailor as normal.

This guide gives you the audit checklist, the most common problems in inherited resumes, and how to tailor once you own the document.

## Why an Inherited Resume Needs an Audit First

Tailoring amplifies whatever is already there. If the base has a problem, every tailored version carries it.

### You answer for every line

Interviewers pick lines from your resume and ask you to expand. "Spearheaded a cross-functional digital transformation initiative" is hard to talk about if someone else wrote it about a spreadsheet you updated. [Tailoring without lying](/blog/tailoring-without-lying) covers the defensibility test that applies to every line.

### Other writers optimise for different things

A professional writer may optimise for impressiveness, a parent for completeness, a college template for uniformity, an AI tool for fluency. None of these is the same as "true, specific and matched to this job".

### Recruiters notice a borrowed voice

Heavy, generic phrasing is easy to spot, and a resume that sounds nothing like you in the interview creates doubt. [Can recruiters tell a resume was written by AI?](/blog/can-recruiters-tell-resume-written-by-ai) covers the patterns they notice.

## The Audit: Three Passes

Go through the resume three times, each time looking for one thing.

### Pass 1: Facts

Check every verifiable detail against reality.

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Job titles | Exactly your official title (a clarifier is fine; an upgrade is not) |
| Dates | Correct months and years, consistent format |
| Employers and locations | Correct names and cities |
| Degrees and grades | Correct degree name, institution, CGPA |
| Certifications | Only ones you hold, with correct names |
| Tools | Only tools you have genuinely used |

Parents and friends often guess at dates and titles; writers sometimes "tidy up" a title into something grander. Fix all of it.

### Pass 2: Claims

For each bullet, ask: could I talk about this for two minutes, with specifics, if an interviewer asked?

| Bullet type | Common issue | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Inflated verb | "Spearheaded", "revolutionised" for routine work | Use the accurate verb: built, ran, updated |
| Unexplained number | "Improved efficiency by 40%" with no idea where 40% came from | Replace with a number you can explain, or remove |
| Borrowed scope | "Led a team" when you were one member | State your actual role |
| Invented tool | Skills you have not used | Remove |
| Vague claim | "Drove strategic initiatives" | Replace with what you actually did |

For building numbers you can defend, see [estimating impact honestly](/blog/estimating-impact-honestly).

### Pass 3: Voice

Read the summary and a few bullets aloud. Would you say this in an interview?

> **Borrowed voice:** "A dynamic, results-oriented professional with a proven track record of leveraging synergies to drive transformational outcomes."
>
> **Your voice:** "Operations analyst who rebuilt the weekly inventory report for 40 stores and cut stockouts by about a fifth."

The second is plainer and far stronger. [How to stop sounding generic on your resume](/blog/how-to-stop-sounding-generic-on-resume) has more before-and-after examples.

## Common Problems by Source

Different writers leave different fingerprints.

| Source | Typical strengths | Typical problems |
|---|---|---|
| Paid professional writer | Clean structure, strong verbs | Inflated claims, generic summary, numbers you cannot explain |
| College placement template | Consistent format, complete education section | Generic objective, weak project descriptions, rigid layout |
| Parent or relative | Complete history, accurate context | Outdated conventions, too long, school-level detail, guessed dates |
| Friend's resume as a base | Relevant format for the field | Their structure and phrases, not yours; mismatched emphasis |
| AI tool | Fluent, fast, keyword-rich | Generic phrasing, invented metrics, repeated buzzwords |
| Online template | Visual design | Two-column layouts, icons and graphics that break ATS parsing |

### AI-written resumes specifically

AI tools produce fluent text quickly but tend to invent specifics - percentages, team sizes, tools - to make bullets sound complete. Check every number and tool against reality. The post on [AI resume tailoring in a human voice](/blog/ai-resume-tailoring-human-voice) covers how to edit AI output so it sounds like you.

### Paid writers specifically

A good writer's structure and phrasing are worth keeping. Where they went wrong is usually in claims: they may have written "managed a ₹2 crore budget" when you tracked spending against it. Correct the claim, keep the good phrasing.

## Fix the Structure Before You Tailor

Structural problems multiply across every version. Fix them once, in the base.

### Parsing and layout

Many templates and design-led resumes use two columns, tables, icons or text in headers that ATS parsers read poorly. The [copy-paste ATS test](/blog/copy-paste-ats-test) shows in thirty seconds whether your content comes through in order. If it does not, move to a single-column layout; see [two-column resumes and ATS](/blog/two-column-resume-ats-safe) and [ATS resume formatting mistakes](/blog/ats-resume-formatting-mistakes).

### Length

Parents and professional writers both tend to produce long resumes. Use [one page or two: the decision](/blog/resume-length-one-page-or-two-decision) to set the right length for your level.

### Sections

Remove outdated sections - "Objective" (unless required), "References available on request", declarations, personal details like marital status (unless required in your market) - and make sure you have a clear summary, experience, skills and education.

## Worked Example 1: A Fresher With a Placement-Cell Template

**Situation:** Aarav's resume was built from his college's placement template, filled in with help from a senior. He is applying off-campus for a data analyst role.

**Audit findings:**

- Objective: "To obtain a challenging position in a reputed organisation where I can utilise my skills." Generic.
- Skills: 18 items, including "Machine Learning" and "Tableau" - he has done one ML tutorial and never used Tableau.
- Projects: "Developed a sales prediction model using ML" - it was a guided course notebook.

**Fixes:**

> **Summary (replacing objective):** "Statistics graduate who analysed 3 years of a local distributor's sales data in Python and SQL to find the product mix behind a 12% sales dip."
>
> **Project (rewritten honestly):** "Built a sales forecasting notebook in Python (pandas, scikit-learn) as part of a guided analytics course; compared three models on 2 years of retail data."

Skills trimmed to what he can discuss: SQL, Python (pandas), Excel, Power BI. Now he tailors - and every line is defensible. For more on the placement template itself, see [campus placement resume mistakes](/blog/campus-placement-resume-mistakes).

## Worked Example 2: A Mid-Career Professional With a Paid Rewrite

**Situation:** Shalini paid for a professional resume rewrite two years ago. It looks polished, and she wants to tailor it for a senior HR business partner role.

**Audit findings:**

- "Spearheaded organisation-wide transformation, driving a 35% improvement in employee engagement." She ran engagement surveys for one division; scores rose about 8 points.
- "Managed a team of 12 HR professionals." She had two direct reports and coordinated with 10 others.

> **Before:** "Spearheaded organisation-wide transformation, driving a 35% improvement in employee engagement."
>
> **After:** "Ran the engagement survey and action-planning process for a 900-person division; engagement scores rose from 62 to 70 over two cycles."

She keeps the writer's clean layout and several strong bullets that were accurate. Then she tailors the summary and bullet order to the HRBP posting. Had she tailored the original, the inflated 35% claim would have appeared in every version and been questioned in every interview.

## Worked Example 3: A Career Changer Using a Friend's Resume as a Base

**Situation:** Tushar used his friend's software engineering resume as a template for his own move into QA engineering. The structure fits, but several phrases are his friend's.

**Audit findings:** the summary describes "5 years building scalable microservices" - his friend's background. Two bullet structures still contain his friend's project details in outline.

**Fixes:** He rewrites the summary from scratch around his own testing experience, replaces every borrowed bullet with his own work, and keeps only the section structure. He then tailors to QA postings, leading with test automation work. For the career-change side, see [resume matching for career changers](/blog/resume-matching-for-career-changers).

## When It Was Written for a Different Job

Inherited resumes are often aimed at the wrong target: a writer built it for the role you had in mind two years ago, or a template assumed a field you have since left. Accuracy is not the only problem - the whole document may point the wrong way.

### Signs the base is aimed at the wrong target

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| Summary names a role you no longer want | The base needs a new summary before any tailoring |
| Skills line leads with tools from your old direction | Reorder around your current target |
| Your most relevant recent work is condensed or missing | It was written before that work existed |
| Keywords match your old field's postings, not your new one | A scan against a current posting will show low coverage |

In that case, treat the audit as the first half of a re-base: fix the facts, then rebuild the summary and bullet selection around your current direction. [Splitting resume versions](/blog/splitting-resume-versions) covers building a new base without starting over.

## Should You Say Someone Helped?

Getting help with a resume is normal - career services, mentors, writers and tools are widely used, and nobody expects you to disclose it. What matters is that you can stand behind every line.

If an interviewer asks directly, answer honestly and briefly: "A career coach helped me with the structure, but everything on it is my work and I'm happy to go into any of it." That answer is fine. What is not fine is being unable to explain a line because you did not write it or it is not true - which is exactly what the audit prevents.

## Once Audited: Tailor as Normal

After the audit, the resume is yours, and tailoring works the usual way.

1. Match your headline to the posting's title where accurate.
2. Rewrite the summary around the posting's top requirement.
3. Reorder bullets in your recent roles so the most relevant lead.
4. Reorder the skills line, putting the posting's tools you have first.
5. Rewrite two or three bullets whose vocabulary is close but not matching.

These steps are covered in detail in [what to change when tailoring](/blog/what-to-change-when-tailoring). The difference now is that you can make them confidently, knowing every line holds up.

## Keep What Works

An audit is not a rewrite from scratch. Keep:

- **Good structure and layout** (if it parses correctly).
- **Strong, accurate phrasing** - a writer's verb choice is often better than your first draft.
- **Correct, specific numbers** you can explain.
- **A clean skills grouping** if it reflects your real skills.

Replace only what fails the facts, claims or voice test.

## Common Mistakes

### Tailoring without auditing

The fastest way to multiply someone else's mistakes across ten applications.

### Keeping inflated claims because they sound good

If you cannot defend a line, it will hurt you in the interview. Replace it with an accurate, specific line.

### Rewriting everything out of distrust

Good phrasing and structure are worth keeping. Audit line by line rather than starting over.

### Not checking the format

A design-heavy template can break ATS parsing. Test it before you tailor.

### Ignoring the voice test

A resume that sounds nothing like you creates doubt when the interviewer meets you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I tailor a resume someone else wrote?

Audit it first: check the facts, make sure you can defend every claim, and adjust the voice so it sounds like you. Fix any layout problems, then tailor the normal way - headline, summary, bullet order and skills.

### Is it okay to use a professionally written resume?

Yes, as long as every line is accurate and you can talk about it. Check a professional rewrite for inflated claims and unexplained numbers before using or tailoring it.

### How do I fix an AI-written resume?

Check every number, tool and claim against reality, replace generic phrases with specific facts, and read it aloud to make sure it sounds like you. AI tools often invent specifics that sound plausible.

### Can recruiters tell if my resume was written by someone else?

They often notice generic, inflated phrasing and a mismatch between the resume's voice and how you speak in interviews. Specific, accurate bullets in your own voice avoid both problems.

### Should I rewrite my resume from scratch?

Usually not. Keep good structure and accurate phrasing, and replace only lines that fail the facts, claims or voice tests. A full rewrite is only needed if the document is mostly inaccurate or badly formatted.

### What if my parent wrote my resume?

Check dates, titles and details carefully, remove outdated sections and school-level detail if you have more recent experience, and make sure the phrasing sounds like you.

### Is using a friend's resume as a template okay?

Using the structure is fine. Replace every phrase and bullet with your own experience - borrowed wording is easy to spot and hard to defend.

### How do I know if a claim on my resume is too strong?

Ask whether you could explain it for two minutes with specifics, and whether a former manager would agree with it. If not, tone it down to what you can defend.
