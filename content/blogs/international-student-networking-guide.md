## Key Takeaways

- Domestic classmates start with networks they did not build. You are not behind — you are starting a different task.
- Your university's alumni network is the fastest substitute and the most underused resource available to you.
- People from your own country working in your target market reply at unusually high rates and understand your specific situation.
- Networking norms differ by country; what reads as appropriately direct in one market reads as pushy in another.
- Referrals matter more for you than for domestic students, because they route around screens where your profile is unfamiliar.

## The gap, stated plainly

A domestic student often arrives at graduation with a network they never consciously built — a parent's colleague, a family friend in the industry, a school friend's older sibling, a summer job manager who remembers them.

You may have none of that in this country. That is a real disadvantage and it is worth naming rather than pretending otherwise.

What it is not is a permanent one. Networks are built from a standing start all the time; it simply has to be deliberate rather than inherited.

## Where to start

**University alumni.** The highest-yield channel by a wide margin. Shared institution is a genuine reason for a stranger to reply, and many people feel some obligation toward students from their school.

Most universities have an alumni platform, and LinkedIn's school filter finds the rest. Filter by company, by city, by course. Alumni who came from your country and studied your subject are the single most responsive group you will find.

**People from your country in your target market.** They understand your situation without explanation — the visa question, the resume conventions, the interview norms, the feeling of starting from zero. They also remember someone helping them. Reply rates in this group are notably high.

Find them through alumni networks, professional associations, and community groups in the city you are targeting.

**Professors and supervisors.** Frequently overlooked. Academics often have industry connections, former students in relevant companies, and genuine willingness to make an introduction for someone whose work they have seen.

**Current and former classmates.** The people ahead of you graduated into exactly the jobs you want. A message to someone a year ahead is low-stakes, and they were in your position twelve months ago.

**Anyone you have worked with.** Internships, part-time jobs, campus roles, research assistantships. These are references and contacts, and students routinely forget them.

## What to actually say

The mechanics that work are the same for everyone: **ask for a conversation, not a job.**

"Would you refer me?" asks a stranger to stake their reputation on you. "Could I ask you two questions about how your team works?" asks for ten minutes.

A message that works is short — under 150 words — identifies the connection immediately, shows a few minutes of research, and makes a small bounded ask. The full mechanics, including subject lines and follow-up timing, are in [cold outreach that gets replies](/blog/usa-cold-email-networking-students), and they apply regardless of where you are from.

One addition for international students: **do not open with your visa situation.** It frames you as a problem to be solved before they know anything about your work. It comes up naturally later, and by then you are a person rather than a case.

## Cultural calibration

Networking norms genuinely differ, and this is where international students most often misjudge.

**United States.** Direct outreach is normal and expected. Asking someone for a coffee chat is unremarkable. Enthusiasm reads well.

**United Kingdom.** More reserved. The same message should be a notch more understated. Over-effusiveness reads as insincere rather than keen.

**Canada.** Between the two, warm and informal, with informational interviews an established norm — the [Canadian informational interview](/blog/canada-informational-interview-guide) is a recognised thing people expect to be asked for.

**Singapore.** Professional and measured. Direct is fine; effusive is not. Introductions through a mutual contact carry more weight than cold approaches.

If you learned professional communication in a culture with strong hierarchy norms, the most common adjustment is that you may be under-asking. Sending a polite message to someone senior to you is normal in all four of these markets and is rarely seen as presumptuous.

## Doing it without feeling transactional

Many students dislike networking because it feels like using people. Two things help.

**Ask for information, not favours.** A conversation about how someone's team works is genuinely interesting to most people — they like talking about their work, and you learn something real. If a referral follows, it follows because they now know you.

**Be useful where you can.** You are not powerless. You can share something relevant you read, introduce two people you know, or give feedback on something they are building. Junior people underestimate how much a specific, thoughtful question is itself a contribution to someone's day.

And accept that most messages go unanswered. A 10-20% reply rate on well-targeted outreach is normal. Twenty messages producing three conversations is a good afternoon's work, not a failure.

## Turning conversations into outcomes

**Follow up within 24 hours.** Thank them, mention one specific thing you took from the conversation, and attach your resume *at this point* — not before. Make sure it is a version that [survives an ATS screen](/ats-analysis), because a referred application still gets parsed.

**Do what you said you would.** If you offered to send something, send it that day. Most people do not, and doing it is a cheap differentiator.

**Keep a record.** Who, where, when, what you discussed, what you promised. By the end of a recruiting season you will not remember.

**Stay in touch lightly.** A short note when you get an offer, or when something they mentioned comes up, keeps a contact alive at almost no cost.

## Why this matters more for you

Referrals do something specific: they get your application read by a human rather than filtered by software.

That is worth more to an international student, because your profile is more likely to be unfamiliar to a screener — an institution they do not recognise, a grading system they cannot interpret, a work-authorisation flag. A human reading your evidence is a better outcome for you than an automated match, which is exactly what a referral buys.

It also compounds. The first job is the hardest, and after it you have colleagues, references and a local track record. The network you build now is the one that makes the second and third moves straightforward.

## Common Mistakes

- **Only contacting people from your own country.** High reply rates, but a narrow network. Mix it.
- **Leading with visa status.** Frames you as a problem before they know your work.
- **Asking for a referral in message one.** Too large an ask from a stranger.
- **Never contacting professors.** Frequently well connected and genuinely willing.
- **Not following up after a good conversation.** Where most promising contacts quietly end.
- **Assuming your home country's formality norms apply.** Usually leads to under-asking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it rude to message strangers on LinkedIn?

Not in these markets, provided the message is short, specific and asks for something small. A blank connection request followed by a wall of text is what reads badly.

### What if my English is not fluent?

Write clearly and briefly rather than formally. Short, correct sentences read better than long ones attempting sophistication. Nobody is assessing your prose in a networking email.

### How many people should I contact?

Twenty well-targeted messages beat a hundred generic ones. Expect a minority to reply, and treat that as normal arithmetic.

### Should I only network with people from my target companies?

No. People one step removed often give better information and make introductions, precisely because they are not evaluating you.

### What if someone offers to help and then goes quiet?

One polite follow-up after a week or so. People get busy, and it is rarely personal. Two follow-ups is the limit.

### When should I start?

Before you need it. Networking in September for autumn recruiting works; networking in March in a panic is visibly different.
