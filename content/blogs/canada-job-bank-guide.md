## Key Takeaways

- Job Bank is the Government of Canada's official job board, and the listings are the least useful thing on it.
- Its wage data by occupation and region is the strongest free salary benchmark in the country.
- Job outlook ratings by province tell you where your occupation is actually in demand before you commit to a city.
- The NOC code lookup matters because your code governs immigration eligibility and provincial nominee streams.
- Employers post there to satisfy LMIA advertising requirements, which surfaces roles where sponsorship is already in play.

Most newcomers to Canada find Job Bank, scroll the listings, decide they are unremarkable, and never return. That is a mistake, because the listings are the least valuable part of the site.

What it actually offers is official labour market data — wages, demand forecasts and occupational classification — which is the information that should be shaping where you apply and what you ask to be paid.

## The wage data

The single most valuable feature.

**What it provides:** low, median and high wage figures for any occupation, filterable by province and region, drawn from official labour market surveys.

**Why it beats every other source:**

- **Official rather than crowdsourced**, so it holds up when cited in a negotiation
- **Occupation-specific** via your NOC code, not a job title guess
- **Regional**, so you get Halifax figures rather than a national blend
- **Free**, with no account required

**For newcomers this is decisive.** Arriving without a local benchmark is the main reason people accept below-market offers, and this removes the excuse entirely — see [the Canada salary benchmarking guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-salary-benchmarking-guide).

**Use the median as your anchor**, not the low figure.

## Job outlook by region

Underused and genuinely strategic.

Job Bank publishes **outlook ratings** for occupations by province and region — an assessment of whether prospects are good, fair or limited over the coming years.

**Why it matters:** it tells you where your occupation is actually needed before you commit to a city, a provincial nominee stream, or a move. A "good" outlook in a smaller province may be a far better bet than a "limited" one in Toronto.

**Combine it with wage data.** A province with strong outlook and reasonable wages relative to housing is a better target than the obvious big-city choice — see [the Calgary jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/calgary-jobs-guide) and [the Ottawa jobs guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/ottawa-jobs-guide).

**It also informs immigration strategy**, since provinces target occupations where they have shortages — see [the Canada provincial nominee guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-provincial-nominee-guide).

## NOC code lookup

Your National Occupational Classification code is more consequential than most newcomers realise.

**What it governs:**

- **Express Entry eligibility** — whether your work experience qualifies for a federal program
- **Category-based draws** — whether your occupation is targeted in a given round
- **Provincial nominee streams** — most of which specify eligible NOC codes
- **Wage and outlook data** — everything above depends on identifying it correctly

**Job Bank's tool lets you search by job title** to find your code, and shows the official duties and requirements for it.

**Getting it wrong closes routes silently.** If your reference letters describe duties that do not match the code you claimed, applications get queried or refused. Identify it before writing anything else — including your employment reference letters, as [the Canada provincial nominee guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/canada-provincial-nominee-guide) covers.

## The listings and LMIA postings

**The listings themselves** are broad but weighted toward roles that employers are required to advertise, plus a lot of regional and trades work. Worth checking, particularly outside major metros where commercial boards are thin.

**The LMIA connection is the interesting part.** Employers seeking a Labour Market Impact Assessment must advertise the role, and Job Bank is a standard place to do it. That means some postings there involve employers already engaged with hiring a foreign worker — see [the LMIA job search guide](https://thetailorcv.com/blog/lmia-job-search-guide).

**Filter for it.** The site allows filtering for roles open to newcomers and for those with LMIA relevance, which is a genuinely useful screen that commercial boards do not offer.

## Using it well

**Do this in order:**

1. **Find your NOC code** and confirm the official duties match your actual work
2. **Check the outlook rating** for that code across several provinces
3. **Pull wage data** — median, by province — for the provinces with good outlook
4. **Compare against housing costs** in those provinces
5. **Then choose where to target**, and only then start applying

Most people do step five first. Reversing the order is what makes the tool valuable.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating it as just a job board.** The listings are the least useful part; the wage, outlook and NOC data are what matter.

**Not using the wage data before negotiating.** It is official, free and occupation-specific, which makes it the most credible benchmark available to a newcomer.

**Anchoring on the low wage figure.** The median is the defensible number to negotiate from.

**Ignoring regional outlook ratings.** They tell you where your occupation is genuinely needed, which should precede any decision about where to move.

**Getting the NOC code wrong.** It governs Express Entry eligibility, category draws and provincial streams, and an error closes routes without telling you.

**Writing reference letters before confirming the NOC code.** Duties must match the code you claim, and rewriting letters afterwards is slow.

**Skipping the LMIA and newcomer filters.** They surface roles where an employer is already engaged with hiring foreign workers, which commercial boards cannot show you.

**Choosing a city before checking its outlook and wages.** Doing it in that order is how people move somewhere their occupation is not in demand.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Job Bank?

The Government of Canada's official job board, which also publishes wage data, regional job outlooks and NOC occupational classification information.

### Is the wage data reliable?

It is drawn from official labour market data rather than self-reporting, which makes it the most credible free salary source in Canada and citable in a negotiation.

### What is a NOC code?

The National Occupational Classification code for your occupation. It determines Express Entry eligibility, whether you match category-based draws, and which provincial nominee streams are open to you.

### How do I find my NOC code?

Job Bank's search tool lets you look it up by job title and shows the official duties, which you should check against what you actually do.

### What are job outlook ratings?

An assessment of employment prospects for an occupation by province and region. They tell you where your occupation is in demand before you commit to a location.

### Are Job Bank listings worth applying to?

Worth checking, particularly outside major cities and for roles connected to LMIA advertising requirements, where employers are already engaged with hiring foreign workers.

### Is it useful if I am already in Canada?

Yes — the wage data and outlook ratings are just as useful for negotiating a raise or planning a move between provinces.

## Make This Practical

Confirm your NOC code first, before writing reference letters or applying anywhere. It governs Express Entry eligibility, category draws and provincial nominee streams, and an incorrect code closes routes silently.

Then use the data before choosing a destination. Check outlook ratings for your occupation across several provinces, pull median wage data for the ones with strong prospects, and compare those against local housing costs — that sequence frequently points somewhere other than Toronto or Vancouver.

Finally, take the median wage figure into every salary conversation. It is official, regional and specific to your occupation, which makes it the most credible benchmark a newcomer can cite — and it is precisely the evidence that stops the Canadian experience objection turning into a below-market offer.
