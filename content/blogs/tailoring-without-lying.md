## Key Takeaways

- Framing changes emphasis and vocabulary; fabrication changes facts. Only the second is lying.
- Four tests catch almost every borderline case: verifiability, defensibility, proportion, and reversibility.
- Renaming real work in the employer's language is translation, not deception.
- The highest-risk claims are the verifiable ones - titles, dates, degrees, employment - because they are checked.
- An unprovable claim that collapses in an interview costs more than the gap it was hiding.

People new to tailoring often worry they are being dishonest. People comfortable with it sometimes drift past the line without noticing. Both need the same thing: a clear test.

## The Underlying Distinction

**Framing** changes how true things are presented - which facts you lead with, what you call them, how much space each gets.

**Fabrication** changes what is true - adding work you did not do, inflating numbers, claiming skills you lack.

Every employer expects the first. A resume is a persuasive document, and presenting your most relevant experience first is the whole point. Nobody expects a neutral chronological archive.

The second is the thing that ends processes and occasionally careers.

## Four Tests

### 1. Verifiability

Could an employer check this, and would the check agree with you?

Titles, dates, employers, degrees and certifications are all verifiable through reference checks and background-check providers. These are the highest-risk place to stretch, because verification is routine rather than exceptional.

Achievements are usually not directly verifiable — but a former manager asked "did she lead that migration?" will answer honestly, and that is a check too.

### 2. Defensibility

If an interviewer picks this line and asks you to talk about it for two minutes, can you?

This test is why skills-section padding fails so reliably. A tool you listed but barely used produces thirty seconds of vagueness in an interview, and the interviewer's conclusion is not "he does not know Kubernetes" but "he claims things he cannot support."

### 3. Proportion

Is the emphasis roughly proportionate to the reality?

Expanding a genuinely relevant three-month project from one bullet to three is fair framing. Expanding a two-week side task into a section that implies it was your main work is not — every individual sentence may be true while the document as a whole misleads.

### 4. Reversibility

Would you be comfortable if the employer saw the version you sent to a different company?

Tailoring produces different emphases, which is fine. It should not produce contradictory claims. If one version says you led a project and another says you supported it, one of them is false.

## Clearly Fine

**Reordering.** Putting the relevant role, bullet or skill first. Nothing is hidden; the order simply serves the reader.

**Renaming in their vocabulary.** You managed client relationships; the posting says stakeholder management. Use theirs. Same work, their word for it.

**Dropping irrelevant detail.** A resume is not a complete record. Omitting a hobby, a short unrelated job from a decade ago, or a tool nobody asked about is editing, not concealment.

**Clarifying an opaque title.** "Business Partner II (HR Business Partner)" shows both. Honest and helpful.

**Adding context that was always true.** Stating your budget, team size or scope because this posting cares about scale.

**Quantifying honestly after the fact.** Working out that the reports you automated saved roughly two days a month, and saying so, is legitimate — provided the estimate is defensible.

## Clearly Not

**Adding a skill you have not used.** Regardless of how peripheral it seems.

**Inflating a title.** Coordinator to Manager, Analyst to Senior Analyst. Verifiable and checked.

**Adjusting dates.** To close a gap or extend tenure. The most easily caught change on a resume.

**Claiming someone else's work.** Taking credit for a project you observed.

**Inventing numbers.** "Increased revenue 40%" when you have no basis for the figure.

**Listing an unfinished degree as complete.** Including "expected" dates that have passed.

## The Grey Zone, Resolved

A few cases that genuinely feel ambiguous.

**"I did this but it was a team effort."** Use accurate verbs. *Led*, *owned*, *contributed to* and *supported* mean different things and interviewers listen for them. "Contributed to a migration that cut costs 30%" is honest; "cut costs 30%" alone implies sole ownership.

**"I used it once three years ago."** Not a skill for your skills section. If it is relevant, mention it in the context where it happened.

**"My title was wrong for what I did."** Clarify with the market term plus scope. Do not substitute a title you did not hold.

**"I don't have the exact number."** Estimate honestly and signal it: "roughly," "approximately," or a range. An honest estimate is defensible; an invented precise figure is not.

**"Everyone exaggerates."** Some do. The ones who get caught do so at reference-check stage, after weeks of process, and the cost is the offer plus the relationship.

## Why the Honest Version Usually Wins Anyway

The practical argument, separate from the ethical one: fabricated claims are weak claims.

A fabricated skill sits in a list with no supporting evidence, which readers discount. A real achievement described properly — with scale, constraint and outcome — is specific in a way invention rarely manages, because you know the details.

Most people underclaim their genuine work far more than they would gain from inventing. Fixing that is both safer and more effective.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it lying to leave a job off my resume?**
Omission is generally acceptable for short or irrelevant roles, but it creates a gap you may be asked about, and application forms that ask for complete history must be answered completely.

**Can I describe a personal project as professional experience?**
Only if labelled accurately. Put it under Projects, not Experience. The work counts; misrepresenting its context does not.

**What if my manager took credit for my work?**
Describe your actual contribution accurately. "Built the model behind the pricing change" is true regardless of who presented it.

**Is using the job posting's exact wording dishonest?**
No, when it describes work you genuinely did. That is translation. Using it for work you did not do is the line.

**How likely is an employer to actually check?**
Titles, dates and degrees are checked routinely by most mid-size and large employers. Assume they will be.
